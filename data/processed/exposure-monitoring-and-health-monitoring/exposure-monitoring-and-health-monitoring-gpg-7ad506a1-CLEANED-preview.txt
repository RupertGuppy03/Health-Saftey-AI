[p8 | prose | and experience.]
Working in hazardous work conditions can adversely affect workers’ health – in both the short and long term. This includes when the work involves substances that are harmful to people’s health (substances hazardous to health).

[p8 | prose | and experience.]
As a person conducting a business or undertaking (PCBU),1 you must ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health and safety of workers, and that other persons are not put at risk by your work. This is your primary duty.

[p8 | prose | and experience.]
You must manage the health risks to workers and others that arise from being exposed to the work carried out by your business. In some circumstances, this could mean monitoring worker exposure (exposure monitoring) and monitoring the health of workers (health monitoring).

[p8 | prose | and experience.]
This guidance covers both exposure monitoring and health monitoring. It provides good practice advice for businesses on selecting external monitoring providers and then on putting in place monitoring programmes. Businesses with in-house monitoring providers may also find aspects of this guidance useful.

[p11 | prose | What is the difference between exposure monitoring and health monitoring?]
What is the difference between exposure monitoring and health monitoring?

[p11 | prose | Exposure monitoring]
Exposure monitoring

[p11 | prose | Exposure monitoring]
Exposure monitoring measures and evaluates what your workers are being exposed to while they are at work.

[p11 | prose | Exposure monitoring]
This can involve workers wearing a device while they work. Examples of personal exposure monitoring:

[p11 | prose | Exposure monitoring]
It should be carried out by suitably qualified, trained and experienced people who know how to carry out the monitoring you need (such as Occupational Hygienists).

[p11 | prose | Exposure monitoring]
Biological exposure monitoring is another type of exposure monitoring. It usually involves taking blood or urine samples to test for a substance (or a metabolite of a substance) workers are working with.

[p11 | prose | Exposure monitoring]
Blood or other invasive samples must be taken by a health practitioner such as an Occupational Health Nurse or phlebotomist (for blood). A suitably qualified, trained and experienced person is needed to interpret the results.

[p11 | prose | Health monitoring]
Health monitoring

[p11 | prose | Health monitoring]
Health monitoring looks at whether a worker’s health is being harmed because of what they are being exposed to while they are at work.

[p11 | prose | Health monitoring]
Examples:

[p11 | prose | Health monitoring]
Well-being programmes, employment prescreening and fitness-to-work examinations are not health monitoring.

[p11 | prose | Health monitoring]
Monitoring should be carried out at the beginning of a worker’s employment (to get baseline readings). Then regular (ongoing) monitoring should be carried out. It should be carried out by suitably qualified, trained and experienced health practitioners with the knowledge, skills, training and experience to carry out the monitoring you need. For example, an Occupational Health Nurse could carry out initial health assessment (health screening) and subsequent routine regular testing. If suspected, workers should be sent to a health practitioner who understands occupational health for a full medical assessment/formal diagnosis and feedback to the PCBU. This could be an Occupational Physician or GP with relevant experience.

[p12 | prose | Health monitoring]
As shown in Figure 2, monitoring information – along with verifying that your control measures are working effectively – can be used to continually improve how you are managing health risks.

[p12 | prose | Health monitoring]

[p12 | prose | Health monitoring]
For more information about managing risk, see our guidance: Identifying, assessing and managing work risks

[p12 | prose | Health monitoring]
You could need initial monitoring carried out, and then regular (ongoing) monitoring.

[p12 | prose | Health monitoring]
You should pay for monitoring costs. Costs could include transport costs if the monitoring occurs off-site.

[p12 | prose | Health monitoring]
If monitoring results show your workers are being harmed or are at risk, take immediate action to eliminate or minimise the health risks to workers. This should include carrying out an investigation, reviewing control measures and deciding what actions you will take (Section 4.8).

[p13 | prose | EXAMPLE]
EXAMPLE

[p13 | prose | EXAMPLE]
Joe runs a workshop with many noisy machines. Joe knew that loud machine noise could permanently damage hearing. Joe also knew that while his workers were likely to be exposed to loud music outside of work, he still had a duty to manage health risks arising from noise at work.

[p13 | prose | Assess the health risk]
Assess the health risk

[p13 | prose | Assess the health risk]
When Joe and his workers carried out a risk assessment, noise was identified as a potential source of harm to workers. As an interim measure, workers were given hearing protection.

[p13 | prose | Assess the health risk]
Joe considered whether he needed to bring in a health and safety professional to check the levels of noise. He decided that someone with the right skills and experience was needed to accurately measure the noise his workers were being exposed to.

[p13 | prose | Assess the health risk]
An Occupational Hygienist came in to check what noise workers were being exposed to throughout the workshop. Their noise levels were higher than the exposure limit of 85 dB(A), as an 8-hour time-weighted average. This meant Joe’s workers were at enhanced risk of developing noise induced hearing loss unless he did something about it.

[p13 | prose | Put control measures in place to eliminate or minimise the risk]
Put control measures in place to eliminate or minimise the risk

[p13 | prose | Put control measures in place to eliminate or minimise the risk]
Joe took immediate action. He read WorkSafe’s noise toolbox and followed a good practice risk management process:

[p13 | prose | Put control measures in place to eliminate or minimise the risk]
– Where Joe could, he replaced machinery with quieter machines.

[p13 | prose | Put control measures in place to eliminate or minimise the risk]
– Where Joe could not replace a machine, he installed noise barriers and moved the machine to a separate room to reduce the amount of noise the workers were exposed to.

[p13 | prose | Put control measures in place to eliminate or minimise the risk]
– Where he could, Joe fitted silencers on compressor exhausts to reduce the noise.

[p13 | prose | Put control measures in place to eliminate or minimise the risk]
– Joe ensured all workers knew about the health risks of excessive noise. He rotated jobs to reduce the number of workers exposed to machine noise and reduced the time workers were exposed to excessive noise.

[p13 | prose | Put control measures in place to eliminate or minimise the risk]
– However, even with the control measures in place, workers were still exposed to unsafe noise levels inside the machine room. So based on the recommendation from the Occupational Hygienist, Class 3 earmuffs were provided to be worn in the machine room. Workers understood why they needed to wear the earmuffs. The earmuffs were fit tested to each worker. Workers were trained to correctly wear, maintain and store the earmuffs. Joe regularly checked that workers were using them properly.

[p13 | prose | Monitor the performance of control measures]
Monitor the performance of control measures

[p13 | prose | Monitor the performance of control measures]
Joe then periodically reviewed the control measures.

[p13 | prose | Monitor the performance of control measures]
Joe wanted to check that the control measures were working well to protect workers from noise. To check the control measures were continuing to work effectively:

[p13 | prose | Monitor the performance of control measures]
– Joe organised the Occupational Hygienist to regularly re-check the noise all workers were being exposed to.

[p13 | prose | Monitor the performance of control measures]
– Joe also decided that it was reasonably practicable and good risk management for workers to undergo regular hearing tests. He engaged an occupational health provider who recommended yearly testing.

[p14 | prose | Monitor the performance of control measures]
As part of Joe’s approach to continuously improve the control measures, he planned to remove the need for hearing protection in the machine room as soon as he could.

[p14 | prose | Monitor the performance of control measures]
As the first step, Joe planned to replace the noisy machinery with much quieter versions that had recently come onto the market. Following this, Joe would work with the Occupational Hygienist to decide on what other measures were needed so that workers did not need to wear hearing protection inside the machine room.

[p14 | prose | Use monitoring findings to improve how health risks are being managed]
Use monitoring findings to improve how health risks are being managed

[p14 | prose | Use monitoring findings to improve how health risks are being managed]
If the monitoring results showed that his workers were being exposed to unsafe noise levels, Joe would review and improve the control measures after seeking advice from the Occupational Hygienist.

[p14 | prose | What Joe did when there was a change in the work]
What Joe did when there was a change in the work

[p14 | prose | What Joe did when there was a change in the work]
Joe knew that in a few months there will be a big order which would mean extended shifts (longer than eight hours) for three months. Joe knew that when workers are exposed to noise for greater than eight hours, the levels of allowable noise decreased. So Joe checked whether the Class 3 earmuffs currently worn by workers would provide adequate hearing protection over the extended shifts.

[p14 | prose | What Joe did when there was a change in the work]
When Joe found out they would not, he knew he had to provide his workers with increased hearing protection. He also reassessed his noise management plan to check that it was still appropriate under the changed work conditions, and if there were other control measures he could put in place.

3.1 Monitoring as part of your primary duty under HSWA

[p16 | prose | 3.1 Monitoring as part of your primary duty under HSWA]
You must ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health and safety of workers, and that other people are not put at risk by your work. In some circumstances, this could mean monitoring worker exposure and the health of workers.2

How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?

[p16 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
A risk assessment of health risks includes the identification of health hazards, assessing the health consequences against the likelihood of exposure, and looking at existing control measures.

[p16 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
To determine if monitoring of worker exposure or worker health is required, a thorough risk assessment should be carried out of:

[p16 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– the workplace

[p16 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– workforce

[p16 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– health hazards

[p16 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– existing control measures

[p16 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– previous monitoring data (if available) and

[p16 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– health issues that have occurred in the past.

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
Carrying out a risk assessment helps to identify which workers or groups of workers need monitoring, and for which health hazards or health effects monitoring should be carried out.

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
Think about:

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– What chemical, physical, or biological hazards are present? In what quantities?

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– What are the health effects associated with exposure to these health hazards?

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– What workplace exposure standards (WES), biological exposure indices (BEI) or other occupational exposure limits are there for these health hazards?

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– How is the workforce organised in terms of worker groups, tasks, work area, and number of workers?

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– What are the processes, tasks, or work practices that are sources of exposure and how workers may be exposed?

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– What materials are involved and how could the raw products, by-products, waste products, or end products affect workers?

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– What existing control measures are there? Are they used and maintained properly?

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
Ask yourself:

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– Where can I obtain information from?

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– What can workers tell me about their tasks?

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
– Am I confident that I am getting all the information I need? – Do I need to engage with an expert on health risk assessment?

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
Monitoring should be considered when the risk assessment indicates workers have an unacceptable or unknown health risk level.

[p17 | prose | How can you carry out a risk assessment of the health risks?]
You may consult a competent person in health risk assessment such as an Occupational Hygienist (see Section 4.3).

Ask yourself:

[p17 | prose | Ask yourself:]

4.1 Things to be aware of first

You must work with other businesses you share monitoring duties with

[p20 | prose | 4.1 Things to be aware of first]
You must, so far as is reasonably practicable, consult, cooperate and coordinate activities with all other businesses you share health and safety duties with in relation to the same matter. This is so all businesses can meet their joint responsibilities.

If this happens, talk with the other business and decide who will organise the monitoring, how information will be shared, and how costs will be split.

SOME POINTS TO NOTE

[p21 | prose | 1. Use personal information for the purposes it has been collected for]
1. Use personal information for the purposes it has been collected for

[p21 | prose | 1. Use personal information for the purposes it has been collected for]
This means using personal information that has been collected during monitoring to manage health risks for that purpose.

[p21 | prose | 1. Use personal information for the purposes it has been collected for]
There are a few exceptions under the Privacy Act to this. For example, worker consent could be sought to use it for other purposes (for example, for research). This could be at the same time workers are asked to consent to take part in the monitoring, or later on if and when required.

[p21 | prose | 2. Take care when sharing a worker’s personal information with others (for example, with other workers or businesses that you share health and safety duties with)]
2. Take care when sharing a worker’s personal information with others (for example, with other workers or businesses that you share health and safety duties with)

[p21 | prose | 2. Take care when sharing a worker’s personal information with others (for example, with other workers or businesses that you share health and safety duties with)]
You can only share personal information when one of the relevant exceptions under the Privacy Act applies, for example:

[p21 | prose | 2. Take care when sharing a worker’s personal information with others (for example, with other workers or businesses that you share health and safety duties with)]
– when the worker has provided written consent for their personal information to be shared

[p21 | prose | 2. Take care when sharing a worker’s personal information with others (for example, with other workers or businesses that you share health and safety duties with)]
– when the reason the personal information was collected for was for it to be shared, and this was told to the worker at the time the information was collected

[p21 | prose | 2. Take care when sharing a worker’s personal information with others (for example, with other workers or businesses that you share health and safety duties with)]
– when information which could identify the worker (identifiable information) has been removed.

[p21 | prose | WHAT IS IDENTIFIABLE INFORMATION?]
WHAT IS IDENTIFIABLE INFORMATION?

[p21 | prose | WHAT IS IDENTIFIABLE INFORMATION?]
Apart from the obvious identifiable information (for example, name, age), other information could be used to identify individuals (for example, the worker’s position, date of monitoring).

[p21 | prose | WHAT IS IDENTIFIABLE INFORMATION?]
The fewer workers that undergo monitoring, the easier it will be to identify someone’s information.

[p21 | prose | WHAT IS IDENTIFIABLE INFORMATION?]
Even if one of the exceptions applies, it is best practice to let workers know before the information is released.

[p21 | prose | WHAT IS IDENTIFIABLE INFORMATION?]
Decide with workers how any personal information collected will be shared. This information should form part of the information you provide workers (see Appendices 9–11 for examples).

[p22 | prose | 3. Store personal information securely and restrict access]
3. Store personal information securely and restrict access

[p22 | prose | 3. Store personal information securely and restrict access]
Any personal information collected during monitoring must be stored securely.

[p22 | prose | 3. Store personal information securely and restrict access]
Think about the best way to store personal information so that only authorised personnel can access it. For example, store hardcopies in a locked drawer or room, or store electronic records on secure devices or computers that are password protected. Keep monitoring results separately from worker records.

EXPOSURE MONITORING (non-biological exposure monitoring)

[p25 | prose | EXPOSURE MONITORING (non-biological exposure monitoring)]
– Your monitoring provider should characterise the risk of work health hazards to prioritise which workers need monitoring and for which hazards.

[p25 | prose | EXPOSURE MONITORING (non-biological exposure monitoring)]

[p25 | prose | EXPOSURE MONITORING (non-biological exposure monitoring)]

[p25 | prose | BIOLOGICAL EXPOSURE MONITORING AND HEALTH MONITORING]

[p26 | prose | Which workers will take part?]
Which workers will take part?

[p26 | prose | Which workers will take part?]
To get the best picture of what your workers are being exposed to, your monitoring provider should recommend which workers should be monitored. You, your monitoring provider and workers should then together discuss who should be monitored.

[p26 | prose | Which workers will take part?]
If you have a lot of workers exposed to the same work conditions, it may not be necessary to monitor all workers. Your monitoring provider will be able to advise how many workers (and which ones) should be monitored to get robust results.

[p26 | prose | EXAMPLE]
EXAMPLE

[p26 | prose | EXAMPLE]
Tom owns a small engineering firm. The work involves welding, and he is worried that breathing in welding fume may be affecting his workers’ health. He brought in an expert who recommended that only the workers who work in the workshop undergo health monitoring (this involves testing their lung function). This meant his office workers – who work in a separate location – would not undergo health monitoring.

[p26 | prose | EXAMPLE]
For exposure monitoring, your workers could be split into similar exposure groups (SEGs), and only randomly selected workers (representatives) from each SEG monitored.

[p26 | prose | EXAMPLE]
EXAMPLE

[p26 | prose | EXAMPLE]
Sarah has a widget manufacturing business. She is worried about the levels of welding fume. She decides to get an Occupational Hygienist in to monitor the welding fume levels workers are being exposed to.

[p26 | prose | EXAMPLE]
When deciding which workers to be involved, it was decided that based on the initial health risk assessment (basic characterisation):

[p26 | prose | EXAMPLE]
– office staff and warehouse staff were not required to take part (due to the low risk of exposure)

[p26 | prose | EXAMPLE]
– welders and the assembly line would take part (the assembly line is near the welding bays). There were 7 welders and 10 assembly line workers.

[p26 | prose | EXAMPLE]
The Occupational Hygienist developed a sampling strategy that would give good confidence on what the exposures are over time by choosing 5 out of 6 welders and 7 out of 10 assembly line workers, randomly selected. This strategy would be sufficient to get a representative sample of the whole groups’ average exposure.

Shirley is the owner of Repaint ‘em – a business that repaints old houses. During its work, her workers are exposed to noise, dust and specifically lead paint dust when using electrical sanders to prepare surfaces.

[p32 | prose | Baseline testing]
Baseline testing

[p32 | prose | Baseline testing]
After discussions between Shirley and workers it was decided that baseline monitoring (on commencing employment) will take place to:

[p32 | prose | Baseline testing]
– check workers’ hearing and lung function (spirometry) and

[p32 | prose | Baseline testing]
– ask questions about potential lead exposure prior to commencing work.

[p32 | prose | Baseline testing]
The baseline test results will allow for changes to be seen in hearing and lung function when the routine testing is done.

[p32 | prose | Routine monitoring]
Routine monitoring

[p32 | prose | Routine monitoring]
It was decided that routine monitoring will take place and include: – monitoring exposure to noise and lead (using an Occupational Hygienist to carry out noise assessments and airborne lead exposure monitoring) and

[p32 | prose | Routine monitoring]
– monitoring exposure to lead (by an Occupational Health Nurse taking blood tests to check for lead levels – biological exposure monitoring) and

[p32 | prose | Routine monitoring]
– monitoring health – hearing tests and spirometry (by an Occupational Health Nurse).

[p32 | prose | Routine monitoring]
Jonno is an experienced Occupational Health Nurse (member of HASANZ and with post grad qualifications) who was bought in to carry out the hearing and spirometry tests, and lead blood tests. This year Jonno performed routine monitoring which including a questionnaire, hearing and lung function tests and took blood samples for lead testing. He found 3 of 6 workers had deteriorating hearing levels and two had higher than expected lead levels.

[p32 | prose | Routine monitoring]
With each worker, Jonno discussed their results and talked about whether there were any changes. Jonno was told that they were using new sanders which were very loud and made much finer dust that seemed to get everywhere.

[p33 | prose | Routine monitoring]
Jonno let Shirley know this (all workers consented to being named).

[p33 | prose | Routine monitoring]
Shirley talked with her workers and found that while the type of respiratory protective equipment (RPE) used was still appropriate, it no longer fitted properly (too old) and needed replacing. The fit testing of new RPE would also be required.

[p33 | prose | Routine monitoring]
Shirley requested that an urgent noise assessment be carried out (she had forgotten to get a noise assessment carried out when the new sanders arrived).

[p33 | prose | Routine monitoring]
In the interim, additional hearing protection was supplied to workers. Shirley was advised that a higher grade of hearing protection was required if these new sanders were going to continue to be used.

[p33 | prose | Routine monitoring]
After discussing with workers, Shirley found that workers preferred using the new sanders as they were much faster and easier to use. Everyone agreed to continue to use the sanders, but only after workers had been trained to use and care for the new hearing protection, and fit tested for the replacement RPE.

Appendix 3: So far as is reasonably practicable

[p43 | appendix | Appendix 3: So far as is reasonably practicable]
section 22 of HSWA

[p43 | appendix | Appendix 3: So far as is reasonably practicable]
Certain PCBU duties (the section 36–43 duties including the primary duty of care) must be carried out ‘so far as is reasonably practicable’.

[p43 | prose | What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’]
What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’

[p43 | prose | What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’]
Just because something is possible to do, does not mean it is reasonably practicable in the circumstances.

[p43 | prose | What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’]
Consider:

[p43 | prose | What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’]
– What possible actions can be taken to ensure health and safety?

[p43 | prose | What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’]
– Of these possible actions, at a particular time, what is reasonable to do?

[p43 | prose | What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’]
Think about the following questions.

[p43 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT THE RISK?]
WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT THE RISK?

[p43 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT THE RISK?]
– How likely is the risk to occur?

[p43 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT THE RISK?]
– How severe is the illness or injury that might occur if something goes wrong?

[p43 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT THE RISK?]
– What is known, or should reasonably be known, about the risk?

[p43 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?

[p43 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
– What is known, or should reasonably be known, about the ways (control measures) to eliminate or minimise the risk?

[p43 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
– What control measures are available?

[p43 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
– How appropriate (suitable) are the control measures to manage the risk? – What are the costs of these control measures?

[p43 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
– Are the costs grossly disproportionate to the risk? Cost must only be used as a reason to not do something when that cost is grossly out of proportion to the risk.

[p43 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
While PCBUs should check if there are widely used control measures for that risk (such as industry standards), they should always keep their specific circumstances in mind. A common industry practice might not be the most effective or appropriate control measure to use.

[p43 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
If PCBUs are not sure what control measures are appropriate, WorkSafe recommends getting advice from a suitably qualified and experienced health and safety professional.

[p43 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
For more information, see our guidance: Reasonably practicable

[p44 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA

[p44 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
More than one PCBU can have a duty in relation to the same matter. These PCBUs have overlapping duties – this means that the duties are shared between them.

[p44 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
Duties regularly overlap:

[p44 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
– in a shared workplace (for example, a building site or a port) where more than one business has control and influence over the work on site.

[p44 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
– in a contracting chain, where contractors and subcontractors provide services to a head contractor or client and do not necessarily share the same workplace.

[p44 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
A PCBU must, so far as is reasonably practicable, consult, cooperate and coordinate activities with all other PCBUs they share duties with so that all PCBUs can meet their joint responsibilities.

[p44 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
A PCBU cannot transfer or contract out of their duties, or pass liability to another person.

[p44 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
However a PCBU can make an agreement with another PCBU to fulfil specific duties. Even if this occurs, all PCBUs are still responsible for meeting their legal duties.

[p44 | prose | EXAMPLE]
A local hotel contracts out housekeeping services to an agency. The hotel and agency both have a duty to ensure the health and safety of the housekeeping workers, so far as is reasonably practicable. This includes the duty to provide first aid facilities.

[p44 | prose | EXAMPLE]
The agency reaches an agreement with the hotel – if their workers need first aid while working at the hotel they can use the hotel’s first aid facilities.

[p49 | prose | WES, BEI and PES explained]
WES, BEI and PES explained

[p49 | prose | WES, BEI and PES explained]
WES are values that refer to the airborne concentration of substances at which it is believed that nearly all workers can be repeatedly exposed day after day without coming to harm.

[p49 | prose | WES, BEI and PES explained]
BEIs are guidance values for assessing biological monitoring results. Biological monitoring measures the concentration of a substance – or its breakdown products - in blood or urine or even exhaled air and the monitoring result is compared to the BEI established for the specific substance.

[p49 | prose | WES, BEI and PES explained]
As described above, a PES is a WES or a BEI that has the purpose of protecting persons in a workplace from harm to health and that is prescribed in:

[p49 | prose | WES, BEI and PES explained]
– regulations or

[p49 | prose | WES, BEI and PES explained]
– a SWI.

[p49 | prose | WES, BEI and PES explained]
See above for information about existing PES.

[p49 | prose | WES, BEI and PES explained]
The WES and BEI values in the WorkSafe WES/BEI book are not PES and are therefore guidance values. They are intended to be used by qualified occupational health practitioners with the appropriate knowledge, skills and experience to apply them for health risk assessment.

[p49 | prose | WES, BEI and PES explained]
Health risk management must consider a number of aspects from identification, to assessment, to control, with exposure monitoring being just one part – not the only part that needs to be considered. WES and BEI are important tools for monitoring worker exposures.
