These guidelines provide advice on how to manage the health and safety risks around the use of machinery in the workplace. They can help persons conducting a business or undertaking (PCBUs) meet their duties under the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA).

[p9 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]

[p9 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]

[p9 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]
– use machinery for processing or manufacturing

[p9 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]
– design, manufacture or supply the machinery

[p9 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]

[p9 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]

[p9 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]

[p9 | prose | 1.3 The use of Australian New Zealand Standard AS/NZS 4024:2019]
1.3 The use of Australian New Zealand Standard AS/NZS 4024:2019

[p9 | prose | 1.3 The use of Australian New Zealand Standard AS/NZS 4024:2019]
You will see references to AS/NZS 4024:2019 throughout this guidance. This is the standard that gives the current state of knowledge for the safeguarding of machinery and plant.

[p9 | prose | 1.3 The use of Australian New Zealand Standard AS/NZS 4024:2019]
PCBUs should refer to AS/NZS 4024:2019 as the primary standard against which to benchmark. This applies to all duty holders involved in the use of machinery including design, manufacture, supply, installation and users.

[p9 | prose | 1.3 The use of Australian New Zealand Standard AS/NZS 4024:2019]
PCBUs can work to other standards but need to show that they can reach the same level of safety, or better, in the circumstances in which the standards are used.

[p9 | prose | 1.3 The use of Australian New Zealand Standard AS/NZS 4024:2019]
The AS/NZS 4024:2019 standards provide guidance only. Compliance with them does not guarantee compliance with HSWA and the relevant regulations.

3.1 Introduction to risk management

[p13 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to risk management]
Machinery in the workplace presents many risks to workers and other people’s health and safety.

[p13 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to risk management]
You must manage these risks so far as is reasonably practicable.

[p13 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to risk management]
As good practice you should:

[p13 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to risk management]
– identify hazards and work out what risks they present

[p13 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to risk management]
– put in place control measures to eliminate or minimise those risks

[p13 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to risk management]
– talk to and engage with your workers and their representatives throughout the risk management process.

[p13 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to risk management]
For more information on risk management, see WorkSafe’s guidance How to manage work risks

[p15 | prose | The first step is to identify hazards – anything that could injure or harm someone.]
The first step is to identify hazards – anything that could injure or harm someone.

[p15 | prose | The first step is to identify hazards – anything that could injure or harm someone.]
Do a workplace inspection to identify all machinery used.

[p15 | prose | The first step is to identify hazards – anything that could injure or harm someone.]
You can identify hazards by:

[p15 | prose | The first step is to identify hazards – anything that could injure or harm someone.]
– Physical inspections. Inspect the machinery and assess where someone could get injured or caught in the machinery.

[p15 | prose | The first step is to identify hazards – anything that could injure or harm someone.]
– Conversation and consultation. Talk with your workers, the people who use the machines and the people who work around the machines. They will know better than anyone what could go wrong. They will also know how the work is done – the short-cuts and different processes they may use to get a job done.

[p15 | prose | The first step is to identify hazards – anything that could injure or harm someone.]
– Task analysis. Look at how each task is carried out and the hazards involved in the task. This should include what happens when there is a blockage, or the machine needs cleaning or maintenance.

[p15 | prose | The first step is to identify hazards – anything that could injure or harm someone.]
– Review incident and injury records. Identify hazards and causes of harm from what has happened before, and the things that nearly happened (the near misses). Also look at what is happening in other businesses in your industry – industry groups and organisations are good for this.

4.2 Machinery hazards

[p19 | prose | 4.2 Machinery hazards]
There is a number of types of hazards to consider when looking at the safe use of machinery. They include (but are not limited to):

[p19 | prose | 4.2 Machinery hazards]
– mechanical hazards

[p19 | prose | 4.2 Machinery hazards]
– ergonomic hazards

[p19 | prose | 4.2 Machinery hazards]
– chemicals and fumes

[p19 | prose | 4.2 Machinery hazards]
– exhaust (gases/fume/diesel) emissions from ICE

[p19 | prose | 4.2 Machinery hazards]
– organisational hazards

[p19 | prose | 4.2 Machinery hazards]
– electrical safety

[p19 | prose | 4.2 Machinery hazards]
– environmental and occupational hazards.

[p19 | prose | 4.2 Machinery hazards]
Each should be identified, their risks assessed and managed.

[p19 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical hazards]
4.3 Mechanical hazards

[p19 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical hazards]
There are many different mechanical hazards presented by machinery that can cause serious harm. These include:

[p19 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical hazards]
– drawing in or trapping

[p19 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical hazards]
– crushing

[p19 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical hazards]
– impact

[p19 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical hazards]
– shearing

[p19 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical hazards]
– friction and abrasion

[p19 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical hazards]
– entanglement

[p19 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical hazards]
– cutting

[p19 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical hazards]
– stabbing and puncturing.

[p19 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical hazards]
For more explanation and examples, see Appendix 4: Mechanical hazards in machinery

[p20 | prose | 4.4 Electrical safety of machinery]
4.4 Electrical safety of machinery

[p20 | prose | 4.4 Electrical safety of machinery]
The wiring and fittings of machinery connected to the mains or electricity generating device must meet all legal requirements and must be installed by or under the supervision of an authorised or licensed electrician.

[p20 | prose | 4.4 Electrical safety of machinery]
All tagging and testing should be carried out by a certified, professional third party in line with legal requirements and relevant standards.

[p20 | prose | 4.4 Electrical safety of machinery]
All portable or handheld machinery that gets power from electricity should be used with an isolating transformer or residual current device, where needed. Get specific advice from an electrician or the electricity supplier on the best device to use.

[p20 | prose | 4.5 Chemicals and fumes]
4.5 Chemicals and fumes

[p20 | prose | 4.5 Chemicals and fumes]
Many chemicals used with machinery can harm workers. Assess all chemicals for hazardous health effects. Put appropriate controls in place to stop or control people’s exposure. To monitor the environment or workers’ health to make sure exposure to the chemicals is not affecting their health, consult a competent person such as an occupational hygienist.

[p20 | prose | 4.5 Chemicals and fumes]
For more information refer to the substance’s safety data sheet, available from your supplier.

[p20 | prose | Control of airborne hazards – ventilation]
Control of airborne hazards – ventilation

[p20 | prose | Control of airborne hazards – ventilation]
Protect workers, so far as is reasonably practicable, from inhaling steam, fumes, dust and other airborne contaminants in the workplace. You can use ventilation, filtration and/or mechanical extraction. Remove any contaminants made as part of the work at the source.

[p20 | prose | Control of airborne hazards – ventilation]
Any mechanical extraction should pull contaminants away from workers’ breathing zone, not through it.

[p20 | prose | Control of airborne hazards – ventilation]
If it is not reasonably practicable to completely remove or isolate the hazardous substance, you must minimise any risk of harm to the employee.

[p20 | prose | Control of airborne hazards – ventilation]
To minimise the risk, an employer can:

[p20 | prose | Control of airborne hazards – ventilation]
– monitor employees’ exposure to the hazard

[p20 | prose | Control of airborne hazards – ventilation]
– monitor employees’ health (with their informed consent)

[p20 | prose | Control of airborne hazards – ventilation]
– provide protective clothing and equipment (such as respirators) and make sure they are used.

[p20 | prose | 4.6 Ergonomic hazards]
4.6 Ergonomic hazards

[p20 | prose | 4.6 Ergonomic hazards]
Ergonomic hazards come about through the way the operator interacts with the machine.

[p20 | prose | 4.6 Ergonomic hazards]
Sometimes machinery is not always designed for how an operator should use the machine. For example, operators may have to overreach, reach above shoulder height, hold awkward postures, and use repetitive or forceful movements.

[p20 | prose | 4.6 Ergonomic hazards]
Having to work this way can cause damage to nerves, muscles and tendons.

[p20 | prose | 4.6 Ergonomic hazards]
Ergonomic hazards can cause serious harm to operators, but they do not need to. These hazards can be removed at the design stage. For more information, see WorkSafe’s guidance Health and safety by design

[p21 | prose | Hazardous manual handling]
Hazardous manual handling

[p21 | prose | Hazardous manual handling]
By considering how and when a machine is used, you can reduce the risk of injury. This includes:

[p21 | prose | Hazardous manual handling]
– How well the working environment is set up? Can operators safely reach frequently used displays, instruments or control panels while keeping correct posture?

[p21 | prose | Hazardous manual handling]
– What type of machinery is used? Does the machinery expose anyone to too much vibration, noise or emissions or does it need physical force to work?

[p21 | prose | Hazardous manual handling]
– How work is organised? How much work needs to be done? How urgent is the work? How many breaks do operators get? How long are the breaks?

[p21 | prose | Hazardous manual handling]
– What physical demands are put on the person using the machinery? Is the work repetitive? Does it require awkward movements or postures? Does the operator have to work in extreme temperatures?

[p21 | prose | Hazardous manual handling]
– Check whether tasks require repetitive movement or there is a risk of musculoskeletal injuries and gradual process disease.

[p21 | prose | Layout and design]
Layout and design

[p21 | prose | Layout and design]
Good layout makes guarding better at keeping people safe. Machines that are poorly placed or too close together can be unsafe, even if guarded.

[p21 | prose | Layout and design]
When designing layout:

[p21 | prose | Layout and design]
– avoid congestion points or worker movements near hazardous machinery

[p21 | prose | Layout and design]
– make sure people can use, clean and maintain the machinery without being harmed

[p21 | prose | Layout and design]
– make space for any waste materials to gather before they are cleared (they should not clutter walkways or work areas)

[p21 | prose | Layout and design]
– note the movements of trucks, materials and people

[p21 | prose | Layout and design]
– mark out walkways and create vehicle movement areas

[p21 | prose | Layout and design]
– mark out ‘no-go’ areas, so people can stay away from dangerous machinery.

[p21 | prose | Layout and design]
Check how close moving parts are to other machinery and fixtures in buildings.

[p21 | prose | Reach and guarding]
Reach and guarding

[p21 | prose | Reach and guarding]
The main point of machine guarding is to stop workers reaching past the guard into the machine. When deciding on the best way to guard a machine, consider how a worker uses and interacts with a machine (ergonomic principles).

[p21 | prose | Reach and guarding]
You can find more information in Ergonomics of machine guarding

[p21 | prose | 4.7 How work is organised]
4.7 How work is organised

[p21 | prose | 4.7 How work is organised]
For machine guarding to work well, employers need to:

[p21 | prose | 4.7 How work is organised]
– understand how materials move through the site

[p21 | prose | 4.7 How work is organised]
– develop safe operating procedures on how to use machinery safely, including maintenance and cleaning

[p21 | prose | 4.7 How work is organised]
– train workers to work safely.

[p21 | prose | 4.7 How work is organised]
New technology, new machinery or changes to machinery can introduce new hazards.

[p21 | prose | 4.7 How work is organised]
At these times, always complete a hazard assessment and consult with workers.

[p22 | prose | Fatigue and shift-work]
Fatigue and shift-work

[p22 | prose | Fatigue and shift-work]
Fatigue is a hazard. PCBUs must take all reasonably practicable measures to minimise the risk of fatigue, such as when employees drive or use dangerous machinery.

[p22 | prose | Fatigue and shift-work]
Shift-work can be hazardous because it disrupts normal rest patterns. Employees need enough recovery time outside work so they can be safe and productive at work.

[p22 | prose | Fatigue and shift-work]
Along with enough sleep, breaks during work hours are important to maintain an employee’s physical and mental well-being. For more information, see WorkSafe’s guidance Fatigue quick guide

[p22 | prose | 4.8 Environmental and occupational health hazards]
4.8 Environmental and occupational health hazards

[p22 | prose | 4.8 Environmental and occupational health hazards]
When reviewing machinery for non-mechanical hazards, consider how the presence of machinery can affect the work environment.

[p22 | prose | 4.8 Environmental and occupational health hazards]
A thorough hazard identification process should consider the effect environmental factors (such as lighting, heat, and cold) can have on workers when using machinery.

[p22 | prose | Working at height]
Working at height

[p22 | prose | Working at height]
People need a suitable work platform to reduce the risk of falling from machinery.

[p22 | prose | Working at height]
Working safely at height may need:

[p22 | prose | Working at height]
– fixed or permanently installed access platforms

[p22 | prose | Working at height]
– mobile elevating work platforms

[p22 | prose | Working at height]
– temporary platforms

[p22 | prose | Working at height]
– fall restraints.

[p22 | prose | Working at height]
For more information, see WorkSafe guidance Working at height in New Zealand

[p22 | prose | Lighting]
Lighting

[p22 | prose | Lighting]
Make sure the work area is appropriately well lit. Poor lighting can be a hazard as can overlighting and reflected light. Sometimes the machine or guards can block normal lighting, so extra local light is needed. Also consider putting local lighting in maintenance areas that are poorly lit, such as inside some electrical compartments where electrical isolation is needed for access.

[p22 | prose | Lighting]
4.9.7 For more information refer to AS/NZS 1680.2.4 Interior lighting – Part 24: Industrial tasks and processes.

[p22 | prose | Noise]
Noise

[p22 | prose | Noise]
Employers must take all reasonably practicable steps to reduce any risk of harm to people from machinery noise.

[p22 | prose | Noise]
Where this is not practicable, employers should isolate people from excessive noise including providing appropriate hearing protection as necessary. For more information on controlling noise, refer to WorkSafe’s guidance on Noise

[p22 | prose | Operating speeds and dangerous vibration]
Operating speeds and dangerous vibration

[p22 | prose | Operating speeds and dangerous vibration]
No machine should be driven or used at an unsafe speed. Where a designer or manufacturer recommends a working speed for a machine, do not operate any faster.

[p22 | prose | Operating speeds and dangerous vibration]
Maintain machines so there is no dangerous vibration when the machine is working or when moving parts and cutters are running.

[p23 | prose | Operating speeds and dangerous vibration]
For more information, see WorkSafe’s guidance on Vibration

[p23 | prose | Machinery stability and security]
Machinery stability and security

[p23 | prose | Machinery stability and security]
All machinery should be secured to the floor or other structure so that it cannot tip, become unstable or create any other hazards, unless it is designed to be portable.

[p23 | prose | Weight of guarding]
Weight of guarding

[p23 | prose | Weight of guarding]
Large machinery may need a lot of guarding, which may need to be removed for maintenance access.

7.1 Types of guards that isolate the hazard

[p34 | prose | 7.1 Types of guards that isolate the hazard]
Fixed guards

[p34 | prose | 7.1 Types of guards that isolate the hazard]
Fixed guards are physical barriers that keep people out of dangerous areas during normal use, maintenance or cleaning. The guard design will be influenced by the need to adjust drive belts and transmission chains and other factors.

[p34 | prose | 7.1 Types of guards that isolate the hazard]
Barriers or fences held securely in place with fasteners or other suitable devices can stop access to dangerous areas.

[p34 | prose | 7.1 Types of guards that isolate the hazard]
Fixed guards can be:

[p34 | prose | 7.1 Types of guards that isolate the hazard]
– permanent – welded into or part of the body of the machine

[p34 | prose | 7.1 Types of guards that isolate the hazard]
– removable – but they can only be removed when the machine is stopped with tools that are not easily available to the operators.

[p34 | prose | 7.1 Types of guards that isolate the hazard]
Do not use wing nuts, wedge insets or anything that can be undone with the fingers.

[p34 | prose | 7.1 Types of guards that isolate the hazard]
Machine guards should be made of substantial materials (such as sheet steel, wire mesh) that cannot be easily damaged.

[p34 | prose | Interlocked guards]
Interlocked guards

[p34 | prose | Interlocked guards]
Interlocked guards work by cutting power to the machine when the guard is opened. They are a good guard to use when a machine needs to be accessed often.

[p34 | prose | Interlocked guards]
If parts keep moving when the machine is not working, you need to use a type of guard that cannot be opened until all parts have stopped moving, or fit devices that stop the machinery. Any brakes fitted to machinery need to be well maintained.

[p34 | prose | Interlocked guards]
Use a suitable anti-freefall device with interlocked rise and fall guards on machine tools that can injure if the guards freefall under gravity.

[p34 | prose | Interlocked guards]
Power-operated guards should take a minimum of force to work so they do not create a trapping hazard. Where it is not possible to reduce the closing force of a guard, fit a safety trip device to the leading edge of the guard that will stop and reverse the guard ifthe guard contacts an object, like a hand.

[p35 | prose | Interlocked guards]
When using barriers such as fences, there is a danger that machines can start when someone is near them, such as when an interlocked door accidentally closes and the machine re-starts. To stop an interlock door or gate closing accidentally fit devices such as a spring or gravity latch, which need a deliberate action from someone to close the door. Interlocked guards should be designed so that any failure or loss of power does not expose people to danger. The design also needs to take into account the possibility of someone being inside the area enclosed by the guard when someone tries to start the machine.

[p35 | prose | Interlocked guards]
Only after doing a risk assessment can you know what type of safety device to install with the guard, and the level of integrity of the related control circuitry. For more information, see AS/NZS 4024.1501 and AS/NZS 4024.1503.

[p35 | prose | Safe by position]
Safe by position

[p35 | prose | Safe by position]
Safe by position relies on putting dangerous machinery parts out of reach of people. This method needs policies and practices in place to make sure that the protection is not compromised as people can often use When deciding how far away to put dangerous machinery, also consider how maintenance people will get access, such as by ladder, scaffold or mobile elevating work platform. When putting controls in place to stop people reaching a hazard, always consider that it is human nature to try and defeat an obstacle. Make sure your controls are not easily defeatable.

[p35 | prose | Trip guards]
Trip guards

[p35 | prose | Trip guards]
When other guarding methods are not practical, you can use trip guards. A trip guard is designed to cut the power if someone reaches into a dangerous part of a machine. However, if this system fails, there is no physical barrier to stop people touching dangerous parts. All safety trip guards should be hardwired to the machine control and power brake systems.

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
8.1 Power controls

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
The power control is the device on a machine that controls the flow of energy to the prime mover. This energy source may be:

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
– electricity

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
– hydraulic oil under pressure

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
– compressed gas.

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
The power control should be able to stop the flow from all energy sources.

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
Interlocks and labels should clearly indicate where there is more than one energy source and stop all sources of energy to the prime movers.

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
The power controls should be:

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
– able to open all energy sources (such as all phases of a three-phase electrical supply)

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
– built and shrouded so the machine cannot be started accidentally

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
– clearly identified, with labels giving information on when and how to start the machine

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
– convenient to use and placed using sound ergonomic principles away from dangerous parts

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
– fail-safe, in case the energy supply is cut

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
– lockable in the off position when a person at the controls might not be able to see staff working on the machine

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
– showing the direction of the movement of the controls, which match up to the motion of the moving parts

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
– unable to be locked out in the on position

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
– unable to indicate off position if it is in fact in the on position.

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
Hydraulic controls should be either dead-man or hold-to-run type with anti-tie down, so that if the control is released the machine stops moving.

[p39 | prose | 8.1 Power controls]
When a machine’s power falls to a low level or stops completely, exposing parts of the machine, this can create a significant hazard when the power is restored. The machine should need the deliberate operation of the power control to start the dangerous parts.

[p40 | prose | Photoelectric safety devices]
Photoelectric safety devices

[p40 | prose | Photoelectric safety devices]
Photoelectric safety devices use light beams that stop machines working when the light beam is broken. This method is often used when fixed or interlocked mechanical guards are not practical. However, if the system fails, there is no physical barrier to stop people being exposed to the hazard. Photoelectric devices can be set to control how much anyone can enter a restricted space, such as a hand but not the arm, or an arm but not the body.

[p40 | prose | Photoelectric safety devices]
Single light beams are not normally suitable because people can reach around the light beam and access the hazard. You can use multiple light beams so there are no gaps that people can reach through, around, under or over. When any of the beams are broken, the power is cut.

[p40 | prose | Photoelectric safety devices]
Consider carefully what distance a light beam curtain is placed from the hazard. If it is too close, someone can reach through the light curtain into danger faster than the control system can stop the machine. If the beam is too far away, someone can stay inside the protected area without interrupting the light beam.

[p40 | prose | Photoelectric safety devices]
You can use extra protection (such as extra light beams/curtains, safety mats or laser scanners) to monitor the area inside the light curtain. You can use photoelectric safety devices with other types of guard to make a safe zone where an operator has to access the machine frequently.

[p40 | prose | Photoelectric safety devices]
As photoelectric systems can fail without visible warning, any failure should not put a user at risk.

[p40 | prose | Photoelectric safety devices]
Photoelectric safety devices should meet and be installed to the standard of AS/NZS 4024.2802 – Application of protective equipment to detect the presence of persons.

[p40 | prose | Automatic push-away guards]
Automatic push-away guards

[p40 | prose | Automatic push-away guards]
With this type of guarding, a barrier moves towards the user when they approach the hazard making them step back, out of reach of the hazard.

[p40 | prose | Automatic push-away guards]
If push-away guards are not carefully designed and maintained then they themselves can become a hazard.

[p40 | prose | Automatic push-away guards]
Users need thorough training to safely use machinery guarded like this.

[p41 | prose | Two-hand controls]
Two-hand controls

[p41 | prose | Two-hand controls]
Two-hand operation is where two operation buttons need to be pushed at the same time, and held, to allow a machine to operate. This makes sure that the operation of the hazardous operation cannot happen until both hands are clear of the danger area. Only use this method to isolate people from machinery hazards as a last resort. Two-hand controls only protect the machine operator, not other people who may be near. Two-hand controls should: - need to be turned on together (so people cannot tape one control down) - need to be held to run, so the machine stops immediately when the control is released - be spaced well apart and shrouded so one arm cannot run both controls. The rear and sides of the machine should be guarded by fixed guards to prevent access by other people.

[p41 | prose | Two-hand controls]
Two-hand controls should meet the standard of AS/NZS 4024.2601 = Two-hand controls devices - principles for design and selection.

[p41 | prose | Pressure-sensitive mats]
Pressure-sensitive mats

[p41 | prose | Pressure-sensitive mats]
Pressure-sensitive mats are designed to cut the machine’s power if someone steps on them to access a dangerous part. Only use pressure-sensitive mats when you cannot use physical barriers or other methods of isolating people from hazards. Pressure-sensitive mats use well-spaced electrical or fluid switches or valves in a mat. The mat covers any entries to a restricted space. Pressure on the mat stops the automatic operation of the machine. You should design the guard so no one can step over or around it into a restricted area. Operate and maintain pressure-sensing safeguard systems to the manufacturers’ instructions. Keep records of any maintenance, inspection, commissioning and alteration to a presence-sensing system, as well as any test results. Make sure workers and health and safety representatives can access the records. Because pressure-sensitive mats do not usually show any visible sign of failure, use a control system that shuts down the machine if a mat fails.

[p42 | prose | Locked guards and gates]
Locked guards and gates

[p42 | prose | Locked guards and gates]
Locking guards and gates need a responsible person (usually a manager) to hold the key at all times.

[p42 | prose | Locked guards and gates]
This person needs to also make sure the gate is not opened until the machine is switched off, isolated and has stopped.

[p42 | prose | Locked guards and gates]
Only use locked guards and gates if there are no practical alternatives.

[p42 | prose | Locked guards and gates]
Senior management in association with staff should write, approve and monitor any safe operating procedures and monitor the effectiveness of the safety process as a temporary means to minimise the hazards.

[p42 | prose | Locked guards and gates]
Isolation, hold cards and lock-out devices can also be used so a machine is not accidentally restarted.

[p42 | prose | Adjustable guards]
Adjustable guards

[p42 | prose | Adjustable guards]
Adjustable guards are made up of a fixed guard with adjustable elements that are moved to suit each task. They can be:

[p42 | prose | Adjustable guards]
– self-adjusting – guards that are forced open by the entry of work

[p42 | prose | Adjustable guards]
– distance guards – barriers that can be moved to a safe distance from the danger zone.

[p42 | prose | Adjustable guards]
Guards that move out of the way for each operation (automatic guards) need special care. Hazards can be created between the guard and:

[p42 | prose | Adjustable guards]
– machine

[p42 | prose | Adjustable guards]
– person

[p42 | prose | Adjustable guards]
– work piece.

[p42 | prose | Adjustable guards]
Staff need full training on using and adjusting these guards. These guards are only effective when people use them correctly.

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
Emergency stop devices

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
When emergency stop devices are installed, they are only ever a back-up for other control measures.

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
Emergency stops should NOT be used to lock-out the machine. Because the actuators can separate from the contacts, the control can show the machine is off when it is actually on.

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
Do a hazard assessment when choosing an emergency stop device and consider:

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
– whether part of the machine still needs to work in an emergency situation

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
– what other safety features still need to work (such as pressure release valves)

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
– whether the stop introduces any new hazards

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
– what level of integrity any associated circuitry needs.

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
Make sure emergency stop devices:

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
– are prominent, and clearly and durably marked

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
– are immediately accessible to each user of the machine

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
– have red handles, bars or push buttons (labelling can also be used) on a yellow background

[p42 | prose | Emergency stop devices]
– are not affected by electrical or electronic circuit failure.

[p42 | prose | Other considerations include:]
Other considerations include:

[p42 | prose | Other considerations include:]
– whether stop devices should be easily seen in contrast to their surroundings

[p42 | prose | Other considerations include:]
– the best access for workers – for example near where someone can be trapped in the machine

[p43 | prose | Other considerations include:]
– the environment the machine is used in (for example whether the device is exposed to dust, chemicals, temperature extremes or vibration)

[p43 | prose | Other considerations include:]
– the number of emergency stop devices needed (if the machine is large, several devices or pull wires may be needed)

[p43 | prose | Other considerations include:]
– a manual way to reset an emergency stop device

[p43 | prose | Other considerations include:]
– a regular testing routine to check the device still works.

[p43 | prose | Other considerations include:]
Badly placed emergency stop devices may slow shutdown in an emergency and encourage dangerous practices, such as:

[p43 | prose | Other considerations include:]
– reaching across moving parts

[p43 | prose | Other considerations include:]
– failing to shut down machinery when there is a problem

[p43 | prose | Other considerations include:]
– allowing one worker to start the machine while another is in a dangerous location (such as cleaning a machine).

[p43 | prose | Other considerations include:]
When there is more than one device, use a safe procedure so machinery cannot restart during maintenance or other temporary situations (such as a blockage of product). A lock-out and tag-out system is essential to isolate the machine from a power source to stop accidental start-up (see Section 6 Lockouts and Isolation procedures)

[p43 | prose | Colour coding]
Colour coding

[p43 | prose | Colour coding]
It is good practice to paint safety guard posts or frames yellow and any mesh black so it can be seen through more easily and staff do not need to open the guards for observation as much.

[p43 | prose | Colour coding]
It is good practice to:

[p43 | prose | Colour coding]
– use high visibility yellow paint for the guard that is different from the machine’s colour

[p43 | prose | Colour coding]
– paint surfaces behind the guard a bright or contrasting colour (like blue or red).

[p43 | prose | Colour coding]
This will make it easier for workers to see if a guard is out-of-place.

[p43 | prose | 8.2 Other control measures]
8.2 Other control measures

[p43 | prose | 8.2 Other control measures]
There are other control measures that minimise the risk of harm that can be used alongside machine guarding.

[p43 | prose | Developing work procedures]
Developing work procedures

[p43 | prose | Developing work procedures]
Work procedures are needed to make sure that hazard control measures are effective. Work procedures should:

[p43 | prose | Developing work procedures]
– define responsibilities for management, supervisors and workers

[p43 | prose | Developing work procedures]
– have systems to make sure appropriate guarding is procured and correctly installed

[p43 | prose | Developing work procedures]
– explain how workers will be trained and supervised to make sure the machinery is only used with the guarding in place

[p43 | prose | Developing work procedures]
– require workers to follow the work procedures

[p43 | prose | Developing work procedures]
– have arrangements to maintain the machine and guards

[p43 | prose | Developing work procedures]
– have a system for workers to report malfunctions or problems with machinery

[p43 | prose | Developing work procedures]
– have emergency procedures and training for staff on what to do.

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
Providing information on machinery

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
PCBUs have a duty to provide workers with training on the machinery being used.

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
The employer or person in control of the workplace should not let anyone use a machine unless they have had training on:

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
– the actual and potential hazards of the machine

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
– all the precautions to be taken.

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
This includes making sure that there is documented information for the user on the particular machine. This information could include:

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
– the machine’s intended use

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
– a description of the machine’s controls (especially emergency stops)

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
– operating instructions, including start-up preparations, process change-over and shutting down

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
– common faults and any reset instructions the user may need

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
– any guards or protective safety devices for particular hazards

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
– safety sign descriptions and details

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
– any prohibited uses or likely misuse

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
– any hazards the manufacturer could not eliminate

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
– any personal protective equipment that needs to be used

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
– any training that is needed.

[p44 | prose | Providing information on machinery]
This documentation could be in printed or electronic form but it should be readily available and easily accessible. It should also be in a form that workers of different languages and literacy levels can easily understand.

[p44 | prose | Maintenance]
Maintenance

[p44 | prose | Maintenance]
Work procedures should identify any maintenance needed to keep control measures effective. Looking at maintenance of control measures is an important part of the implementation process.

[p44 | prose | Maintenance]
So maintenance can be done safely, consider:

[p44 | prose | Maintenance]
– the ease of accessing parts

[p44 | prose | Maintenance]
– ensuring machinery parts are safe to maintain

[p44 | prose | Maintenance]
– ease of handling

[p44 | prose | Maintenance]
– designing machinery to reduce the range of tools and equipment needed for maintenance.

[p44 | prose | Talking with your workers]
Talking with your workers

[p44 | prose | Talking with your workers]
Involving your workers (and their health and safety representatives) in managing risks around the use of machinery is essential. It is also good practice.

[p44 | prose | Talking with your workers]
Your workers will likely know more about the hazards of their work than you do. These are tasks that they do every day. They know what can go wrong and they know the shortcuts – the actions and processes that make the job simpler or faster but which can often create risk.

[p44 | prose | Talking with your workers]
Involve them when:

[p44 | prose | Talking with your workers]
– new machinery is introduced

[p44 | prose | Talking with your workers]
– changes are made to existing machinery

[p44 | prose | Talking with your workers]
– changes are made to how they work.

[p44 | prose | Talking with your workers]
They can help develop measures to eliminate or minimise hazards before any incident or injury occurs.

[p45 | prose | Training and information]
Training and information

[p45 | prose | Training and information]
Employers must make sure, so far as is reasonably practicable, that all workers receive training appropriate to their work. The workers should:

[p45 | prose | Training and information]
– have adequate knowledge and experience so that they are not likely to cause harm to themselves or other people

[p45 | prose | Training and information]
– be adequately trained in the safe use of plant and machinery

[p45 | prose | Training and information]
– be adequately supervised by someone with knowledge and experience if they themselves are still gaining knowledge and experience.

[p45 | prose | Personal protective equipment (PPE)]
Personal protective equipment (PPE)

[p45 | prose | Personal protective equipment (PPE)]
Workers may need PPE when working with machinery particularly machines that make heat, fumes, noise or other hazards.

[p45 | prose | Personal protective equipment (PPE)]
PPE should be provided by the employer. If PPE is to be used it must be fit for purpose and:

[p45 | prose | Personal protective equipment (PPE)]
– suitable for the nature of the work and any hazards associated with the work

[p45 | prose | Personal protective equipment (PPE)]
– a suitable size and fit and be reasonably comfortable

[p45 | prose | Personal protective equipment (PPE)]
– be compatible with any other PPE the worker is required to wear.

[p45 | prose | Personal protective equipment (PPE)]
Workers must have training in how to wear and use their PPE.

[p45 | prose | Personal protective equipment (PPE)]
When assessing PPE needs, talk with your workers about what new risks the PPE may create, and how you can eliminate them or minimise them. For example, some types of hearing protection can affect workers’ situational awareness such as preventing them hearing approaching mobile plant.

[p45 | prose | 8.3 Safe systems of work]
8.3 Safe systems of work

[p45 | prose | 8.3 Safe systems of work]
A safe system of work is a formal work procedure developed after a systematic examination of a task to identify all the hazards. It is an administrative control.

[p45 | prose | 8.3 Safe systems of work]
It defines safe ways to work so hazards and risks are minimised. When hazards cannot be eliminated or isolated, you may need to use a safe system of work.

[p45 | prose | 8.3 Safe systems of work]
A safe system of work should never be used as the main hazard control without first assessing whether the hazards can be eliminated, or isolated with guarding, either provided by the manufacturer or retrofitted to existing machinery.

[p45 | prose | 8.3 Safe systems of work]
Workers need extra training, more supervision and other protective measures when using a safe system of work. These also need to be documented.

[p45 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures

[p45 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
Once control measures are in place, they should be regularly monitored and reviewed. To do this, it is useful to ask the following questions:

[p45 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– Have control measures been implemented as planned?

[p45 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– If control measures have not been implemented, why not, and what is happening in the meantime?

[p45 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– Are the control measures being used correctly?

[p45 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– Are the control measures working?

[p45 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– Have the control measures isolated or minimised the risk from the hazard as intended?

[p45 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– Have the control measures made any new hazards?

[p45 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– Have the control measures made any existing hazards worse?

[p46 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
To answer these questions, you should:

[p46 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– talk with workers, supervisors and any health and safety representatives

[p46 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– measure levels of exposure (for example, take noise measurements where a noise source was identified)

[p46 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– refer to manufacturers’ instructions

[p46 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– monitor incident reports

[p46 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– contact industry associations, unions, government bodies or health and safety consultants.

[p46 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
When deciding when to monitor and review control measures, consider:

[p46 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– the level of risk – high risk hazards need more frequent assessments

[p46 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– the type of work practices or machinery involved

[p46 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– whether new methods, tasks, equipment, hazards, operations, procedures, rosters or schedules have been introduced

[p46 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– whether the environment has changed

[p46 | prose | 8.4 Reviewing effectiveness of control measures]
– any indication that risks are not being controlled.

[p48 | prose | 9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures]
9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures

[p48 | prose | 9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures]
Lockout systems are used to safely isolate machinery from its power source. They are used when someone needs to inspect, repair, maintain, alter or clean the machine, or when it is to be withdrawn for assessment or repair.

[p48 | prose | 9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures]
The method used to isolate depends on the type of machinery.

[p48 | prose | 9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures]
Employers should develop these safe operating procedures with employees. Once a procedure has been put in place it should be strictly obeyed.

[p48 | prose | 9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures]
Employers need to make sure there is a safe system to isolate all machinery from power sources.

[p48 | prose | 9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures]
They must:

[p48 | prose | 9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures]
– have procedures to prepare a machine for the application of isolation devices, locks and tags

[p48 | prose | 9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures]
– train and instruct workers in the system so they are competent to isolate or lock-out and tag-out machinery

[p48 | prose | 9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures]
– give supervision to make sure that isolation procedures are followed.

[p48 | prose | 9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures]
Workers trained in the safe system of isolation for machinery need to make sure the system is followed at all times.

[p48 | prose | 9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures]
If the machine is powered by electricity, the employer or supervisor should have a qualified electrician remove the equipment from the source of electricity. Where other sources of power are used, the parts that are removed to achieve isolation should also be kept in a place where they cannot be accessed by other workers.

[p48 | prose | 9.1 Lockout systems and isolation procedures]
If access to machinery is required and it is not practical to stop it, the employer, principal or duty holders needs to ensure that a competent person is present.

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
Only people competent in the work systems and processes of the business, and the operation of the machinery should make decisions about the isolation of the machinery.

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
The competent person is the key person to:

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
– stop and isolate the machine

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
– minimise any risks associated with identified hazards (including telling any workers who may be affected by isolating the machine).

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
The competent person needs to make sure:

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
– all energy sources are de-energised and isolated using an isolation device, and locked out using a lock-out device

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
– all energy-isolating devices are activated and all switches and valves are in the off or safe position, to stop any attempts to activate the machine

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
– stored energy is released or restrained, including, for example, completing the cycle of a flywheel, releasing steam and bleeding valves

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
– an out-of-service tag is fixed to the machine and danger tags are fixed to the energy sources and operating controls

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
– tests are in place to de-energise and isolate all energy sources, to make sure the machine cannot be re-energised/ re-started

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
– the machinery is isolated before any inspection, repair, maintenance, alterations, cleaning or withdrawal happens.

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
The competent person who isolated the machinery needs to be the one to remove the lock-out equipment and make the machine operational again.

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
A procedure should be in place where this is not possible (such as where work is done over a number of shifts or the worker has gone home sick).

[p49 | prose | 9.2 A competent person is key to planned isolation]
If the competent person cannot complete all steps in a planned isolation, they need to make sure a competent person develops written procedures and that these are followed by the person doing the work.

[p49 | prose | 9.3 Isolation, hold cards and lockout devices]
9.3 Isolation, hold cards and lockout devices

[p49 | prose | 9.3 Isolation, hold cards and lockout devices]
Chains, clasps and locks are examples of devices that can be used to isolate machinery. Isolation devices should be reliable and clear.

[p49 | prose | 9.3 Isolation, hold cards and lockout devices]
Each lock should:

[p49 | prose | 9.3 Isolation, hold cards and lockout devices]
– be strong enough to take physical abuse, either intentional or unintentional

[p49 | prose | 9.3 Isolation, hold cards and lockout devices]
– be made of material suitable for the environment

[p49 | prose | 9.3 Isolation, hold cards and lockout devices]
– have only one key and one owner who is responsible for it.

[p49 | prose | 9.3 Isolation, hold cards and lockout devices]
Master or spare keys should be kept in a designated place, away from the workplace and under the control of a competent person.

[p49 | prose | 9.3 Isolation, hold cards and lockout devices]
There needs to be strict procedures about when to use spare keys. They need to only be used in an emergency after thorough safety checks are done.

[p49 | prose | 9.3 Isolation, hold cards and lockout devices]
Lock-out and tag-out cards should be used together and be attached to the power controls of isolated machinery. This reduces the chance of someone starting the machinery inadvertently.

[p49 | prose | 9.3 Isolation, hold cards and lockout devices]
Lockout and tagout cards need to clearly state that under no circumstances should the machinery be connected to the power source until the hold card is removed by the person named on the tagout card.

10.2 Basic rules for guard design

[p53 | prose | 10.2 Basic rules for guard design]
The basic rules for guard design are:

[p53 | prose | 10.2 Basic rules for guard design]
– use materials of suitable strength and good quality

[p53 | prose | 10.2 Basic rules for guard design]
– use the right guard. Custom-designed is best – poorly designed or inappropriate guards can cause injuries

[p53 | prose | 10.2 Basic rules for guard design]
– the environment and the needs of operators and maintenance workers affect how well a guard works.

[p53 | prose | 10.2 Basic rules for guard design]
If a guard is used from another machine, check carefully that it:

[p53 | prose | 10.2 Basic rules for guard design]
– fits the target machine

[p53 | prose | 10.2 Basic rules for guard design]
– is strong enough for the new use

[p53 | prose | 10.2 Basic rules for guard design]
– controls the risk

[p53 | prose | 10.2 Basic rules for guard design]
– is not faulty.

[p53 | prose | 10.2 Basic rules for guard design]
When selecting or designing a guard, think about how it might be misused or how workers may set out to defeat it. It is human nature to try and find an easier or quicker way of doing something. This applies very much with operators, machinery and guarding.

[p53 | prose | 10.2 Basic rules for guard design]
Talk with your operators and workers. They are the ones who will tell you about how they actually do the task (rather than how you think they should do the task) and the short-cuts they will look for.

[p53 | prose | 10.2 Basic rules for guard design]
Design your guarding so there is less possibility (or less incentive) to try and defeat it.

[p56 | prose | 10.9 Conveyors (bulk handling)]
10.9 Conveyors (bulk handling)

[p56 | prose | 10.9 Conveyors (bulk handling)]
Conveyors move materials from one place to another. Types of conveyors include belt, auger, roller, and bucket conveyors.

[p56 | prose | Hazards]
Hazards

[p56 | prose | Hazards]
The main hazards of a conveyor are the many in-running nip-points, which can entangle, crush and abrade people. The drive system can also pose risks of entanglement or abrasion.

[p56 | prose | Control measures]
Control measures

[p56 | prose | Control measures]
Fixed guards that enclose in-running nip-points and the drive mechanism are usually the best way to guard conveyors.

[p56 | prose | Control measures]

[p57 | prose | Emergency stop controls]

[p57 | prose | 10.10 Press brakes]
10.10 Press brakes

[p57 | prose | 10.10 Press brakes]
A press brake is a variable stroke machine generally used for straight bending and forming of material, such as sheet metal and heavy gauge material.

[p57 | prose | Hazards]
Hazards

[p57 | prose | Hazards]
For press brakes, the main hazards are:

[p57 | prose | Hazards]
– the die sets mounted to the main moving beam and table coming together to form the product

[p57 | prose | Hazards]
– the work piece and the press frame coming together in the fold-forming process.

[p57 | prose | Hazards]
The impact from both can have a pinching, crushing, cutting or shearing motion, which creates a risk to the operator of being crushed or cut.

[p57 | prose | Hazards]
Drive belts on press brakes have in-running nip points, which present a risk of entanglement and abrasion. Hydraulic hoses may leak or burst, causing slip hazards and workers getting sprayed with hydraulic fluids under pressure.

[p58 | prose | Control measures]
Control measures

[p58 | prose | Control measures]
The front dies of a press brake and its sides and rear require guarding. Three forms of guarding for the front of the dies on a press brake are:

[p58 | prose | Control measures]
– a fixed guard

[p58 | prose | Control measures]
– interlocked guard

[p58 | prose | Control measures]
– a light or presence-sensing system.

[p58 | prose | Control measures]
Where workers have to hold or stabilise the material, or need frequent access to closing dies, presence-sensing devices may be required to ensure safe operation. Presence-sensing devices may be light curtains or light beams. Automatic stops should also be guarded and back-gauging equipment is recommended.

[p58 | prose | Control measures]
Presence-sensing devices (cameras, light curtains or light beams) may not protect the operator in all circumstances.

[p58 | prose | Control measures]
Press brake with fixed guards and pressure- sensitive light curtain

[p58 | prose | Safe system of work for press brakes]
Safe system of work for press brakes

[p58 | prose | Safe system of work for press brakes]
On occasions it may not be possible to perform work with the guarding system in place. Removal of or turning off a guard should only occur if the guard makes it impracticable to perform close work or jobbing and a hazard and risk assessment is carried out by a competent person. A safe system of work should be developed in conjunction with the employer and operators and approved by a competent person with appropriate knowledge and experience of machine safety.

[p58 | prose | Safe system of work for press brakes]
In cases where guarding of any moving parts of the plant does not eliminate risks of entanglement, or where it is not practicable to guard the parts, people should not be able to operate or pass close to the moving part unless a safe system of work is in place to reduce the risks.

[p58 | prose | Safe system of work for press brakes]
Additional training, experience and higher levels of supervision, and other protective measures may be required and will need to be documented.

[p58 | prose | Safe system of work for press brakes]
For more information on safe systems of work see section 8.3 in this guideline.

[p59 | prose | Closed tool method]
Closed tool method

[p59 | prose | Closed tool method]
The closed tool method reduces the press brake’s opening to 6 mm which limits the risk of introducing a part of the body into this hazardous zone. The 6 mm is measured from distance between the point of the upper tool and the top of the bottom die.

[p59 | prose | Closed tool method]
Where possible, the closed tool method should be used with a safety light curtain, a laser beam device or a two-hand control device.

[p59 | prose | 10.11 Robotics]
10.11 Robotics

[p59 | prose | 10.11 Robotics]
Using robots can remove the more traditional hazards of working with machinery. They can do high-risk work, for example in the biotechnology field.

[p59 | prose | 10.11 Robotics]
It is wrong to think that robotic operations are safe just because there is little or no worker interaction. Hazards when using robotics can come from:

[p59 | prose | 10.11 Robotics]
– errors during use

[p59 | prose | 10.11 Robotics]
– ejection of materials

[p59 | prose | 10.11 Robotics]
– trapping points

[p59 | prose | 10.11 Robotics]
– failures and malfunctions.

[p59 | prose | 10.11 Robotics]
Hazards can also come up during installation, repair and maintenance. There may also be biological, chemical or environmental hazards.

[p59 | prose | 10.11 Robotics]
A hazard assessment should be done to ensure workers’ safety during all phases of the machinery’s life and use. Follow a hazard management process (with reference to the manufacturer’s instructions) during installation or commissioning, testing, start-up, repair and maintenance.

[p59 | prose | Hazards and risks]
Hazards and risks

[p59 | prose | Hazards and risks]
Robots have inherent dangers. Some of the hazards of industrial robot use include:

[p59 | prose | Hazards and risks]
– Impact. Robots can move at high speed in an unexpected direction either in a straight line or circular directions. The robot can also eject work pieces, off- cuts or molten metal. Workers are at risk from being hit by the robot or parts of the work.

[p59 | prose | Hazards and risks]
– Trapping points. These can be made by the robot’s movement or other machinery – such as work carriages, pallets or transfer mechanisms. With the robot itself, trapping points are found on the arm of the robot, between the arm and the column, and between the arm and fixed objects. Workers can be crushed by or entangled with the robot, including being crushed between a rapidly rotating robot arm and barriers close by.

[p59 | prose | Hazards and risks]
– Control errors. These come from faults in the control system of the robot (such as software, electrical interference, programme corruption and sub- controls associated with the electrics, hydraulics and pneumatics).

[p59 | prose | Hazards and risks]
– Human error. These can happen during programming, teaching, maintenance and repair, working close to the robot or at loading or unloading stations.

[p59 | prose | Hazards and risks]
– Failure or malfunction. Electrics, hydraulics and pneumatics can all create hazards when they fail.

[p59 | prose | Hazards and risks]
– Biological or chemical hazards. These can happen when robots are used to reduce the risks from hazardous or infectious processes. Workers may risk inhaling or absorbing hazardous substances. In this case, the work process needs close attention, along with any breakdown or emergency procedures that may be needed – such as for spillage, contamination or breach of the system.

[p59 | prose | Hazards and risks]
– Environmental hazards. These include dust vapours, fumes, lasers, noise, radiation and flammable and explosive atmospheres that can cause serious harm, such as burns and inhaling or absorbing hazardous substances and hearing loss.

[p60 | prose | Control measures]
Control measures

[p60 | prose | Control measures]
Industrial robots can be made safe using one or more guarding and presence- sensing devices. Control measures include:

[p60 | prose | Control measures]
– enclosing the robot

[p60 | prose | Control measures]
– restricting access

[p60 | prose | Control measures]
– turning the robot off when people are near.

[p60 | prose | Control measures]
There are three levels around a robot workstation with different hazards and precautions to be considered.

[p60 | prose | Control measures]
1. Level 1 is the workstation perimeter, usually a physical barrier guarded with an interlock gate and possibly with presence-sensing devices.

[p60 | prose | Control measures]
2. Level 2 is within the workstation (where the robot moves). Here safety systems should detect if someone is present, usually with presence-sensing devices.

[p60 | prose | Control measures]
3. Level 3 is contact with the robot. A safety system should detect a person touching the robot and stop it moving immediately using safety trip devices or sensors.

[p60 | prose | Control measures]
vertical light curtains

[p60 | prose | Control measures]
FIGURE 16: Robot cell showing levels 1,2 and 3

[p60 | prose | Control measures]
Fixed or distance guards at (Level 1) are practical as long as the guard does not interfere with the mechanism of the robot. Someone should have to use tools to remove the guards to enter the restricted danger area. Guards or fences should be placed so people cannot reach into a restricted area.

[p61 | prose | Control measures]
Any openings for feeding material in should be designed to keep every part of a person away from any hazard.

[p61 | prose | Control measures]
To stop trapping, any fixed barriers should be at least 500 mm from the robot work envelope (extreme reach of the robot arm and tooling).

[p61 | prose | Control measures]
Design and place presence-sensing devices (such as photoelectric curtains) to detect if anyone enters a restricted space or danger area (Level 2). The device should stop the automatic operation of the robot when entry is detected. Operation must also stop if this device fails.

[p61 | prose | Control measures]
You can use laser scanners or pressure-sensitive mats as a back-up safety protection for high-risk machinery in areas inside the primary light curtain. This way the system cannot restart while someone is inside the area protected by a light curtain.

[p61 | prose | Additional control measures]
Additional control measures

[p61 | prose | Additional control measures]
Because robots are highly technical and programmable, consider extra safeguards beyond just guarding moving parts. These include making sure:

[p61 | prose | Additional control measures]
– only competent people can access and start the robot system

[p61 | prose | Additional control measures]
– no one can access the robot through or by removing associated machinery, such as conveyors, transfer systems, loading stations or trolleys.

[p61 | prose | Additional control measures]
If people have to enter the robot cell (Level 2) while the robot is working, the control system should make sure the robot runs with reduced force. The robot also needs a sensor to stop it immediately if it hits someone.

[p61 | prose | Additional control measures]
Safe operating procedures can also minimise some of the risks of working with robotics. A safe work system should have procedures for entry, including who can access the robot to do identified tasks, maintenance and repair. Inspecting and maintaining a robot can present different hazards from working with the robot. Assess all hazards for risks.

[p61 | prose | Additional control measures]
Staff must be trained to control the hazards of working with industrial robot machines. Inadequate training can increase risks at most stages of robot operation.

[p61 | prose | Control systems]
Control systems

[p61 | prose | Control systems]
Robots usually have programmable electronic start and control systems. These control systems should be protected from unauthorised access, such as by putting them in a lockable cabinet or room. Make and place controls so people cannot accidently start the robot. This can be done a number of ways, including shrouding, guarding, gating or appropriate positioning.

[p61 | prose | Control systems]
If people can access the robot, it needs to be isolated from its power source.

[p61 | prose | Control systems]
For more information see:

Master switches

[p61 | prose | Master switches]
Robots should have master switches to cut power to any moving part of the robot.

[p64 | prose | 11.1 Buying second-hand machinery]
11.1 Buying second-hand machinery

[p64 | prose | 11.1 Buying second-hand machinery]
The duties of suppliers do not apply to the sale of second-hand plant, and second-hand plant that is sold ‘as is’. PCBUs purchasing second-hand machinery still have a duty to make sure that the plant or machinery does not create risks for their workers.

[p64 | prose | 11.1 Buying second-hand machinery]
If you are a PCBU buying second-hand plant or machinery, you should:

[p64 | prose | 11.1 Buying second-hand machinery]
– make sure it is suitable for what you want it to do (for example, is it powerful enough?)

[p64 | prose | 11.1 Buying second-hand machinery]
– check as much as possible the history and previous use of the machine. Make sure it is not at the end of its life.

[p64 | prose | 11.1 Buying second-hand machinery]
– get as much information as possible on the specifications, use, characteristics and maintenance of the machine. This might be from the seller or from the manufacturer.

[p64 | prose | 11.1 Buying second-hand machinery]
– think about where you are going to be using it. Is there sufficient space or could the position cause risks for the operator or other workers?

[p64 | prose | 11.1 Buying second-hand machinery]
– look closely at any guarding the machine currently has. Is it built to a suitable standard? Will it work in your operation?

[p64 | prose | 11.1 Buying second-hand machinery]
– consider having it checked by a certified engineer

[p64 | prose | 11.2 Buying machinery from overseas with CE marking]
11.2 Buying machinery from overseas with CE marking

[p64 | prose | 11.2 Buying machinery from overseas with CE marking]
Under HSWA, PCBUs have a duty to ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, that machinery or plant is safe.

[p64 | prose | 11.2 Buying machinery from overseas with CE marking]
The benchmark for machinery safety in New Zealand is that the machinery meets AS/NZS 4024 standards.

[p64 | prose | 11.2 Buying machinery from overseas with CE marking]
A CE marking is a mandatory mark for certain products sold on the European market. It indicates compliance with the European Union Directives.

[p64 | prose | 11.2 Buying machinery from overseas with CE marking]
A machine built in Europe will generally be built to CE certification and would generally meet New Zealand standards. A machine built outside of Europe that uses the CE certification may not be built to those standards.

[p64 | prose | 11.2 Buying machinery from overseas with CE marking]
So, a machine with CE mark does not mean that it automatically meets New Zealand’s specific legal requirements under HSWA.

[p64 | prose | 11.2 Buying machinery from overseas with CE marking]
To be certain that an imported machine meets AS/NZS 4024 standards, it is recommended that it is checked by a suitable competent person, such as a Chartered Professional Engineer (CPEng), a Certified Machinery Safety Expert or a recognised accredited laboratory.

Appendix 3: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA

[p71 | appendix | Appendix 3: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
A PCBU in the supply chain (upstream) also has a duty to ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, that the work they do or the things they provide to other workplaces do not create health and safety risks.

[p71 | appendix | Appendix 3: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
An upstream PCBU is a business that:

[p71 | appendix | Appendix 3: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
– designs plant, substances, or structures

[p71 | appendix | Appendix 3: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
– manufactures plant, substances, or structures

[p71 | appendix | Appendix 3: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
– imports plant, substances, or structures

[p71 | appendix | Appendix 3: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
– supplies plant, substances, or structures

[p71 | appendix | Appendix 3: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
– installs, constructs or commissions plant or structures.

[p71 | appendix | Appendix 3: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
Upstream businesses are in a strong position to eliminate or minimise risk. They can influence and sometimes eliminate health and safety risks through designing, manufacturing, importing or supplying products that are safe for the end user.

[p71 | prose | Example]
Example

[p71 | prose | Example]
A worker using a badly designed or poorly manufactured saw may be at risk of injury. This risk should have been eliminated or minimised, so far as was reasonably practicable, by the designer or manufacturer.

[p76 | prose | Entanglement hazards]
Entanglement is when someone is caught in a machine by loose items (such as clothing, gloves, ties, jewellery, long hair, cleaning rags, bandages or rough material being fed into the machine). Contact that can lead to entanglement includes: - touching a single rotating surface (such as plain shafting, couplings, spindles, chucks, lead screws, mandrels or rotating work pieces including plain bar material)

[p76 | prose | Entanglement hazards]
- catching on projections or in gaps. Belt fasteners and other projecting items (such as keys, set screws and cotter pins) are typical projection hazards. Fan blades, spoked wheels (such as pulleys, sprockets, gear wheels and flywheels), mixer and beater arms and spiked cylinders create gap-related hazard - touching moving materials in motion (such as in centrifuges, tumble driers and dough mixers or swarf) - reaching between counter rotating parts (such as gear wheels, rolling mills) - reaching between a rotating part and another part moving along it (such as a power transmission belt and its pulley, a chain and chain wheel, a rack and pinion, a conveyor belt and any of its pulleys, a rope and its storage reel)

[p76 | prose | Entanglement hazards]
- reaching between rotating and fixed parts (such as flywheels and the machinery bed, screw or worm conveyors and their casings, mixers, extruder screw and barrel, the edge of an abrasive wheel, or an incorrectly adjusted work rest).

[p86 | prose | Questions to ask to identify hazards:]
Questions to ask to identify hazards:

[p86 | prose | Questions to ask to identify hazards:]
– Where fixed guards are provided, are they of substantial construction and secured into position while machinery is in operation?

[p86 | prose | Questions to ask to identify hazards:]
– Where interlocked guards are provided, do they prevent operation of the machinery when open, and are the guards prevented from opening while the machinery is in operation?

[p86 | prose | Questions to ask to identify hazards:]
– Where a presence-sensing system is used, does it operate as intended and stop the machinery when light beams or sensors are interrupted?

[p86 | prose | Questions to ask to identify hazards:]
– Do guards protect against hazards at the rear and sides of machinery?

[p86 | prose | Questions to ask to identify hazards:]
– Are pre-operational checks conducted to ensure safety features are in working order?

[p86 | prose | Questions to ask to identify hazards:]
– Are adequate isolation procedures provided for maintenance?

[p86 | prose | Questions to ask to identify hazards:]
– Are manufacturers’ manuals available?

[p86 | prose | Questions to ask to identify hazards:]
– Are machine controls protected to prevent unintentional operation, clearly marked and within easy reach of the operator?

[p86 | prose | Questions to ask to identify hazards:]
– Are warning signs and decals clearly visible?

[p86 | prose | Questions to ask to identify hazards:]
– Where it is not practical to provide guarding and people are required to operate or pass close to dangerous moving parts, is a safe system of work in place to reduce risks?

[p86 | prose | Questions to ask to identify hazards:]
– Is it practical to provide a higher level of guarding than currently provided? – Are operators and maintenance workers adequately trained, familiar with the operation and set up of machinery and able to demonstrate safety features?

[p87 | prose | 2. Assess the level of risk for identified hazards]
2. Assess the level of risk for identified hazards

[p87 | prose | 2. Assess the level of risk for identified hazards]
– Gather information about the hazard(s). Consult with relevant people, including workers.

[p87 | prose | 2. Assess the level of risk for identified hazards]
– Work out the likelihood of an injury or harm occurring. Consider how many people are likely to be exposed to the hazard and for how long. Take into account different situations or conditions that might exist at the workplace and could increase risk, such as changes to operations, inspection, cleaning, maintenance, servicing and repairs and new or inexperienced workers.

[p87 | prose | 2. Assess the level of risk for identified hazards]
– Use the information you have gathered to assess the potential consequences of any injury or harm occurring from the hazard(s); for example, whether people die or suffer major, minor or negligible injuries.

3. Control measures

[p87 | prose | 3. Control measures]
