[p16 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
1.1 What are these guidelines about?

[p16 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
These guidelines provide advice on how to manage the health and safety risks road and roadside workers are exposed to while at work. They can help persons conducting a business or undertaking (PCBUs) to meet their duties under the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA).

[p16 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
The guidelines are grouped into five parts:

[p16 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
Part A has a general introduction to applying a risk management approach in the road and roadside work environment, and guidance on how to provide for road and roadside worker health and safety throughout the contracting chain.

[p16 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
Part B offers good practice advice for managing common health risks for road and roadside workers.

[p16 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
Part C offers good practice advice for managing common safety risks for road and roadside workers.

[p16 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
Part D offers good practice advice regarding worker facilities, training and certifications, personal protective equipment (PPE), and worker inductions.

[p16 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
Appendices 1–6 include a glossary of terms and more detailed explanations of key health and safety duties under HSWA.

[p16 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]
1.2 Who should read these guidelines?

[p16 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]
These guidelines are for any PCBU who is involved in road or roadside work at any point in the contracting chain, for example:

[p16 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]
– a PCBU that has workers working on a road or roadside

[p16 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]
– a PCBU that has control of a road or roadside and contracts work to be done on that road or roadside

[p16 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]
– a PCBU that subcontracts other PCBUs to do work on a road or roadside

[p16 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]
– a PCBU that is responsible for designing or implementing temporary traffic management plans for road or roadside work.

[p17 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]
These guidelines are written to accommodate the wide range of knowledge and experience of PCBUs that may refer to it. In some instances, PCBUs may need to refer to relevant experts for further guidance on how to implement or execute recommended practices.

[p17 | prose | 1.2 Who should read these guidelines?]
These guidelines may also be useful for health and safety professionals when providing health and safety advice to PCBUs who are involved with road or roadside work, or those who are part of the contracting chain.

[p20 | prose | 2.1 Introduction to risk management]
2.1 Introduction to risk management

[p20 | prose | 2.1 Introduction to risk management]
This section offers guidance for PCBUs on ways you can apply good risk management principles in the road and roadside work environment.

[p20 | prose | 2.1 Introduction to risk management]
Risks to health and safety arise from people being exposed to a hazard (a source or cause of harm).

[p20 | prose | 2.1 Introduction to risk management]
As a PCBU, you must manage your work risks so that workers and other peoples’ health and safety is not put at risk by the work that you do.

[p20 | prose | 2.1 Introduction to risk management]
Risk management is about:

[p20 | prose | 2.1 Introduction to risk management]
– identifying hazards and assessing risks

[p20 | prose | 2.1 Introduction to risk management]
– applying control measures to eliminate or minimise those risks

[p20 | prose | 2.1 Introduction to risk management]
– regularly reviewing control measures.

[p20 | prose | 2.1 Introduction to risk management]
You must consult with your workers and their representatives at all steps of the risk management process. For more information, see Appendix 6: Worker engagement, participation and representation

[p20 | prose | 2.1 Introduction to risk management]
If you are planning work that will likely be undertaken by contractors and subcontractors, where reasonably practicable, you should consult with those contractors and subcontractors who are going to be doing the work when planning out how to manage risks. For more specific details, see Section 3.0: Managing risk throughout the contracting chain

[p20 | prose | 2.2 Identify hazards]
2.2 Identify hazards

[p20 | prose | 2.2 Identify hazards]
With your workers, identify hazards that could be associated with the work activity.

[p20 | prose | 2.2 Identify hazards]
A hazard is a potential source or cause of harm (such as a physical injury, or harm to a person’s health or their mental wellbeing) and can include a person’s behaviour.

[p20 | prose | 2.2 Identify hazards]
Every work environment or work activity will be different. Looking at your work environment and thinking about things that could go wrong may help you to identify hazards. Reviewing your incident and injury records (including near misses) may also help identify hazards.

[p21 | prose | 2.2 Identify hazards]

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
2.3 Assess the risk

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
You will need to carry out a risk assessment for each hazard you have identified. You should involve your workers in this process.

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
Below are some of the things you should consider when carrying out a risk assessment.

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
Who might be exposed to the hazard?

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
– Think about who could be involved in, or near, the work activity. For example:

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
- workers

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
- contractors

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
- site visitors

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
- motorised road users

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
- pedestrians, cyclists, and other vulnerable road users.

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
– Do you have any workers that could be considered vulnerable? For example:

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
- new, young, or inexperienced workers

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
- migrant workers

[p22 | prose | 2.3 Assess the risk]
- workers with low literacy levels (they may not be able to understand written safety information).

[p22 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?

[p22 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– Is the hazard present all the time, some of the time, or very rarely?

[p22 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– What factors could influence when the hazard will occur or be present?

[p22 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
- Are there certain times of the year that are busier than others?

[p22 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
- Are there likely to be seasonal variations to the level of risk?

[p22 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
- Are there certain activities that have increased exposure to the hazard?

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
How could workers or others be physically harmed? This could include:

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– being hit by a vehicle

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– being hit by mobile plant

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– being physically assaulted by public road users or other workers – falling from a vehicle or some other height

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– being hit by falling objects

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– being injured by sharp objects or pinch points on work equipment or machinery

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– being hit by items falling off a vehicle (unsecured or unstable loads)

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– being injured as a result of improper use of a vehicle or mobile plant

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– being injured when carrying out manual tasks

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– slipping, tripping, or falling from slippery, uneven, steep, or unstable surfaces.

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
How could workers’ health or mental wellbeing be harmed? For example, this could happen:

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– through exposure to hazardous levels of fumes or excessive noise or biological or chemical matter

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– through excessive use of vibrating plant

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– through a lack of access to facilities such as toilets or sanitation, and shelter and healthy food options

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– through fatigue or stress from working long hours or shift work disrupting sleep patterns

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– from verbal abuse by public road users, other staff, management, or contractors

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– from dealing with the results of road crashes or traumatic situations (including having to work at or near sites of previous fatalities).

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
How severe could the harm be?

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– Could the hazard result in serious harm or a fatality?

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
How likely are these consequences?

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– How likely is it that someone could be harmed by the hazard?

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
How does or could the hazard interact with other hazards or risk?

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– Does the risk presented by the hazard increase or change in the presence of other hazards? For example, working near a sharp bend in the road could be made even more dangerous if fog further reduces driver visibility.

[p23 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
Decide which risks to deal with first. Risks with potentially significant, life changing consequences such as serious injury or death, or chronic ill-health should be prioritised.

[p23 | prose | 2.4 Manage the risk]
2.4 Manage the risk

[p23 | prose | 2.4 Manage the risk]
You must take all reasonably practicable actions to eliminate risks to health and safety. Many of the examples listed above can be eliminated through well considered design and planning.

[p23 | prose | 2.4 Manage the risk]
If elimination is not reasonably practicable, you must minimise those risks so far as is reasonably practicable.

[p23 | prose | 2.4 Manage the risk]
The ways of managing risks are ranked from the highest level of protection and reliability to the lowest. This ranking is known as the hierarchy of control measures.

[p23 | prose | 2.4 Manage the risk]
Using the hierarchy of control measures to manage risks will help you make sure you are using the most effective control measures first (see Figure 2).

[p24 | prose | FIGURE 2: Hierarchy of control measures]
FIGURE 2: Hierarchy of control measures

[p24 | prose | FIGURE 2: Hierarchy of control measures]
Common risk areas for road and roadside workers and recommended control measures are covered in more detail in Part B and Part C of these guidelines.

[p24 | prose | FIGURE 2: Hierarchy of control measures]
You should use a combination of control measures. Using multiple control measures means that if a single control measure fails, workers will not be left without any protection.

[p24 | prose | Reasonably practicable]
Reasonably practicable

[p24 | prose | Reasonably practicable]
Eliminating the risk should be the preferred approach when deciding what control measure is the most appropriate.

[p24 | prose | Reasonably practicable]
However, elimination may not always be reasonably practicable, and you may need to consider lower-level control measures.

[p24 | prose | Reasonably practicable]
When considering whether you can use a particular control measure to ensure health and safety, you can take into account and weigh up relevant matters including:

[p24 | prose | Reasonably practicable]
– the likelihood of the risk concerned occurring or workers being exposed to the hazard

[p24 | prose | Reasonably practicable]
– the degree of harm that might result

[p24 | prose | Reasonably practicable]
– what the person concerned knows, or ought reasonably to know, about:

[p24 | prose | Reasonably practicable]
the hazard or risk

[p24 | prose | Reasonably practicable]
- ways of eliminating or minimising the risk

[p24 | prose | Reasonably practicable]
– the availability and suitability of ways to eliminate or minimise the risk

[p24 | prose | Reasonably practicable]
– after assessing the extent of the risk and the available ways of eliminating or minimising the risk, the cost associated with available ways of eliminating or minimising the risk, including whether the cost is grossly disproportionate to the risk.

[p24 | prose | Reasonably practicable]
For more information, see WorkSafe’s fact sheet: Reasonably practicable

[p25 | prose | 2.6 Managing risk across a system]
2.6 Managing risk across a system

[p25 | prose | 2.6 Managing risk across a system]
In the road and roadside work environment, think about how you will manage risk across the whole system – that is:

[p25 | prose | 2.6 Managing risk across a system]
– across a project, from design to completion and future maintenance

[p25 | prose | 2.6 Managing risk across a system]
– across all work activities, workers, and others affected by the work (road users and the general public).

[p25 | prose | 2.6 Managing risk across a system]
Rather than managing each risk in isolation, think about how control measures for each risk could impact on other parts of the system or the workflow, to avoid risks being transferred or created elsewhere.

[p25 | prose | 2.6 Managing risk across a system]
You may need to weigh up the benefits of a particular control measure against any potential risks it creates elsewhere and look at a solution that provides the least total risk to all affected parties.

Train workers to recognise unanticipated risks and know what control measures to use. Workers need to be able recognise when a situation has become unsafe. They also need to have the authority to stop work if they decide they are unable to properly manage the risks they have identified.

[p28 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to managing risk throughout the contracting chain]
3.1 Introduction to managing risk throughout the contracting chain

[p28 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to managing risk throughout the contracting chain]
This section offers guidance for PCBUs on ways to manage shared health and safety duties while working in a contracting chain in the road and roadside work environment.

[p28 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to managing risk throughout the contracting chain]
Contracting is when a PCBU (called the contracting PCBU), hires another PCBU (called a contractor) to carry out work for them.

[p28 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to managing risk throughout the contracting chain]
A contractor may also hire a PCBU (called a subcontractor). This is known as a contracting chain and is the most common business model used in road and roadside work.

[p29 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to managing risk throughout the contracting chain]
Contract types may include:

[p29 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to managing risk throughout the contracting chain]
– project contracts (such as construction, installation, or upgrade work)

[p29 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to managing risk throughout the contracting chain]
– maintenance and repair activity contracts (including towing)

[p29 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to managing risk throughout the contracting chain]
– service and monitoring contracts (such as waste and recycling collection).

[p29 | prose | 3.1 Introduction to managing risk throughout the contracting chain]
Contractors and subcontractors may be individuals or businesses. Contractors and subcontractors (and their employees) who are carrying out work for the contracting PCBU are considered to be workers of the contracting PCBU under HSWA.

[p29 | prose | 3.2 Overlapping duties in the contracting chain]
3.2 Overlapping duties in the contracting chain

[p29 | prose | 3.2 Overlapping duties in the contracting chain]
PCBUs operating in a contracting chain will have shared health and safety duties with other PCBUs in that contracting chain (known as overlapping duties).

[p29 | prose | 3.2 Overlapping duties in the contracting chain]
All PCBUs, so far as is reasonably practicable, must consult, cooperate, and coordinate together to manage their overlapping duties.

[p29 | prose | 3.2 Overlapping duties in the contracting chain]
There are four main points to remember about overlapping duties:

[p29 | prose | 3.2 Overlapping duties in the contracting chain]
– PCBUs have a duty to consult, cooperate with, and coordinate activities with all other PCBUs they share overlapping duties with, so far as is reasonably practicable.

[p29 | prose | 3.2 Overlapping duties in the contracting chain]
– PCBUs cannot contract out of their health and safety duties or push risk onto others in a contracting chain.

[p29 | prose | 3.2 Overlapping duties in the contracting chain]
– PCBUs can enter into reasonable agreements with other PCBUs to make sure that everyone’s health and safety duties are met. But PCBUs must monitor each other, to make sure each PCBU continues to do what was agreed.

[p29 | prose | 3.2 Overlapping duties in the contracting chain]
– The more influence and control a PCBU has over a work site or a health and safety matter, the more responsibility they are likely to have.

[p29 | prose | 3.2 Overlapping duties in the contracting chain]
The size of the PCBU, or its financial resources, does not automatically equate to its ability to influence and control health and safety matters. PCBUs need to consult, cooperate, and coordinate with all other PCBUs (regardless of their size) to make sure everyone can meet their health and safety duties.

[p29 | prose | 3.2 Overlapping duties in the contracting chain]
For more general information on managing overlapping duties, see Appendix 2: Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 duties

[p29 | prose | 3.3 Expectations for contracting PCBUs]
3.3 Expectations for contracting PCBUs

[p29 | prose | 3.3 Expectations for contracting PCBUs]
The contracting PCBU is usually the initiator of a contract for work or services. Contracting PCBUs are sometimes referred to as the client or principal. Tenders may be put out and a procurement process is usually followed.

[p29 | prose | 3.3 Expectations for contracting PCBUs]
In road and roadside work the most common contracting PCBUs will be:

[p29 | prose | 3.3 Expectations for contracting PCBUs]
– road controlling authorities (RCAs) such as Waka Kotahi for state highways or local government authorities for local roads

[p29 | prose | 3.3 Expectations for contracting PCBUs]
– utility or service providers (such as internet or electricity companies)

[p29 | prose | 3.3 Expectations for contracting PCBUs]

3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
Cost-based procurement and contract negotiations should not weaken health and safety standards. Contracting PCBUs must make sure people working in their contracting chain are healthy and safe while at work, so far as is reasonably practicable.

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
Where relevant, the contracting PCBU should take all reasonably practicable steps to eliminate health and safety risks at the design stage of a project before going out to tender.

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
The contracting PCBU should make health and safety requirements or desired health and safety outcomes very clear to prospective contractors. For example:

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
– making sure the notice of procurement clearly says that agreed health and safety requirements also apply to all subcontractors in the contracting chain

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
– where health and safety standards are prescribed, such as in a request for tender (RFT), making sure they are prescriptive enough to allow potential contractors and subcontractors to make cost estimates as accurate as possible

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
– considering specifying an agreed minimum provisional sum to be allocated to meet agreed health and safety requirements. This can minimise the risk of health and safety funding being reallocated to other areas by the contractor or subcontractor if cost overruns occur in other areas

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
– considering a request for proposal (RFP) that allows scope for prospective contractors to propose better or more innovative approaches to managing health and safety risks and be willing to consider funding and supporting such proposals. This could be achieved by setting out desired health and safety outcomes and allowing the prospective contractors to propose an approach that they believe will meet or exceed the desired outcomes

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
– using data and knowledge gained from previous projects that have had successful health and safety outcomes to help inform good practice expectations

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
– allowing sufficient time for responders to prepare a well considered proposal or tender.

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
When reviewing proposals or tenders, the contracting PCBU should avoid focusing mainly on the price or timeframes offered. Lowest-price models generally do not allow for or encourage innovative practices. Instead, you should:

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
– consider the proposed methodology – is it based on safe work methods?

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
– consider if the proposal or tender addresses health risks as well as safety risks (not just temporary traffic management)

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
– check that the proposal or tender includes a detailed plan for how these risks will be managed (including for their subcontractors)

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
– check the proposal or tender is realistic about delivery timeframes. For example, if requiring a cap on the number of hours a worker can do in a week (to minimise the risks associated with worker fatigue), make sure delivery timeframes allow for this

[p34 | prose | 3.7 Good contracting principles and health and safety standards]
– question prices that seem too low – is the proposal or tender fit for purpose and will it keep workers and other persons healthy and safe, or has it been designed for approval?

[p43 | prose | 5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause]
5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause

[p43 | prose | 5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause]
Often there can be more than one thing (or factor) that could lead to a worker’s health being harmed. Examples of these factors include:

[p43 | prose | 5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause]
– Physical factors, such as task duration, task repetition, forces or loads, and awkward postures.

[p43 | prose | 5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause]
– Environmental factors, such as noise, temperature, and lighting

[p43 | prose | 5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause]
– Organisational factors, such as rosters and shifts, training, and worksite communication.

[p43 | prose | 5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause]
– Individual factors, such as body size, fitness, previous injures, and fatigue.

[p43 | prose | 5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause]
– Psychological factors, such as job demands, stress, workplace relationships, and available support.

[p43 | prose | 5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause]
These factors can be cumulative – each factor alone may not create a risk, but when combined these factors may cause harm.

[p43 | prose | 5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause]
Workers may be ‘managing their work okay’ until additional events push them towards discomfort, injury, or mental harm.

[p43 | prose | 5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause]
For example, consider a worker who works in a job that involves a repetitive task that also requires concentration. Usually, they can take regular breaks and swap tasks with a co-worker to avoid doing the same task for a long time (which could lead to musculoskeletal injuries). However:

[p43 | prose | 5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause]
– their co-worker calls in sick, so they cannot rotate tasks

[p43 | prose | 5.3 Work-related health problems often have more than one cause]

9.4 Diesel exhaust

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
Diesel engine exhaust is a complex mixture of gases and particles including carbon monoxide, nitrogen dioxide, sulphur dioxide, and diesel particulate matter (DPM). Diesel engine exhaust can cause cancer.

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
Road and roadside workers may be exposed to diesel exhaust from things like diesel-powered mobile plant and diesel-powered generators, and from diesel exhaust from passing vehicles.

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
Most outdoor work environments will have low concentrations of diesel exhaust because it can disperse easily, so the risk to workers will be low. However, some environmental factors can limit the dispersal of diesel exhaust and make its concentration higher. Examples of such factors include:

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
– tunnels and trenches (including cut and cover roads)

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
– city streets (where tall buildings can create a canyon effect which stops diesel exhaust from dispersing)

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
– highways with acoustic barriers in place (while blocking noise, these barriers can also block airborne pollution from dispersing)

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
– worksite sight screens (these can also block airborne pollution from dispersing).

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
Short-term acute symptoms of diesel exhaust exposure include:

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
– irritation of the eyes, nose, and throat

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
– headaches

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
– dizziness or light-headedness

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
– nausea

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
– coughing

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
– laboured breathing, or having difficulty breathing

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
– tightness of chest.

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
Exposure to excessive levels of carbon monoxide can lead to loss of consciousness and death.

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
Some workers may be more sensitive to diesel exhaust or air pollution, for example those with certain pre-existing conditions.

[p59 | prose | 9.4 Diesel exhaust]
Long-term exposures can lead to more serious, chronic health problems such as cardiovascular disease, cardiopulmonary disease, and cancer.

[p59 | prose | Control measures for diesel exhaust exposure]

[p59 | prose | Control measures for diesel exhaust exposure]

[p59 | prose | Control measures for diesel exhaust exposure]

[p59 | prose | Control measures for diesel exhaust exposure]
– using equipment with enclosed cabs with good ventilation to maintain air quality. For more information, see WorkSafe’s webpage: Maintaining air quality in enclosed cabins

[p59 | prose | Control measures for diesel exhaust exposure]

[p59 | prose | Control measures for diesel exhaust exposure]

[p59 | prose | Control measures for diesel exhaust exposure]

[p62 | prose | 10.1 Introduction to managing hazardous substance risks]

[p62 | prose | 10.1 Introduction to managing hazardous substance risks]

[p62 | prose | 10.1 Introduction to managing hazardous substance risks]

12.1 Introduction to managing extreme temperature risks

[p71 | prose | 12.1 Introduction to managing extreme temperature risks]
This section offers guidance for PCBUs on managing the risks to road and roadside workers of working in extreme temperatures.

[p71 | prose | 12.1 Introduction to managing extreme temperature risks]
The outdoor nature of most road and roadside work means road and roadside workers are particularly vulnerable to the effects of working in extreme temperatures.

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
12.2 Working in extreme heat

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
Working in extremely hot environments can put workers’ bodies under stress.

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
If their bodies have to work too hard to stay cool, it can cause heat-related illness and injuries. These can be fatal if ignored.

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
Heat-related illness and injuries are a risk, especially when working outdoors in summer or in high humidity, or when exposed to radiant heat.

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
High body temperatures that can cause harm to workers can occur:

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
– in the summer months

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
– in humid environments

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
– during highly physical activities (such as manual rubbish/recycling bin collection, manual excavation, asphalt laying, and roadside gardening)

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
– when warm or heavy clothing is being worn, including PPE

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
– when working near a source of radiant heat. For example:

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
- near machinery or generators

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
- during asphalt or emulsion application

[p71 | prose | 12.2 Working in extreme heat]
- standing on hot road surfaces.

[p71 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
Control measures for work in extreme heat

[p71 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
While it may not be possible to eliminate extreme heat in the outdoor environment, there are steps that can be taken to minimise the effects of working in extreme heat. For example, by:

[p71 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– replacing heat-producing plant with plant that produces less heat

[p71 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– insulating heat-producing plant or use heat screens to reduce radiant heat

[p71 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– using mechanical aids to reduce worker effort

[p71 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– making sure vehicle and mobile plant cabs have air conditioning

[p71 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– providing protection from the sun while working

[p71 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– scheduling work for cooler times of the day or year

[p72 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– frequently rotating workers on tasks

[p72 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– allowing extra rest breaks and providing climate-controlled rest facilities away from the heat and sun

[p72 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– educating workers on the importance of staying hydrated, making cool drinks available at all times, and encouraging them to drink water at the beginning and end of the day

[p72 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– encouraging self-paced work where possible

[p72 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– making sure workers are acclimatised to the conditions

[p72 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– avoiding putting workers with pre-existing medical conditions that may make them susceptible to heat stress in jobs where they will be exposed to extreme heat

[p72 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– making sure PPE is as light as possible and breathable (without compromising its protective function). For more information, see Section 27.5: Make sure PPE does not create new risks

[p72 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme heat]
– training workers on how to identify signs and symptoms of heat-related illness in themselves and their workmates. For more information, see WorkSafe’s guidance: Working safely in extreme temperatures

Provide PPE for working in extreme heat

[p72 | prose | Provide PPE for working in extreme heat]
– face shields

– sunhats

[p72 | prose | Provide PPE for working in extreme heat]
– heat-resistant footwear.

[p72 | prose | Provide PPE for working in extreme heat]
Sweating is the body’s main way of keeping cool. Make sure that, where possible, PPE and uniforms are made from light, breathable materials so workers can sweat freely.

[p72 | prose | 12.3 Working in extreme cold]

[p72 | prose | 12.3 Working in extreme cold]

[p72 | prose | 12.3 Working in extreme cold]

[p72 | prose | 12.3 Working in extreme cold]

[p72 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme cold]

[p73 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme cold]

[p73 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme cold]
– providing specialised protective clothing that protects from cold, wind, and water

[p73 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme cold]
– providing drying facilities so wet or damp clothing and PPE can be dried during breaks and between shifts

[p73 | prose | Control measures for work in extreme cold]

[p73 | prose | Provide PPE for working in extreme cold]

[p73 | prose | Provide PPE for working in extreme cold]

UV radiation is a type of radiation that is given off by the sun and some artificial sources (such as UVB work lights). This radiation can damage the genetic material (DNA) of skin cells, causing harm to a person.

[p75 | prose | 13.1 Introduction to managing UV/sun exposure risks]
UV radiation cannot be seen or felt, so workers may be exposed to harmful UV radiation without knowing.

[p75 | prose | 13.1 Introduction to managing UV/sun exposure risks]
UV radiation can harm a worker in several ways, including:

[p75 | prose | 13.1 Introduction to managing UV/sun exposure risks]
– skin cancer

[p75 | prose | 13.1 Introduction to managing UV/sun exposure risks]
– sunburn

[p75 | prose | 13.1 Introduction to managing UV/sun exposure risks]
– eye damage (such as cataracts).

[p75 | prose | 13.1 Introduction to managing UV/sun exposure risks]
The total amount of UV radiation that a worker may be exposed to when working outside depends on the factors described below.

[p75 | table | 13.1 Introduction to managing UV/sun exposure risks]
| FACTOR AFFECTING UV EXPOSURE | THINGS TO CONSIDER |
| --- | --- |
| The time of day and time of year | UV levels are highest when the sun is high (around midday) and during summertime (when the sun is higher in the sky for longer). |
| The weather conditions | UV levels are generally higher on a cloudless day. However, UV radiation can pass through cloud cover and reach harmful levels even on a cloudy day. |
| Work surfaces | Workers can be exposed to UV radiation as it reflects off surfaces such as roads and footpaths. The lighter the colour of the surface, the more UV radiation will be reflected. |
| Medical and chemical exposure | Certain medication and chemical exposures can also increase the chances of UV damage because they cause photosensitivity (which makes a person more sensitive to UV radiation). |

All skin types can be damaged by UV rays.

[p79 | prose | 14.1 Introduction to managing impairment risks]
14.1 Introduction to managing impairment risks

[p79 | prose | 14.1 Introduction to managing impairment risks]
This section offers guidance for PCBUs on managing the risks or impairment for road and roadside workers.

[p79 | prose | 14.1 Introduction to managing impairment risks]
A worker’s fitness to work can be impaired by things like fatigue, medication, drugs, and alcohol. This can create a risk to their health and safety and the safety of those around them.

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
14.2 Fatigue

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
Road and roadside work relies on workers being physically and mentally alert.

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
Fatigue is a physiological state where someone is not able to mentally and physically function as they usually would. There are four main factors that can contribute to someone developing fatigue:

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
– missing out on sleep

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
– being awake for too long

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
– working and sleeping in the wrong parts of the body clock cycle

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
– having a high workload (mental and/or physical).

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
Fatigue is a major risk for road and roadside workers. Examples of conditions that can lead to road and roadside workers experiencing fatigue include:

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
– the increase in 24-hour operations

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
– longer shifts

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
– an increase in overtime hours

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
– on-call or night duties

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
– long driving times (some workers have to travel before and after their shifts – particularly, to reach remote worksites or emergency response sites)

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
– personal health or lifestyle factors.

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
As the level of fatigue experienced by a worker increases, there is a higher likelihood that the worker will experience impairment. This can affect their decision- making abilities, increasing the likelihood of negative safety or health outcomes.

[p79 | prose | 14.2 Fatigue]
Fatigue could also cause micro-sleeps, where the person briefly falls asleep. Micro-sleeps are potentially deadly if they occur at the wrong time – for example, when the person is in control of a vehicle or mobile plant.

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
14.3 Control measures for fatigue

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
There are several control measures that can be used to reduce the chances of workers experiencing fatigue, for example:

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– creating work schedules that allow for sufficient rest during work shifts and between shifts (create these in consultation with your workers)

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– making sure working hours are not too long. If longer working days are required, consult with your workers before deciding these hours. Consider staggered start and finish times, longer rest breaks, and periods off work

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– scheduling tasks suitably throughout a work period. A person’s ability to remain alert and focused can be reduced between 3.00am and 5.00am, and between 3.00pm and 5.00pm. If reasonably practicable, avoid critical jobs, such working at height, manual tasks, or tasks using mobile plant or vehicles, during these times

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– monitor and place limits around the number of overtime hours that can be worked. Avoid incentives to work excessive hours

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– monitoring and placing limits around shift swapping and on-call duties

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– designing rosters that follow natural sleeping rhythms and allow for good sleep opportunity and recovery time

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– avoiding work that starts before 6.00am, where possible. If night work is required, limit the number of night shifts in a row that your workers can work

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– factoring in travel times to worksites. Long travel times to remote worksites will contribute to fatigue, as will the effects of staying away from home for days at a time

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– checking that casual staff or labour hire workers have not recently worked a shift somewhere else (for example, the same day or the previous night). If they had, they might already be tired.

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
You can also manage the effects of fatigue by managing physical and mental work demands. For example:

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– limiting periods of excessive mental or physical demands by often rotating those tasks between workers

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– making sure plant, machinery, and equipment are fit for purpose

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– making sure workloads are manageable. Take into account workflow changes due to factors such as machinery breakdowns, unplanned absences, or resignations

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
– avoiding unreasonable deadlines.

[p80 | prose | 14.3 Control measures for fatigue]
Consider seeking expert advice from a suitably qualified health and safety professional that specialises in fatigue management. They can assess your workers, schedules, systems, and practices, and provide advice on fatigue risk management.

Worker responsibilities in managing fatigue

[p81 | prose | Worker responsibilities in managing fatigue]

14.4 Impairment from drugs and alcohol

[p81 | prose | 14.4 Impairment from drugs and alcohol]
Medication, drugs, and alcohol use can result in impaired performance at work. This can make a worker a risk to themselves and others around them.

[p81 | prose | 14.4 Impairment from drugs and alcohol]
If workers are impaired, or may become impaired, by prescription medication, they should inform their site manager. Workers may perform work while taking prescription medication if a medical practitioner has cleared them to do so.

[p81 | prose | 14.4 Impairment from drugs and alcohol]
If a worker is impaired or suspected to be impaired by alcohol or illegal drugs, they should not be allowed to work.

[p81 | prose | 14.4 Impairment from drugs and alcohol]
Workers should be educated on the risks of working while impaired. These risks are especially high when working on or near vehicles and mobile plant, and when controlling traffic.

[p82 | prose | Drugs and alcohol policy]

[p82 | prose | Drugs and alcohol policy]

[p82 | prose | Drugs and alcohol policy]

[p82 | prose | Drugs and alcohol policy]
– post-incident testing

[p82 | prose | Drugs and alcohol policy]
– reasonable cause testing.

You should clearly identify safety-sensitive roles where drug testing may be applicable for managing risk.

[p82 | prose | Drugs and alcohol policy]

[p82 | prose | Drugs and alcohol policy]

[p82 | prose | Drugs and alcohol policy]

[p86 | prose | 15.4 Support for workers who are exposed to vehicle crashes or traumatic situations]

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
Often a good way to eliminate or minimise the risks of working near live traffic is to close the road while the work is being done. This should be the preferred option where it is reasonably practicable to do so.

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
Typically, the decision to close a road will need to be agreed upon by all parties – specifically the RCA and the contractor. When deciding if closing the road is a reasonably practical step to take, consider what alternative options may be available ensure safety and minimise disruption to road users. For example, consider:

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
– if there are suitable alternative routes that road users can take to safely bypass the road closure (this includes vulnerable road users such as pedestrians and cyclists)

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
– if it would be possible to publicise the road closure ahead of time, allowing road users to plan ahead, or consider alternative routes or travel times

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
– if the work can be completed at a time when the road is less busy, therefore requiring less disruption to road users – for example, close the road at night to complete the works (assuming the works can be done safely in night-time conditions)

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
– if the work can be done over several days with intermittent road closures or one-way detours.

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
When considering the available options for closing the road, you also need to assess what, if any, new risks may be created, increased, or transferred by doing so. For example, consider:

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
– if the road closure would result in new or increased risk to road users

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
– if the road closure would result in new or increased risk to workers while setting up and maintaining the closure

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
– if the road closure would result in new or increased risk to vulnerable road users such as pedestrians or cyclists.

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
You will need to assess whether any new risks outweigh the benefits of closing the road. In addition to consulting with workers and other affected PCBUs in the contracting chain, you may also need to consult with affected parties such as road user organisations or vulnerable road user groups.

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
Often, closing the road can be more cost effective. Closing the road usually means work can be completed with less interruption. Not only may it be safer for workers, but work may be completed faster and with less cost.

[p104 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
See the Supporting Material section of the NZGTTM for road closure examples and case studies showing how the risks to workers were balanced against the risks to road users.

[p105 | prose | 19.4 Eliminate or minimise the risks from working near live traffic – close the road]
The convenience of road users should not be prioritised over the safety of road or roadside workers.

19.5 Further ways to minimise the risks from working near live traffic

[p105 | prose | 19.5 Further ways to minimise the risks from working near live traffic]
If it is not reasonably practicable to close the road entirely while work is being done, you will need to plan how you will minimise the risk to workers and others and allow for road users to safely pass the worksite.

[p105 | prose | Short duration activities]
Short duration activities

[p105 | prose | Short duration activities]
All reasonably practicable risk management options should be considered no matter how long the work is expected to take. This will also include weighing up the risks of implementing certain control measures against the risks of the activity itself.

[p105 | prose | Short duration activities]
Where possible, convert short duration foot activities to vehicle-based activities (for example, use a truck-mounted object retrieval system to collect debris off a highway or motorway).

[p105 | prose | Short duration activities]
The length of time a worker will be on the road or roadside completing a task should not alone determine if or what control measures are used.

[p105 | prose | Short duration activities]
Isolate workers from road users

[p105 | prose | Short duration activities]
Where reasonably practicable, you should try to physically isolate the work activity and workers from road users. Examples of how this can be done include:

[p105 | prose | Short duration activities]
– using temporary road safety barriers

[p105 | prose | Short duration activities]
– using truck-mounted attenuators (especially for mobile works)

[p105 | prose | Short duration activities]
– installing safety fences (this would be appropriate when isolating workers from pedestrians and non-motorised road users).

[p105 | prose | Short duration activities]
For breakdown assist situations, both vehicles should be positioned as far off to the side of the road as possible. Tools and equipment should be accessible from the side of the response vehicle facing away from moving traffic.

[p105 | prose | Short duration activities]
For kerbside rubbish and recycling collection, workers should only operate from the side of the vehicle facing away from moving traffic.

[p105 | prose | Control the movements of road users]
Control the movements of road users

[p105 | prose | Control the movements of road users]
You could minimise the risks presented by road users by creating a TTM zone that lets road users pass the worksite in a controlled way. Examples of how this can be done include:

[p105 | prose | Control the movements of road users]
– lowering the speed limit

[p105 | prose | Control the movements of road users]
– using piloted convoys

[p105 | prose | Control the movements of road users]
– using automated stop/go systems to allow single direction traffic though the TTM zone at a time

[p105 | prose | Control the movements of road users]
– setting out temporary lanes in a way that causes road users to slow down

[p105 | prose | Control the movements of road users]
– installing temporary speed feedback signs

[p105 | prose | Control the movements of road users]
– diverting footpaths or cycleways away from the worksite.

Use signage and other administrative control measures

[p106 | prose | Use signage and other administrative control measures]
You should also consider what administrative control measures you can put in place. Examples include:

[p106 | prose | Use signage and other administrative control measures]
– setting up exclusion zones to separate workers within the worksite from road users travelling through the TTM zone

[p106 | prose | Use signage and other administrative control measures]
– putting up warning signs for road users (make sure these are not blocking footpaths or cycleways)

[p106 | prose | Use signage and other administrative control measures]
– requesting police support for speed enforcement

[p106 | prose | Use signage and other administrative control measures]

[p106 | prose | Use signage and other administrative control measures]

[p106 | prose | 19.6 Traffic management plans (TMPs)]
19.6 Traffic management plans (TMPs)

[p106 | prose | 19.6 Traffic management plans (TMPs)]
This section provides a general overview of TMPs. You should also refer to guidance provided by the RCA for more detailed information on TMPs.

[p106 | prose | Documenting a TMP]
Documenting a TMP

[p106 | prose | Documenting a TMP]
A TMP is a document that describes the design, implementation, maintenance, and removal of traffic management while work is being done on or near a road. A TMP will also typically document the roles and responsibilities of all involved.

[p106 | prose | Documenting a TMP]
TMPs should be created by a suitably qualified temporary traffic management planner (TTMP).

[p106 | prose | Documenting a TMP]
When creating these plans the TTMP should:

[p106 | prose | Documenting a TMP]
– follow the findings and recommendations of the risk assessment that has already been done in accordance with HSWA (as outlined in these guidelines) and any additional guidance provided by the RCA

[p106 | prose | Documenting a TMP]
– make sure all equipment to be used is consistent with the specifications listed in the NZGTTM

[p106 | prose | Documenting a TMP]
– where reasonably practicable, consult with all PCBUs and their workers that will be implementing the TMP and/or working within the TTM zone while the TMP is in place

[p106 | prose | Documenting a TMP]
– where reasonably practicable, consult will all road user stakeholder groups that will be affected by the TMP

[p106 | prose | Documenting a TMP]
– consider their upstream duties as a designer. For more information on upstream duties, see Appendix 5: Upstream duties

[p106 | prose | Documenting a TMP]
Refer to any additional guidance provided by the RCA for a more detailed list of what should be included in TMP documentation.

[p106 | prose | Checking a TMP (TMP peer review)]
Checking a TMP (TMP peer review)

[p106 | prose | Checking a TMP (TMP peer review)]
Before being implemented, TMPs should be checked to make sure that:

[p106 | prose | Checking a TMP (TMP peer review)]
– the plan does not clash with other planned work

[p106 | prose | Checking a TMP (TMP peer review)]
– the plan does not cause unintended negative impacts

[p106 | prose | Checking a TMP (TMP peer review)]
– the plan has correctly identified and assessed risk, and determined the most appropriate control measures – ensuring the lowest total risk to all affected people.

[p106 | prose | Checking a TMP (TMP peer review)]
Refer to any additional guidance provided by the RCA on the steps involved in TMP peer review and who should do the peer reviews.

[p107 | prose | Implementing a TMP]
Implementing a TMP

[p107 | prose | Implementing a TMP]
Implementing a TMP on site is usually led by a suitably qualified site traffic management specialist (STMS) and supported by suitably qualified traffic controllers (TCs).

[p107 | prose | Implementing a TMP]
Where there is a separate traffic management provider (contractor or subcontractor) providing traffic management for a worksite – all PCBUs on site should work together and follow the advice of the STMS in relation to TTM matters.

[p107 | prose | Implementing a TMP]
Work should not begin at the worksite until the STMS has confirmed that the TTM zone is ready, and the workers have been briefed.

[p107 | prose | Implementing a TMP]
No one should interfere with or make changes to the TTM zone set-up without having the approval of the STMS first. Workers should notify the STMS if they see any issues with the set-up (see Monitoring/reviewing a TMP below).

[p107 | prose | Monitoring and reviewing a TMP]
Monitoring and reviewing a TMP

[p107 | prose | Monitoring and reviewing a TMP]
An important step in effective risk management is monitoring and reviewing control measures. For TMPs, this may involve checking the following:

[p107 | prose | Monitoring and reviewing a TMP]
– Is the TMP is still relevant for the conditions. For example:

[p107 | prose | Monitoring and reviewing a TMP]
- are the traffic volumes as planned for?

[p107 | prose | Monitoring and reviewing a TMP]
is the weather as planned for?

[p107 | prose | Monitoring and reviewing a TMP]
is the work activity as planned for?

[p107 | prose | Monitoring and reviewing a TMP]
- have any other conditions changed that mean the TMP is no longer appropriate?

[p107 | prose | Monitoring and reviewing a TMP]
– Are there any new risks that were not planned for?

[p107 | prose | Monitoring and reviewing a TMP]
If new risks have emerged that were not planned for, the TMP should be updated as soon as reasonably practicable to account for the new risks, see Changing a TMP below.

[p107 | prose | Monitoring and reviewing a TMP]
– Assuming the TMP is still relevant for the conditions, has the site been delivered or set up correctly according to the TMP?

[p107 | prose | Changing a TMP]
Changing a TMP

[p107 | prose | Changing a TMP]
Sometimes a TMP will need to be changed to accommodate changing conditions, unanticipated risks, or because of a formal review. Changes need to be documented (including the reasons for the change). Where applicable, changes should be communicated to:

[p107 | prose | Changing a TMP]
– the TTMP

[p107 | prose | Changing a TMP]
– the STMS or TC working to the plan

[p107 | prose | Changing a TMP]
– the RCA

[p107 | prose | Changing a TMP]
– other PCBUs in the contracting chain involved with the work (using already established communication channels).

[p107 | prose | Changing a TMP]
Refer to any additional guidance provided by the RCA for more detailed information on changing TMPs.

[p107 | prose | Changing a TMP]
Note: There may be other parties that need to be contacted if changes to TMPs are required. Examples include rail corridor operators, public transport system operators, or airport operators.

20.1 Introduction to managing the risks of working near mobile plant

[p110 | prose | 20.1 Introduction to managing the risks of working near mobile plant]

[p110 | prose | 20.2 What could go wrong when working near mobile plant?]
20.2 What could go wrong when working near mobile plant?

[p110 | prose | 20.2 What could go wrong when working near mobile plant?]
Mobile plant used in road and roadside work can cause serious injury or death by rolling over, or by striking or colliding with workers, vehicles, or plant. Common hazards include:

[p110 | prose | 20.2 What could go wrong when working near mobile plant?]

[p110 | prose | 20.2 What could go wrong when working near mobile plant?]

– multiple vehicles or mobile plant operating at the same time in a confined area

– mobile plant operating on uneven surfaces or potentially unstable ground

[p110 | prose | 20.2 What could go wrong when working near mobile plant?]
– mobile plant malfunctioning or breaking down

[p110 | prose | 20.2 What could go wrong when working near mobile plant?]
– people using mobile plant in an unsafe manner (such as exceeding safe speeds or exceeding load limits).

[p110 | prose | 20.2 What could go wrong when working near mobile plant?]

[p111 | prose | 20.3 Minimise the risks from working near mobile plant]
20.3 Minimise the risks from working near mobile plant

[p111 | prose | 20.3 Minimise the risks from working near mobile plant]
Keep people and mobile plant separated

[p111 | prose | 20.3 Minimise the risks from working near mobile plant]
Wherever mobile plant is operating, there is a risk to workers or others who are nearby.

[p111 | prose | 20.3 Minimise the risks from working near mobile plant]
As it is not usually practicable to eliminate the use of mobile plant, the preferred control measure is to isolate workers or others from mobile plant while it is in operation.

[p111 | prose | 20.3 Minimise the risks from working near mobile plant]
Examples of how this could be done include:

[p111 | prose | 20.3 Minimise the risks from working near mobile plant]
– creating an exclusion zone around mobile plant while in operation, so that only those directly involved with the operation of the mobile plant are permitted within the exclusion zone

[p111 | prose | 20.3 Minimise the risks from working near mobile plant]
– using temporary pedestrian barriers or fencing, supported by signage, to indicate exclusion zones

[p111 | prose | 20.3 Minimise the risks from working near mobile plant]
– making sure vulnerable road users such as pedestrians and cyclists have a safe way to get past the worksite, in particular past site entry and exit points.

[p111 | prose | 20.3 Minimise the risks from working near mobile plant]
Where relevant, keep worker and visitor parking clearly separated from the work area. Avoid having small vehicles parked alongside, in front, or behind larger vehicles.

– setting worksite-specific speed limits for mobile plant operating within the worksite

[p111 | prose | Control the speed of mobile plant on site]

[p112 | prose | Use the right mobile plant for the job]
Use the right mobile plant for the job

[p112 | prose | Use the right mobile plant for the job]
Mobile plant used at worksites should be suitable for the purpose for which they are used. Using mobile plant for a task that it is not suited for can create risks for the driver and other people at the worksite.

[p112 | prose | Use the right mobile plant for the job]
When choosing the right mobile plant for a job, consider:

[p112 | prose | Use the right mobile plant for the job]
– what the mobile plant will be doing

[p112 | prose | Use the right mobile plant for the job]
– if the mobile plant is designed for the purpose you want to use it for

[p112 | prose | Use the right mobile plant for the job]
– if the mobile plant can handle the required weight and type of load that you want it to carry

[p112 | prose | Use the right mobile plant for the job]
– if the mobile plant is suitable for the surface it will be operating on

[p112 | prose | Use the right mobile plant for the job]
– if the mobile plant could be at risk from rolling over

[p112 | prose | Use the right mobile plant for the job]
– if the worker operating the mobile plant is trained and competent enough to use it safely for that task

[p112 | prose | Use the right mobile plant for the job]
– if the mobile plant is currently certified and warranted. All mobile plant must meet relevant standards and have required registrations.

[p112 | prose | Use the right mobile plant for the job]
If you hire mobile plant, the hire company must provide the operating manual and instructions on how to use the mobile plant correctly and safely. This includes any weight information or noise ratings (so you can make sure workers have the correct PPE for working with that mobile plant).

[p113 | prose | Collision avoidance technology and proximity warning devices]
Collision avoidance technology and proximity warning devices

[p113 | prose | Collision avoidance technology and proximity warning devices]
Technological safety control measures can also contribute to making a worksite safer for workers, road users, and others passing near worksites. For example:

[p113 | prose | Collision avoidance technology and proximity warning devices]
– mobile plant proximity warning devices

[p113 | prose | Collision avoidance technology and proximity warning devices]
– personal proximity warning devices (these can be particularly useful if workers have to wear hearing protection that affects their ability to hear nearby mobile plant)

[p113 | prose | Collision avoidance technology and proximity warning devices]

[p113 | prose | Collision avoidance technology and proximity warning devices]

[p113 | prose | Collision avoidance technology and proximity warning devices]

[p114 | prose | Seatbelts]

[p114 | prose | Keep mobile plant well maintained]
Keep mobile plant well maintained

[p114 | prose | Keep mobile plant well maintained]
All mobile plant should be kept in good working order. Mobile plant should be maintained according to manufacturer recommendations and time or mileage guidelines. Mobile plant used in extreme conditions may need to be checked more often.

[p114 | prose | Keep mobile plant well maintained]
Operators should visually check their mobile plant at the beginning of every shift before using it. Provide operators with a checklist to guide them on what to look for. When using a checklist, you should:

[p114 | prose | Keep mobile plant well maintained]
– make sure operators have enough time to complete their mobile plant check

[p114 | prose | Keep mobile plant well maintained]
– keep the checklist as simple as possible

[p114 | prose | Keep mobile plant well maintained]
– include a system for reporting problems and making sure they are dealt with and closed off.

[p114 | prose | Keep mobile plant well maintained]
- Make sure mobile plant that fails a safety critical check is taken out of service and not allowed to be used until the problem is fixed (see Figure 21). For more information, see WorkSafe’s webpage: Keeping workers safe with machine lockouts

[p114 | prose | Keep mobile plant well maintained]
– include a section for workers to add comments or identify any other issues.

[p114 | prose | Keep mobile plant well maintained]
It is ultimately the responsibility of the PCBU in control of the plant, not the worker, to make sure mobile plant is in good working order. You should monitor mobile plant checklists to make sure the checks are carried out properly and that identified issues are dealt with.

[p114 | prose | Keep mobile plant well maintained]
Poorly maintained mobile plant can also create health risks for operators, such as whole-body vibration from poor suspension or fumes exposure from clogged up vents.

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
Safe operators

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
Worksite mobile plant should only be operated by workers who are competent in the safe use of that mobile plant.

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
Workers should have the relevant skills, experience, and certifications for the specific mobile plant and worksite conditions they are operating in.

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
A competent operator is someone that:

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
– has had training and supervision on the operation of the mobile plant. This includes:

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
the practical mechanics of operating the mobile plant

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
- how to operate it in a safe way based on the environmental conditions.

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
– has the required licences and certifications to safely operate the mobile plant. For more information, see Section 28.0: Training, certifications, and competency

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
– has the level of fitness and general abilities required to operate the mobile plant

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
– is well enough on the day to operate the mobile plant safely, for example:

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
- not suffering from fatigue. For more information, see Section: 14.2 Fatigue

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
- not under the influence of medication or another substance that could impair their ability. For more information, see Section 14.4 Impairment from drugs and alcohol

[p115 | prose | Safe operators]
It is particularly important that new or less experienced drivers are closely monitored following their training to make sure they are operating the mobile plant safely.

Get condition reports

[p132 | prose | Get condition reports]

Make sure workers have the right equipment for the conditions

[p132 | prose | Make sure workers have the right equipment for the conditions]

[p132 | prose | Make sure workers have the right equipment for the conditions]

[p132 | prose | Have a check-in system]

[p133 | prose | 24.5 Working in high winds]

[p133 | prose | 24.5 Working in high winds]

[p133 | prose | 24.5 Working in high winds]
– injuries from dust or debris in the air

[p133 | prose | 24.5 Working in high winds]

[p133 | prose | 24.5 Working in high winds]

[p133 | prose | 24.5 Working in high winds]

[p134 | prose | Control measures for working in high wind conditions]

[p134 | prose | Control measures for working in high wind conditions]

[p134 | prose | Control measures for working in high wind conditions]

[p134 | prose | Control measures for working in high wind conditions]

[p134 | prose | Control measures for working in high wind conditions]

[p134 | prose | Control measures for working in high wind conditions]

[p134 | prose | Control measures for working in high wind conditions]
– Securing latches, doors, windows, scaffolding, and ladders.

[p134 | prose | Control measures for working in high wind conditions]
– Avoiding carrying or lifting large objects. If it cannot be avoided, make sure workers have additional support or assistance. Large objects (such as TTM signs) can act as a sail in high winds.

[p134 | prose | Control measures for working in high wind conditions]
– Using buildings or vehicles to help block the wind.

[p134 | prose | Control measures for working in high wind conditions]
– Wearing safety glasses or goggles when appropriate.

This includes temporary or mobile workplaces, which are typical of road and roadside work. Facilities that must be made accessible to workers, so far as is reasonably practicable, include:

[p148 | prose | 27.1 Introduction to personal protective equipment (PPE)]
27.1 Introduction to personal protective equipment (PPE)

[p148 | prose | 27.1 Introduction to personal protective equipment (PPE)]
This section offers guidance for PCBUs on the requirements for providing PPE for road and roadside workers. PPE can be used if there is still risk remaining after all other reasonably practicable control measures have been put in place. For road and roadside work, standard PPE requirements may include:

[p148 | table | 27.1 Introduction to personal protective equipment (PPE)]
| hard hat or helmet | protective or steel toed boots |
| --- | --- |
| eye protection | hi-visibility clothing |
| protective clothing (such as wet weather clothing) | hearing protection |
| gloves | sunscreen |

[p148 | prose | 27.1 Introduction to personal protective equipment (PPE)]
Additional PPE (depending on the risks present) include:

[p148 | prose | 27.1 Introduction to personal protective equipment (PPE)]
PCBUs should engage with workers when deciding what is the most appropriate PPE for their task and working environment.

[p149 | prose | 27.2 Who can provide PPE]
27.2 Who can provide PPE

[p149 | prose | 27.2 Who can provide PPE]
A PCBU who directs the carrying out of work at a workplace must provide PPE to workers carrying out the work unless PPE has been provided by another PCBU in the contracting chain.

[p149 | prose | 27.2 Who can provide PPE]
The worker can also choose to provide their own PPE but only if they genuinely volunteer to do so for their comfort or convenience. If the worker chooses to provide their own PPE, the PCBU must still make sure the PPE will provide appropriate protection for the worker.

[p149 | prose | 27.2 Who can provide PPE]
PCBUs cannot pass on the cost of providing PPE (in full or part) to their workers.

[p149 | prose | 27.3 Providing replacement PPE]
27.3 Providing replacement PPE

[p149 | prose | 27.3 Providing replacement PPE]
PCBUs must provide workers with replacement PPE free-of-charge as and when needed.

[p149 | prose | 27.3 Providing replacement PPE]
PPE must be clean, hygienic, and in good working order. PCBUs should make sure that PPE is maintained, repaired, or replaced so that it continues to minimise risk to the worker who uses it. It should be replaced whenever it becomes worn out, is no longer providing adequate protection, or is past its use-by date.

[p149 | prose | 27.3 Providing replacement PPE]
Replacing PPE should be assessed based on need – annual replacement of PPE may not be sufficient.

[p149 | prose | 27.3 Providing replacement PPE]
Adding PPE allowances to workers’ pay to cover future PPE expenses is not recommended. It is not reasonable to expect a worker to keep money aside from each pay period for future PPE purchases. There is a risk workers may wear PPE past its use-by date, especially if they have not accrued enough allowance to cover an expensive item.

[p149 | prose | 27.4 PPE must be fit for purpose]
27.4 PPE must be fit for purpose

[p149 | prose | 27.4 PPE must be fit for purpose]
Any PPE, including high-visibility clothing, must meet basic PPE requirements for fit, function and performance, and be reasonably comfortable to wear.

[p149 | prose | 27.4 PPE must be fit for purpose]
Workers should receive training in how to wear, use, clean, and store their PPE correctly. For more information, see WorkSafe’s webpage: Personal protective equipment – a guide for businesses

[p149 | prose | 27.5 Make sure PPE does not create new risks]
27.5 Make sure PPE does not create new risks

[p149 | prose | 27.5 Make sure PPE does not create new risks]
When assessing PPE needs, discuss with your workers what new risks the proposed PPE may create and how you can eliminate or minimise those risks. The following are examples of risks associated with different types of PPE:

[p149 | prose | Hearing protection]
Hearing protection

[p149 | prose | Hearing protection]
Some types of hearing protection can affect workers’ situational awareness (for example, preventing them from hearing approaching vehicles).

[p149 | prose | Hearing protection]
There are types of hearing protection that can protect workers hearing without compromising situational awareness.

[p149 | prose | Heavy or thick clothing]
Heavy or thick clothing

[p149 | prose | Heavy or thick clothing]
Heavy or thick clothing may cause workers to overheat, affect their mobility, or create entanglement risks.

[p149 | prose | Heavy or thick clothing]
Talk with your workers about alternative clothing options (such as lighter more breathable fabrics or closer fitting options).

[p150 | prose | Long sleeves and trousers]
Long sleeves and trousers

[p150 | prose | Long sleeves and trousers]
Long sleeves and trousers can provide good protection from a number of hazards, including:

[p150 | prose | Long sleeves and trousers]
– bitumen spray

[p150 | prose | Long sleeves and trousers]
– dust

[p150 | prose | Long sleeves and trousers]
– small flying objects such as gravel chips

[p150 | prose | Long sleeves and trousers]
– sharp objects

[p150 | prose | Long sleeves and trousers]
– chemicals

[p150 | prose | Long sleeves and trousers]
– electricity.

[p150 | prose | Long sleeves and trousers]
However, having blanket site rules requiring long sleeves and trousers may lead to unnecessary discomfort for workers (especially during summer months).

[p150 | prose | Long sleeves and trousers]
Consider if short sleeves and shorts can be worn safely (with the use of sunscreen when needed), and only require long sleeves and trousers for tasks where they are providing specific protection.

[p150 | prose | Long sleeves and trousers]
You could also consider if alternative protection (for example, chaps and gaiters) may be appropriate.

27.6 Worker duties and PPE

[p150 | prose | 27.6 Worker duties and PPE]

– how it should be cared for and stored.

[p150 | prose | 27.6 Worker duties and PPE]
Workers should tell their manager as soon as there are any issues with the PPE (such as when it is no longer fitting properly or has broken).

[p152 | prose | 28.1 Introduction to training, certifications, and competency]
28.1 Introduction to training, certifications, and competency

[p152 | prose | 28.1 Introduction to training, certifications, and competency]
This section offers guidance for PCBUs on making sure their workers have the right training, certifications, and competency.

[p152 | prose | 28.1 Introduction to training, certifications, and competency]
All road and roadside workers should have the appropriate certifications, licences, and training in how to perform their job or task in a manner that is safe and healthy for them and others.

[p152 | prose | 28.1 Introduction to training, certifications, and competency]
All road and roadside workers should also receive training in how to keep safe when working in the road or roadside environment.

[p152 | prose | 28.1 Introduction to training, certifications, and competency]
Worker training requirements will depend on:

[p152 | prose | 28.1 Introduction to training, certifications, and competency]
– the level of risk their job involves

[p152 | prose | 28.1 Introduction to training, certifications, and competency]
– what industry or occupation-specific training requirements and certifications (including licences) are required

[p152 | prose | 28.1 Introduction to training, certifications, and competency]
– the knowledge, experience, and previous training of the worker.

[p152 | prose | 28.2 Check workers have required certifications, licences, and training]
28.2 Check workers have required certifications, licences, and training

[p152 | prose | 28.2 Check workers have required certifications, licences, and training]
PCBUs at the top of the contracting chain have a responsibility to make sure all workers have the appropriate training and certifications for the work they will be doing. This includes workers that may be several steps down the contracting chain.

[p152 | prose | 28.2 Check workers have required certifications, licences, and training]
Where reasonably practicable, arrangements should be made to specify and check these training, qualifications, and licensing/certification requirements at the planning stages of a project.

[p152 | prose | 28.2 Check workers have required certifications, licences, and training]
PCBUs should make sure workers meet industry training and certification requirements before they start work. They should check:

[p152 | prose | 28.2 Check workers have required certifications, licences, and training]
– workers have the relevant temporary traffic management (TTM) qualifications before working as a traffic controller (TC)

[p152 | prose | 28.2 Check workers have required certifications, licences, and training]
– workers have the appropriate licences and training to operate specific types of mobile plant (such as for tracks and rollers)

[p152 | prose | 28.2 Check workers have required certifications, licences, and training]
– any industry-required health and safety, and site access training.

[p152 | prose | 28.2 Check workers have required certifications, licences, and training]
Only competent persons who have the relevant certifications for training others should provide training.

[p153 | prose | 28.3 Check worker competency before work begins]
28.3 Check worker competency before work begins

[p153 | prose | 28.3 Check worker competency before work begins]
Before starting work, all workers, including temporary workers, should be assessed on their current competence and experience, and what further training they may require. This is usually done by the site manager or someone the PCBU deems competent to make that assessment.

[p153 | prose | 28.3 Check worker competency before work begins]
A competent person is someone who has the appropriate skills, training, knowledge, and experience to perform the task or role.

[p153 | prose | 28.3 Check worker competency before work begins]
Having a current licence or certification is not always an indication of a worker’s actual level of competence when on the job. In some instances, workers may need further onsite training or retraining before starting work.

[p153 | prose | 28.4 Supervise new or inexperienced workers]
28.4 Supervise new or inexperienced workers

[p153 | prose | 28.4 Supervise new or inexperienced workers]
Supervision may be needed for new or inexperienced workers until they have shown they are competent to carry out the tasks unsupervised.

[p153 | prose | 28.4 Supervise new or inexperienced workers]
Experienced workers may also need to be supervised for new tasks or if they have not done that particular task in a long time.

[p153 | prose | 28.5 Ongoing training]
28.5 Ongoing training

[p153 | prose | 28.5 Ongoing training]
Workers should receive ongoing training, which may include:

[p153 | prose | 28.5 Ongoing training]
– refresher training when needed

[p153 | prose | 28.5 Ongoing training]
– training when new vehicles or plant, or new features are introduced

[p153 | prose | 28.5 Ongoing training]
– training when the work changes and the task needs to be done differently

[p153 | prose | 28.5 Ongoing training]
– when workers are due for recertification

[p153 | prose | 28.5 Ongoing training]
– when competence or qualification requirements change

[p153 | prose | 28.5 Ongoing training]
– continuing professional development.

[p153 | prose | 28.5 Ongoing training]
Training needs should be monitored.

[p153 | prose | 28.6 Keep a record of all training and certifications]
28.6 Keep a record of all training and certifications

[p153 | prose | 28.6 Keep a record of all training and certifications]
Keep a record of all training that has been completed, and certifications and licences that have been issued for each worker.

[p153 | prose | 28.6 Keep a record of all training and certifications]
Records should also include when refresher training or recertification is due. This will help make sure the most appropriate person is allocated a particular task and identify workers who need refresher training.

[p153 | prose | 28.6 Keep a record of all training and certifications]
This information should be made available to others in the contracting chain that have a duty towards those workers (provided the worker has consented to this information being shared).

[p153 | prose | 28.6 Keep a record of all training and certifications]
All storing of worker training information and sharing of that information is subject to The Privacy Act 2020

[p155 | prose | 29.1 Introduction to inductions]
29.1 Introduction to inductions

[p155 | prose | 29.1 Introduction to inductions]
This section offers guidance for PCBUs on how to use worker inductions to promote good health and safety practices at road and roadside worksites.

[p155 | prose | 29.1 Introduction to inductions]
Inductions are a useful way to introduce workers and visitors to key information and resources they need to keep themselves and other workers safe and healthy while at work.

[p155 | prose | 29.1 Introduction to inductions]
This information can be shared at an initial induction when a worker joins a project or workplace. It can also be shared at daily briefings such as toolbox or tailgate talks.

[p155 | prose | 29.1 Introduction to inductions]
Keep a record of workers who have completed induction and any other site access requirements.

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
29.2 Initial inductions

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
Initial worker inductions are useful for setting the scene for workers.

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
Initial inductions should include relevant site-specific information and requirements such as:

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– clear descriptions of the key roles and responsibilities of those on site, what these people look like, where they can be found, and how to contact them (having a photo board may help)

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– entering and exiting the site

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– speed limits

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– site layout

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– locations of facilities, including first aid

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– emergency procedures

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– known hazards on site and the control measures being used to manage the risks arising from hazards (for example, harnesses to be used for all work at height)

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– potential hazards that could be encountered (especially for mobile work)

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– how to report an incident or hazard

[p155 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– who their health and safety representatives (HSRs) are

[p156 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– how worker mental any physical health and wellbeing is promoted and supported (and where they can seek help if needed)

[p156 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– rules that should be complied with

[p156 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
– health and safety documents, policies, and plans specific to the worksite, such as a vehicle movement plan.

[p156 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
Some induction information such as general health and safety principles may be covered in industry-accepted health and safety, and site access training assessments that are completed by workers beforehand.

[p156 | prose | 29.2 Initial inductions]
Site inductions are usually administered by the site manager or a designated representative.

[p156 | prose | 29.3 Te ao Māori in induction – whakawhanaungatanga]
29.3 Te ao Māori in induction – whakawhanaungatanga

[p156 | prose | 29.3 Te ao Māori in induction – whakawhanaungatanga]
In a te ao Māori context, induction can be likened to whakawhanaungatanga which is, in essence, an introduction of oneself. This is important for all workers as it helps in:

[p156 | prose | 29.3 Te ao Māori in induction – whakawhanaungatanga]
– making connections

[p156 | prose | 29.3 Te ao Māori in induction – whakawhanaungatanga]
– making sure everyone knows who they are working with and who they need to know (in terms of emergency or reporting lines)

[p156 | prose | 29.3 Te ao Māori in induction – whakawhanaungatanga]
– building trust and confidence in each other.

[p156 | prose | 29.3 Te ao Māori in induction – whakawhanaungatanga]
Worker inductions allow workers the opportunity to introduce who they are and where they come from. Introductions can provide opportunities for personal connections to be made (not just within their business but across other businesses, when applicable).

[p156 | prose | 29.3 Te ao Māori in induction – whakawhanaungatanga]
Where relevant, consider inviting workers’ whānau to whānau inductions (a separate event run for whānau) so members of their whānau can feel a part of what is happening. Undertaking a holistic approach regarding workers and their whānau can help achieve a healthier and safer home, and work-life balance.

[p156 | prose | 29.4 Pre-work briefings (toolbox/tailgate talks)]
29.4 Pre-work briefings (toolbox/tailgate talks)

[p156 | prose | 29.4 Pre-work briefings (toolbox/tailgate talks)]
Pre-work briefings (sometimes known as toolbox talks or tailgate talks) are a good way to check-in with workers at the beginning of each shift. They can be used to:

[p156 | prose | 29.4 Pre-work briefings (toolbox/tailgate talks)]
– let workers know what will happening on site that day, and how it will be done in a healthy and safe way

[p156 | prose | 29.4 Pre-work briefings (toolbox/tailgate talks)]
– advise workers of any specific activities and risks to look out for that day (for example, expected weather conditions, or new or additional mobile plant in operation at the worksite)

[p156 | prose | 29.4 Pre-work briefings (toolbox/tailgate talks)]
– check with workers on their wellbeing and readiness for work.

[p156 | prose | 29.4 Pre-work briefings (toolbox/tailgate talks)]
This could be backed up with a visual aid such as a whiteboard with key details.

Karakia

[p156 | prose | Karakia]
Consider having a karakia at the commencement of shifts/toolbox talks/health and safety meetings.

[p156 | prose | Karakia]
The use of karakia (regardless of the religion or belief systems of your workers), is a means of creating a positive space for all. Karakia can be used to start and finish off the day, and is also be a means of bringing everyone together. Karakia can be used to connect workers to their environment and asks for that environment to protect them.

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
29.5 Tips for preparing an induction

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
When preparing induction material, you should consult with workers and all PCBUs in the contracting chain. You should also aim to:

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
– keep it as brief as possible

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
– use simple straightforward language

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
– tailor the document to suit the type of worksite or activity and cover the health and safety risks relevant to that site or activity. There is no such thing as a one-size-fits-all induction document

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
– use visual aids such drawings, diagrams, or objects where possible (they can often convey the message much more effectively than words)

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
– consider the range of literacy and language skills of workers that may need to read and understand it

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
– consider the range of skills, knowledge, and experience of new workers and visitors to the site (avoid using jargon or acronyms without explaining what they mean)

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
– consider providing any induction documentation in other languages.

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
All PCBUs on site should be fully aware of their roles and responsibilities before any activity is started. Site inductions should be completed for every new worker or visitor to a site.

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
In addition, the site manager or designated representative should:

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
– review the induction document regularly. Reviews should include considering any feedback from workers and other PCBUs in the contracting chain

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
– monitor all workers on site to make sure they are working safely and complying with the site rules

[p157 | prose | 29.5 Tips for preparing an induction]
– make sure action is taken when someone does not follow the site rules, or they operate in an unsafe manner.

Appendix 2: Health and Safety at Work Act duties

[p166 | appendix | Appendix 2: Health and Safety at Work Act duties]
The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA) is New Zealand’s key work health and safety law.

[p166 | appendix | Appendix 2: Health and Safety at Work Act duties]
All work and workplaces are covered by HSWA unless they have been specifically excluded. For example, HSWA does not apply to the armed forces in certain situations.

[p166 | appendix | Appendix 2: Health and Safety at Work Act duties]
HSWA sets out the work health and safety duties that duty holders must comply with.

[p166 | appendix | Appendix 2: Health and Safety at Work Act duties]
There are four types of duty holder under HSWA:

[p166 | appendix | Appendix 2: Health and Safety at Work Act duties]
– a person conducting a business or understanding (PCBU)

[p166 | appendix | Appendix 2: Health and Safety at Work Act duties]
– an officer

[p166 | appendix | Appendix 2: Health and Safety at Work Act duties]
– a worker

[p166 | appendix | Appendix 2: Health and Safety at Work Act duties]
– an ‘other person’ at the workplace.

[p166 | appendix | Appendix 2: Health and Safety at Work Act duties]
Most duties under HSWA relate to how work is carried out. However some duties are linked to where work is carried out: the workplace.

[p166 | appendix | Appendix 2: Health and Safety at Work Act duties]
A workplace is a place where work is being carried out or usually carried out for a business or undertaking. It includes any place where a worker goes or is likely to be while at work section 20 of HSWA

[p168 | appendix | Appendix 3: So far as is reasonably practicable]
Appendix 3: So far as is reasonably practicable

[p168 | appendix | Appendix 3: So far as is reasonably practicable]
section 22 of HSWA

[p168 | appendix | Appendix 3: So far as is reasonably practicable]
Certain PCBU duties (the section 36–43 duties including the primary duty of care) must be carried out ‘so far as is reasonably practicable’.

[p168 | prose | What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’]
What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’

[p168 | prose | What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’]
Just because something is possible to do, does not mean it is reasonably practicable in the circumstances.

[p168 | prose | What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’]
Consider:

[p168 | prose | What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’]
– What possible actions can be taken to ensure health and safety?

[p168 | prose | What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’]
– Of these possible actions, at a particular time, what is reasonable to do?

[p168 | prose | What to consider when deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’]
Think about the following questions.

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT THE RISK?]
WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT THE RISK?

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT THE RISK?]
– How likely is the risk to occur?

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT THE RISK?]
– How severe is the illness or injury that might occur if something goes wrong?

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT THE RISK?]
– What is known, or should reasonably be known, about the risk?

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
– What is known, or should reasonably be known, about the ways (control measures) to eliminate or minimise the risk?

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
– What control measures are available?

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
– How appropriate (suitable) are the control measures to manage the risk?

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
– What are the costs of these control measures?

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
– Are the costs grossly disproportionate to the risk? Cost must only be used as a reason to not do something when that cost is grossly out of proportion to the risk.

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
While PCBUs should check if there are widely used control measures for that risk (such as industry standards), they should always keep their specific circumstances in mind. A common industry practice might not be the most effective or appropriate control measure to use.

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
If PCBUs are not sure what control measures are appropriate, WorkSafe recommends getting advice from a suitably qualified and experienced health and safety professional.

[p168 | prose | WHAT IS KNOWN ABOUT POSSIBLE CONTROL MEASURES?]
For more information, see our guidance: Reasonably practicable

[p169 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA

[p169 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
More than one PCBU can have a duty in relation to the same matter. These PCBUs have overlapping duties – this means that the duties are shared between them.

[p169 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
Duties regularly overlap:

[p169 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
– in a shared workplace (for example, a building site or a port) where more than one business has control and influence over the work on site.

[p169 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
– in a contracting chain, where contractors and subcontractors provide services to a contracting PCBU or client and do not necessarily share the same workplace.

[p169 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
A PCBU must, so far as is reasonably practicable, consult, cooperate and coordinate activities with all other PCBUs they share duties with so that all PCBUs can meet their joint responsibilities.

[p169 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
A PCBU cannot transfer or contract out of their duties, or pass liability to another person.

[p169 | appendix | Appendix 4: Working with other PCBUs – overlapping duties section 34 of HSWA]
However a PCBU can make an agreement with another PCBU to fulfil specific duties. Even if this occurs, all PCBUs are still responsible for meeting their legal duties.

[p169 | prose | EXAMPLE]
EXAMPLE

[p169 | prose | EXAMPLE]
A local hotel contracts out housekeeping services to an agency. The hotel and agency both have a duty to ensure the health and safety of the housekeeping workers, so far as is reasonably practicable. This includes the duty to provide first aid facilities.

[p169 | prose | EXAMPLE]
The agency reaches an agreement with the hotel – if their workers need first aid while working at the hotel they can use the hotel’s first aid facilities.

[p169 | prose | EXAMPLE]
For more information, see our guidance: Overlapping duties

Appendix 5: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA

[p170 | appendix | Appendix 5: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
A PCBU in the supply chain (upstream) also has a duty to ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, that the work they do or the things they provide to other workplaces do not create health and safety risks.

[p170 | appendix | Appendix 5: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
An upstream PCBU is a business that:

[p170 | appendix | Appendix 5: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
– designs plant, substances, or structures

[p170 | appendix | Appendix 5: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
– manufactures plant, substances, or structures

[p170 | appendix | Appendix 5: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
– imports plant, substances, or structures

[p170 | appendix | Appendix 5: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
– supplies plant, substances, or structures

[p170 | appendix | Appendix 5: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
– installs, constructs or commissions plant or structures.

[p170 | appendix | Appendix 5: Upstream duties sections 39–43 of HSWA]
Upstream businesses are in a strong position to eliminate or minimise risk. They can influence and sometimes eliminate health and safety risks through designing, manufacturing, importing or supplying products that are safe for the end user.

[p170 | prose | EXAMPLE]
EXAMPLE

[p170 | prose | EXAMPLE]
A worker using a badly designed or poorly manufactured saw may be at risk of injury. This risk should have been eliminated or minimised, so far as was reasonably practicable, by the designer or manufacturer.

[p170 | prose | EXAMPLE]

[p170 | prose | EXAMPLE]
A designer creates or modifies a design for plant, substances or structures that are to be used or operated, or could be used or operated, in a workplace.

[p170 | prose | EXAMPLE]

[p170 | prose | EXAMPLE]
– to make sure the products they design do not create health and safety risks for the people that use them and those nearby

[p170 | prose | EXAMPLE]
– to make sure the products they design have been tested so they are safe for use in a workplace

[p170 | prose | EXAMPLE]
– to give the following information to those who will use the designed products:

[p170 | prose | EXAMPLE]
the design’s purpose or intended use

[p170 | prose | EXAMPLE]
the results of any calculations or tests

[p170 | prose | EXAMPLE]
- any general and current relevant information about how to safely use, handle, store, construct, inspect, clean, maintain, repair, or otherwise work near the designed products.

[p170 | prose | EXAMPLE]
These requirements apply across the product’s entire lifecycle – from manufacture and construction, through to everyday use, decommissioning and disposal.

These requirements apply across the product’s entire lifecycle – from construction or assembly, through to everyday use, decommissioning and disposal.

[p172 | prose | Upstream duties for installers, constructors or commissioners of plant or structures section 43 of HSWA]
Upstream duties for installers, constructors or commissioners of plant or structures section 43 of HSWA

[p172 | prose | Upstream duties for installers, constructors or commissioners of plant or structures section 43 of HSWA]
An installer/constructor builds and/or assembles and installs plant and structures that may be used at a workplace. A commissioner performs adjustments, tests and inspections on plant and structures before they are used for the first time in a workplace.

[p172 | prose | Upstream duties for installers, constructors or commissioners of plant or structures section 43 of HSWA]
An installer, constructor or commissioner has a duty, so far as is reasonably practicable, to make sure that the way the plant or structure is installed, constructed or commissioned does not create health and safety risks to the people that come into contact with it across the product’s entire lifecycle – from construction or assembly, through to everyday use, decommissioning and disposal.

[p172 | prose | Upstream duties for installers, constructors or commissioners of plant or structures section 43 of HSWA]
For more information, see our guidance: An additional health and safety duty for businesses that install, construct or commission plant or structures for workplaces

[p173 | prose | Engage with workers and enable their participation]
Engage with workers and enable their participation

[p173 | prose | Engage with workers and enable their participation]
A PCBU has two main duties related to worker engagement and participation:

[p173 | prose | Engage with workers and enable their participation]
– to engage with workers on health and safety matters that affect or are likely to affect workers, so far as is reasonably practicable, and

[p173 | prose | Engage with workers and enable their participation]
– to have practices that give workers reasonable opportunities to participate effectively in the ongoing improvement of work health and safety.

[p173 | prose | Engage with workers and enable their participation]
A PCBU can engage with workers by:

[p173 | prose | Engage with workers and enable their participation]
– sharing information about health and safety matters so that workers are well-informed, know what is going on and can contribute to decision-making

[p173 | prose | Engage with workers and enable their participation]
– giving workers reasonable opportunities to have a say about health and safety matters

[p173 | prose | Engage with workers and enable their participation]
– listening to and considering what workers have to say at each step of the risk management process

[p173 | prose | Engage with workers and enable their participation]
– considering workers’ views when health and safety decisions are being made

[p173 | prose | Engage with workers and enable their participation]
– updating workers about what decisions have been made.

[p173 | prose | Engage with workers and enable their participation]
A PCBU must engage with workers during specified times, including when identifying hazards and assessing risks.

[p173 | prose | Engage with workers and enable their participation]
A PCBU must have clear, effective, and ongoing ways for workers to suggest improvements or raise concerns.

[p174 | prose | Worker representation]
Worker representation

[p174 | prose | Worker representation]
Workers can be represented by a Health and Safety Representative (HSR), a union representing workers, or a person that workers authorise to represent them (for example, a community or church leader, or another trusted member of the community).

[p174 | prose | Worker representation]
HSRs and Health and Safety Committees (HSCs) are two well-established methods of participation and representation. If workers are represented by an HSR, worker engagement must also involve that representative.
