[p8 | prose | appropriate to their work environment.]
This guide provides practical advice on how to identify, control, and monitor the health and safety risks associated with working in extremely hot or cold environments.

[p8 | prose | appropriate to their work environment.]
Extremely hot or cold environments are places where temperatures are so high or low that they can cause heat-related or cold-related illness or injury to a person. Air temperature as well as other factors (for example humidity, radiant heat, and high wind) can contribute to creating an extreme temperature environment. Heat-related and cold-related illnesses and injuries can be fatal.

[p8 | prose | appropriate to their work environment.]
Extreme temperatures may be an ongoing risk (such as in a cool store) or a seasonal risk (such as outdoor work in summer or winter).

[p8 | prose | appropriate to their work environment.]
The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA) requires that all risks, including risks associated with working in extreme temperatures, must be managed so far as is reasonably practicable. For more information see Appendix B: What does the law say?

[p8 | prose | Who should read this guide?]
Who should read this guide?

[p8 | prose | Who should read this guide?]
This guide is for any PCBU (a person conducting a business or undertaking) who manages a workplace, or whose workers carry out work activities in environments that can be extremely hot or extremely cold.

[p8 | prose | Who should read this guide?]
It may also be useful for health and safety professionals who are providing extreme temperature risk assessments or monitoring for PCBUs.

[p9 | prose | 1.1 The human perception and response to hot and cold]
1.1 The human perception and response to hot and cold

[p9 | prose | 1.1 The human perception and response to hot and cold]
Six main factors combine to influence how the thermal environment will affect a person. These factors (environmental and personal) may be independent of each other, but together contribute to a worker’s overall feeling of being hot, cold, or just right.

[p9 | prose | Environmental factors]
Environmental factors

[p9 | prose | AIR TEMPERATURE]
AIR TEMPERATURE

[p9 | prose | AIR TEMPERATURE]
Air temperature is how hot or cold the air around us is. Although this is the easiest of all the influences to measure, it is not the only consideration. If air temperature is the only measurement taken, it is hard to predict how the temperature will affect an individual.

[p10 | prose | HUMIDITY]
HUMIDITY

[p10 | prose | HUMIDITY]
Humidity is the moisture content of the air. Humidity is especially important in hot or high activity situations. If the air has a high moisture content (over 80%), sweat will not evaporate as quickly, slowing a worker’s ability to stay cool and making them feel hotter. Relative humidity of between 40% and 70% is ideal as it usually does not have a major impact on thermal comfort.

[p10 | prose | RADIANT HEAT/REFLECTED HEAT]
RADIANT HEAT/REFLECTED HEAT

[p10 | prose | RADIANT HEAT/REFLECTED HEAT]
Radiant heat is emitted from anything that is hot, such as sunlight, furnaces, dryers and ovens. In time radiant heat will heat the air, but it will heat people and nearby solid surfaces more quickly. Heat can also be reflected on to people off reflective or light coloured surfaces.

[p10 | prose | AIR SPEED]
AIR SPEED

[p10 | prose | AIR SPEED]
Air speed is the amount of air movement. Draughty or windy conditions can help cool people in hot environments, but chill them in cold environments.

[p10 | prose | Personal factors]
Personal factors

[p10 | prose | CLOTHING]
CLOTHING

[p10 | prose | CLOTHING]
Clothing insulates people from the environment. It can help keep people warm in cold environments, but make it harder to stay cool in hot environments.

[p10 | prose | PHYSICAL ACTIVITY]
PHYSICAL ACTIVITY

[p10 | prose | PHYSICAL ACTIVITY]
As physical activity increases, so does the body’s heat production. In a cool situation, physical activity can help to warm a person. In a hot situation, physical activity can make a person feel even hotter.

[p10 | prose | Other factors]
Other factors

[p10 | prose | Other factors]
Other factors that may put a person at risk from high and low temperatures include:

[p10 | prose | Other factors]
– sex – males generally have a higher metabolic rate (produce body heat more readily) than women

[p10 | prose | Other factors]
– age – children and elderly people in particular

[p10 | prose | Other factors]
– underlying health conditions

[p10 | prose | Other factors]
– body build and weight

[p10 | prose | Other factors]
– use of certain prescribed medicines

[p10 | prose | Other factors]
– use of substances such as alcohol or illegal drugs

[p10 | prose | Other factors]
– acclimatisation

[p10 | prose | Other factors]
– temporary health issues (for example diarrhoea, hangover, fever).

[p10 | prose | How these influences interact to affect temperature perception]
How these influences interact to affect temperature perception

[p10 | prose | How these influences interact to affect temperature perception]
Some of the above influences may cancel each other out, while others can combine to put people at even greater risk of heat-related or cold-related illness or injury. For example:

[p10 | prose | How these influences interact to affect temperature perception]
– High temperatures and high humidity will make people feel even hotter and put the body under extra strain.

[p10 | prose | How these influences interact to affect temperature perception]
– High physical activity and high radiant heat will make people feel even hotter and put the body under extra strain.

[p10 | prose | How these influences interact to affect temperature perception]
– A person doing a strenuous activity while wearing heavy clothing and exposed to radiant heat could be at risk of overheating – even on a cool day.

[p10 | prose | How these influences interact to affect temperature perception]
– A person doing light work and wearing light clothing in mild weather could still be vulnerable to cold-related illness or injury if there are strong winds.

[p11 | prose | Core body temperature regulation]
Core body temperature regulation

[p11 | prose | Core body temperature regulation]
The human body needs to maintain a core temperature of about 37°C to function normally. The body has its own mechanisms for maintaining a normal core temperature when challenged by hot or cold conditions.

[p11 | prose | PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN HOT CONDITIONS]
PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN HOT CONDITIONS

[p11 | prose | PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN HOT CONDITIONS]
Evaporation of sweat from the skin’s surface has a cooling effect.

[p11 | prose | PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN HOT CONDITIONS]
Increased blood flow to the skin

[p11 | prose | PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN HOT CONDITIONS]
Blood cools more quickly near the surface of the skin.

[p11 | prose | PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN COLD CONDITIONS]
PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN COLD CONDITIONS

[p11 | prose | PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN COLD CONDITIONS]
Reduced blood flow to the skin

[p11 | prose | PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN COLD CONDITIONS]
Blood flow is redirected to the body’s core (chest and abdomen) This reduces heat loss and prioritises keeping vital organs warm.

[p11 | prose | PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN COLD CONDITIONS]
Shivering

[p11 | prose | PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN COLD CONDITIONS]
Involuntary muscle activity (shivering) creates metabolic heat in the body.

[p11 | prose | PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN COLD CONDITIONS]
Physical responses to hot and cold conditions

[p11 | prose | PHYSICAL RESPONSE IN COLD CONDITIONS]
When hot or cold conditions are extreme, or the body’s natural ways of warming up or cooling down are restricted, the body may struggle to maintain a stable core temperature. The body may begin to suffer from heat-related or cold- related illnesses or injury. These illnesses and injuries can be fatal.

[p14 | prose | Types of heat-related illness and injury]
– heat rashes

[p14 | prose | Types of heat-related illness and injury]
– heavy sweating

[p14 | prose | Types of heat-related illness and injury]
– muscle cramps.

[p14 | prose | Types of heat-related illness and injury]
If the early signs are not identified and treated, this can lead to heat exhaustion and heat stroke.

2.3 Personal protective equipment (PPE) for work in extremely hot environments

[p20 | prose | PPE can be worn to reduce any remaining risk of heat-related illness and injury]
PPE can be worn to reduce any remaining risk of heat-related illness and injury

[p20 | prose | PPE can be worn to reduce any remaining risk of heat-related illness and injury]

[p21 | prose | Examples of PPE available to help keep workers cool in hot environments]
Examples of PPE available to help keep workers cool in hot environments

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT SHIELDS AGAINST EXTREME HEAT]
CLOTHING THAT SHIELDS AGAINST EXTREME HEAT

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT SHIELDS AGAINST EXTREME HEAT]
This includes flame-resistant clothing (such as Nomex©) and heat-reflective clothing. This clothing can be useful in work areas with extreme radiant heat that cannot be controlled through elimination, substitution, isolation, engineering, or administrative control measures.

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT SHIELDS AGAINST EXTREME HEAT]
Most heat-reflective clothing is not breathable. This means the ability for sweat to evaporate is limited, leaving workers at risk of overheating. It may be necessary to wear clothing underneath that provides personal cooling to reduce this risk (see below).

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
Clothing with built-in cooling systems includes:

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
– Water-activated evaporative cooling vests, caps and scarves: These are first filled with or submersed in water, then wrung out and worn over the top of workers’ clothing or on the head. The water interacts with polymer crystals. As the water evaporates it helps reduce body temperature. They are lightweight, do not require electricity, and can be washed and re-used. In addition to vests, cooling caps can be worn under helmets, and ties and scarves can be applied at pulse points. However, as these clothing options rely on evaporation, they are not as effective in high humidity environments.

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
– Ice-chilled cooling vests and straps: These use ice/gel packs that are pre- chilled in a freezer then placed in pockets inside the cooling vest or strap. Because they are very cold to the touch, they are worn over workers’ clothes.

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
– Phase change cooling vests: These use cooling packs that draw heat into them. They usually last between 1.5–4 hours. After that time they can have the opposite effect and make the wearer hotter.

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
– Thermoelectric cooling vests: These cool down the inner surface of the vest. They are powered by a portable battery.

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
– Cool flow cooling vests: These use a water flow system that pumps chilled water through a vest using hoses. However, workers must remain tethered to the cooling reservoir.

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
When considering these types of clothing, you should think about:

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
– How accessible is the area workers will be working in?

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
– How long do the cooling properties of the clothing need to last?

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
– How many workers are going to need this clothing?

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
– What back-up facilities will the various types of cooling equipment need (for example, air supplies, electricity, or freezing facilities)?

[p21 | prose | CLOTHING THAT PROVIDES PERSONAL COOLING]
– Will wearing the PPE create any other risks such as limiting mobility?

[p21 | prose | PPE can contribute to heat-related illness and injury]
PPE can contribute to heat-related illness and injury

[p21 | prose | PPE can contribute to heat-related illness and injury]
Although PPE can help protect workers from multiple risks while working, it can also contribute to the risk of workers suffering a heat-related illness or injury. Heavy or layered PPE can restrict sweating – one of the body’s natural temperature control functions. As air flows over the skin, sweat evaporates and draws away body heat with it, creating a cooling effect.

[p22 | prose | PPE can contribute to heat-related illness and injury]
When selecting PPE/clothing for workers:

[p22 | prose | PPE can contribute to heat-related illness and injury]
– make sure wherever possible that PPE is light and breathable

[p22 | prose | PPE can contribute to heat-related illness and injury]
– select uniforms that are made from natural, breathable fibres

[p22 | prose | PPE can contribute to heat-related illness and injury]
– give workers a range of options with uniforms or other required clothing so they can add and remove layers as needed to remain comfortable

[p22 | prose | PPE can contribute to heat-related illness and injury]
– select light colours (light colours reflect heat away)

[p22 | prose | PPE can contribute to heat-related illness and injury]
– offer clothes for outdoor workers that provide suitable protection from UV radiation (long-sleeves are best)

[p22 | prose | PPE can contribute to heat-related illness and injury]
– make sure the PPE does not interfere with the functioning of any other required PPE.

[p22 | prose | PPE can contribute to heat-related illness and injury]
To maximise heat exchange, clothes should be able to transmit moisture. Moisture can be transmitted through breathable fabrics or through clothing openings (as the neck, ankle, and arms).

[p22 | prose | Sweating and frequent hydration is usually the best option]
Sweating and frequent hydration is usually the best option

[p22 | prose | Sweating and frequent hydration is usually the best option]
A critical method of body temperature control is for a person to sweat freely, and frequently replace lost fluids. When a person puts on clothing that protects or shields them from a hot environment, sweat may no longer be able to evaporate. This shuts off the body’s internal heat reduction system.

[p22 | prose | Sweating and frequent hydration is usually the best option]
Workers should only wear PPE that is absolutely necessary after all other reasonably practicable control measures have been used to keep them safe from extreme heat. Excessive PPE requirements can create a new risk for workers in the form of heat-related illness.

[p22 | prose | 2.4 Dehydration]
2.4 Dehydration

[p22 | prose | 2.4 Dehydration]
Working in a hot environment causes sweating which helps keep people cool, but also means losing vital water and salts that must be replaced. You should provide access to cool drinking water and encourage workers to drink it frequently in small amounts before, during and after working. Avoid tea, coffee or carbonated drinks.

[p22 | prose | 2.4 Dehydration]
Workers should make sure they are well hydrated before beginning work in hot environments. Thirst is not a good indicator that it is time to start drinking. By the time a person feels thirsty they are most likely already starting to suffer from the effects of dehydration.

[p22 | prose | 2.4 Dehydration]
During heavy work in a hot environment workers should generally drink at least 2–4 glasses of cool water every hour. Engage with your workers to come up with strategies and ideas for making sure they get enough hydration.

[p22 | prose | 2.4 Dehydration]
In some cases a defined rehydration programme may be needed. A health and safety professional can provide advice on appropriate hydration schedules for each worker including:

[p22 | prose | 2.4 Dehydration]
– amount

[p22 | prose | 2.4 Dehydration]
– frequency

[p22 | prose | 2.4 Dehydration]
– type of hydration – for example water or electrolyte solution.

[p22 | prose | 2.4 Dehydration]
Workers should avoid ice-cold water as this can cause stomach cramps.

[p23 | prose | 2.5 Acclimatisation to hot environments]
2.5 Acclimatisation to hot environments

[p23 | prose | 2.5 Acclimatisation to hot environments]
Acclimatisation is when a worker’s body gradually adapts to working in a hotter than normal environment

[p23 | prose | 2.5 Acclimatisation to hot environments]
Your workers may acclimatise to working in higher temperatures if they are working in a hot environment long-term. Depending on their level of fitness, sex and ethnicity, this usually takes between 7–15 days.

[p23 | prose | 2.5 Acclimatisation to hot environments]
During the process of acclimatisation the body goes through the following changes:

[p23 | prose | 2.5 Acclimatisation to hot environments]
– a gradual increase in sweating, increasing heat loss

[p23 | prose | 2.5 Acclimatisation to hot environments]
– sweat becomes less salty as the body learns to conserve salts (losing salts through sweat is what leads to muscle cramps)

[p23 | prose | 2.5 Acclimatisation to hot environments]
– weight loss (helps with heat loss as a layer of insulating body fat is lost)

[p23 | prose | 2.5 Acclimatisation to hot environments]
– increased water consumption (to replace water lost though increased sweating). After a person becomes acclimatised, they feel thirst whenever their body needs more fluid.

[p23 | prose | 2.5 Acclimatisation to hot environments]
People only remain acclimatised as long as they are regularly exposed to hot conditions. If workers change to a cooler work task, or are away from work for an extended time, they will probably lose their acclimatisation.

[p23 | prose | 2.5 Acclimatisation to hot environments]
When returning to work in extreme heat, workers may be less tolerant of the heat than before. You need to manage their return to work with this in mind. A suitably qualified health and safety professional will be able to provide you with advice on safe practices for returning a worker to work in extreme heat.

Identifying and controlling extreme cold risks will reduce the chances of cold-related illness or injury happening at your work.

[p25 | prose | Identifying and controlling extreme cold risks will reduce the chances of cold-related illness or injury happening at your work.]
Cold-related illnesses and injuries can happen when workers are exposed to:

[p25 | prose | Identifying and controlling extreme cold risks will reduce the chances of cold-related illness or injury happening at your work.]
– low air temperature

[p25 | prose | Identifying and controlling extreme cold risks will reduce the chances of cold-related illness or injury happening at your work.]
– high or cold wind/air flow

[p25 | prose | Identifying and controlling extreme cold risks will reduce the chances of cold-related illness or injury happening at your work.]
– dampness/water (water conducts heat away from the body 25 times faster than dry air).

[p25 | prose | Identifying and controlling extreme cold risks will reduce the chances of cold-related illness or injury happening at your work.]
Exposure to these conditions can cause skin temperature to drop and in time the body’s core temperature to drop below the healthy average of 37°C.

[p25 | prose | Identifying and controlling extreme cold risks will reduce the chances of cold-related illness or injury happening at your work.]
Cold-related illnesses and injury are risks especially when working outdoors in cold weather or working in artificially cold environments, such as refrigerated areas. In wet environments illness and injury can happen even at moderate temperatures.

[p25 | prose | Identifying and controlling extreme cold risks will reduce the chances of cold-related illness or injury happening at your work.]
Unlike heat-related illness and injury, the early symptoms of cold-related illness or injury normally cause quite obvious discomfort, causing workers in most situations to take action before injury or illness occurs.

[p25 | prose | Identifying and controlling extreme cold risks will reduce the chances of cold-related illness or injury happening at your work.]
PCBUs whose workplace temperatures often drop to uncomfortable levels need to consider cold-related illness or injury as a risk to workers and should create a cold management plan.

Non-freezing injuries

[p25 | prose | CHILBLAINS]
CHILBLAINS

[p25 | prose | CHILBLAINS]
Chilblains are caused by extended exposure to cold, but not freezing, temperatures. They normally occur on the extremities such as ears, fingers and toes. The affected skin swells, goes red and usually has tingling and pain.

[p26 | prose | TRENCH FOOT/IMMERSION FOOT]
TRENCH FOOT/IMMERSION FOOT

[p26 | prose | TRENCH FOOT/IMMERSION FOOT]
Trench foot and immersion foot result from feet being left cold and wet for extended periods of time. Symptoms include tingling, itching and burning feelings in the feet and toes, swelling of the lower legs and blisters. The best way to avoid trench foot/immersion foot is to keep feet warm and dry, change socks often, and make sure socks and shoes are not too tight, to promote good circulation.

[p26 | prose | Freezing injuries]
Freezing injuries

[p26 | prose | FROSTNIP]
FROSTNIP

[p26 | prose | FROSTNIP]
Frostnip affects the top layers of skin when exposed to extreme cold. It usually affects the extremities such as ears, noses, fingers and toes. The top layers of skin turn white and may feel hard but deeper tissue is still soft and healthy. Wearing gloves and hats can help protect against frostnip.

[p26 | prose | FROSTBITE]
FROSTBITE

[p26 | prose | FROSTBITE]
Frostbite occurs when body tissue freezes due to exposure to extreme cold or by contact with extremely cold objects (such as metal objects in sub-zero conditions). Blood vessels can be severely and permanently damaged by frostbite. In extreme cases tissue death occurs, which can lead to gangrene and amputation.

[p26 | prose | FROSTBITE]
Frostbite most often affects the extremities such as the face and ears, fingers and toes.

[p26 | prose | FROSTBITE]
Signs of frostbite include:

[p26 | prose | FROSTBITE]
– cold, tingling, stinging or aching in the affected area

[p26 | prose | FROSTBITE]
– numbness

[p26 | prose | FROSTBITE]
– aching

[p26 | prose | FROSTBITE]
– skin turns red, then purple, then very pale or white

[p26 | prose | FROSTBITE]
– skin is very cold to the touch

[p26 | prose | FROSTBITE]
– in severe cases the skin will become hard or blister.

[p26 | prose | FROSTBITE]
Skin that has had cold damage in the past will be much more at risk of frostbite in the future.

[p26 | prose | Hypothermia]
Hypothermia

[p26 | prose | Hypothermia]
Hypothermia is the most serious cold-related illness. Hypothermia occurs when the body can no longer maintain a core temperature of about 37°C.

[p26 | prose | Hypothermia]
When the body is unable to warm itself, and drops below 36°C, hypothermia occurs. If left untreated it can lead to permanent organ damage and death.

[p26 | prose | Hypothermia]
If a person is experiencing any symptoms of hypothermia, they should be removed from the cold environment. Seek immediate medical help. Hypothermia can be fatal if left untreated.

[p27 | prose | Immersion hypothermia]
Immersion hypothermia

[p27 | prose | Immersion hypothermia]
Occupations that require immersion in water, such as commercial diving, carry a greater risk of workers developing cold-related illnesses such as immersion hypothermia. Water draws heat away from the body 25 times faster than air. Even in water temperatures as high as 20°C, hypothermia is still a risk.

[p27 | prose | Immersion hypothermia]
To reduce the risk of developing immersion hypothermia, workers/divers should wear wetsuits thick enough to provide protection in cooler waters and deeper dives.

[p27 | prose | Immersion hypothermia]
Accessories such as hoods, gloves and booties also provide good thermal protection when diving in cooler waters.

[p27 | prose | Immersion hypothermia]
If using a drysuit, thermal undergarments should be worn.

[p27 | prose | Immersion hypothermia]
Once out of the water, workers should change into warm, dry clothing as soon as possible, especially if there is significant wind-chill present. Damp wetsuits contribute to heat loss even further through evaporation.

[p27 | prose | Immersion hypothermia]
See Section 8 First aid for heat-related and cold-related illnesses and injuries for a summary of symptoms and first aid treatments for cold-related illnesses and injury.

[p33 | prose | 3.3 Personal protective equipment (PPE) for work in extremely cold environments]
3.3 Personal protective equipment (PPE) for work in extremely cold environments

[p33 | prose | PPE can be used to reduce any remaining risk of cold-related illness and injury]
PPE can be used to reduce any remaining risk of cold-related illness and injury

[p33 | prose | PPE can be used to reduce any remaining risk of cold-related illness and injury]
PPE is typically considered the last option when providing protection from harm while working. However when protecting workers from extreme cold, especially in outdoor environments, clothing/PPE has a very important role.

[p33 | prose | PPE can be used to reduce any remaining risk of cold-related illness and injury]
Clothing provides workers with protection from the cold by:

[p33 | prose | PPE can be used to reduce any remaining risk of cold-related illness and injury]
– providing insulation by trapping warm air between clothing layers.

[p33 | prose | PPE can be used to reduce any remaining risk of cold-related illness and injury]
– protecting the body from wind-chill

[p33 | prose | PPE can be used to reduce any remaining risk of cold-related illness and injury]
– keeping workers dry.

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
General requirements for providing PPE

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
PPE for protecting against the effects of extreme cold can be provided by:

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
– the person conducting a business or undertaking (PCBU)

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
– another PCBU (costs could be shared)

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
– the worker (if they genuinely and voluntarily choose to provide their own PPE).

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
The Health and Safety at Work (General Risk and Workplace Management) Regulations 2016 require PCBUs to make sure PPE is:

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
– suitable for the nature of the work and any risks associated with that work

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
– a suitable size, fit, and reasonably comfortable to wear

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
– suitably maintained, repaired and replaced

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
– compatible with any other PPE.

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
These requirements apply to PPE supplied by you or your workers.

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
PCBUs must also provide workers with information and training about how to properly wear or use PPE, and how to store and maintain it.

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
PCBUs cannot pass on the cost (in full or part) of providing PPE to workers.

[p33 | prose | General requirements for providing PPE]
For more general advice on providing PPE see WorkSafe’s guidance: Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

[p33 | prose | What to think about when choosing PPE for work in extremely cold environments]
What to think about when choosing PPE for work in extremely cold environments

[p33 | prose | What to think about when choosing PPE for work in extremely cold environments]
When considering what PPE is needed to protect against cold illness or injury you need to think about:

[p33 | prose | TEMPERATURE]
TEMPERATURE

[p33 | prose | TEMPERATURE]
– What is the average temperature where work will be taking place?

[p33 | prose | TEMPERATURE]
– Are you protecting workers against moderately cold or sub-zero temperatures?

[p33 | prose | TEMPERATURE]
– Will workers be moving between work in cold and warm environments?

[p33 | prose | WEATHER CONDITIONS]
WEATHER CONDITIONS

[p33 | prose | WEATHER CONDITIONS]
– Will workers be exposed to strong or cold winds?

[p33 | prose | WEATHER CONDITIONS]
– Will workers to be exposed to rainy or wet conditions?

[p33 | prose | WEATHER CONDITIONS]
– Will these conditions likely occur some of the time or a lot of the time?

[p34 | prose | WORK TASKS]
WORK TASKS

[p34 | prose | WORK TASKS]
– Do work tasks require manual dexterity or a lot of physical manoeuvrability?

[p34 | prose | WORK TASKS]
– Are workers likely to build up a sweat, despite the cold conditions?

[p34 | prose | WORK TASKS]
– Will the PPE add any risks, for example limiting mobility?

[p34 | prose | COMPATIBILITY WITH OTHER PPE]
COMPATIBILITY WITH OTHER PPE

[p34 | prose | COMPATIBILITY WITH OTHER PPE]
– Could the PPE interfere with the functioning of any other required PPE?

[p34 | prose | COMPATIBILITY WITH OTHER PPE]
If suitable protective clothing is not available to prevent cold illness or injury from happening, work should be suspended until suitable clothing is available or weather conditions improve.

[p34 | prose | COMPATIBILITY WITH OTHER PPE]
Workers should have a spare set of dry clothing that can be changed into if clothing gets wet.

[p34 | prose | Layering is best for keeping warm and comfortable]
Layering is best for keeping warm and comfortable

[p34 | prose | Layering is best for keeping warm and comfortable]
Clothing should be worn in multiple layers rather than a single thick garment. This traps warm air, which insulates against the cold. Having several layers also gives workers the option to open or remove a layer before they get too warm and start sweating, or to add a layer when they take a break. Sweating in extremely cold environments can increase the risks of cold-related illness or injury because the resulting damp clothing can cool, and even freeze.

[p34 | prose | Layering is best for keeping warm and comfortable]
For working in windy conditions (where workers cannot be shielded) a windproof outer layer should be provided.

[p34 | prose | Layering is best for keeping warm and comfortable]
For working in wet conditions, waterproof clothing should be provided. Workers can cool much more quickly when damp and cold are combined.

[p34 | prose | Choose the right fabric]
Choose the right fabric

[p34 | prose | Choose the right fabric]
Different fabrics suit different needs for:

[p34 | prose | Choose the right fabric]
– outer/weather-proof layers, choose waterproof and windproof but breathable fabrics (such as Gor-Tex© or Nylon)

[p34 | prose | Choose the right fabric]
– a middle insulating layer that will absorb moisture from body and provide insulation, choose wool fabrics

[p34 | prose | Choose the right fabric]
– an inner layer that draws moisture away from the body, choose synthetics such as polypropylene.

[p34 | prose | Choose the right fabric]
Cotton is not recommended as it holds moisture easily and loses its insulation properties when wet.

[p34 | prose | Getting the correct fit is important]
Getting the correct fit is important

[p34 | prose | Getting the correct fit is important]
Avoid tight clothing as this restricts blood flow. Good blood flow, especially to the extremities, is essential for helping prevent cold injuries such as frost nip and frostbite.

[p34 | prose | Getting the correct fit is important]
Looser fitting clothing allows warm air to be trapped between layers but tight clothing does not allow for this. Loose clothing also provides better ventilation to help avoid sweating.

[p34 | prose | Getting the correct fit is important]
However, clothing should not be so baggy that it creates a risk of being caught in machinery.

[p35 | prose | Caring for thermally protective clothing]
Caring for thermally protective clothing

[p35 | prose | Caring for thermally protective clothing]
Thermally protective clothing should be cleaned and stored appropriately:

[p35 | prose | Caring for thermally protective clothing]
– Clothing should be kept clean to prevent air spaces (which increase insulation) from becoming clogged with dirt.

[p35 | prose | Caring for thermally protective clothing]
– Moisture should be kept off clothes by removing snow or shaking off water before entering heated shelters.

[p35 | prose | Caring for thermally protective clothing]
– Facilities should be available for drying clothing items that become damp or wet from water or sweat.

[p35 | prose | Protect the head, face and eyes]

– woollen caps and beanies

[p35 | prose | Protect the head, face and eyes]
– balaclavas

[p35 | prose | Protect the head, face and eyes]
– face and neck warmers

[p35 | prose | Protect the head, face and eyes]
– thermal liners (for wearing under hard hats)

[p35 | prose | Protect the head, face and eyes]
– protective glasses

[p35 | prose | Protect the head, face and eyes]
– ear muffs

[p35 | prose | Protect the head, face and eyes]
– lip balm and moisturising lotions to prevent lesions.

[p35 | prose | Protect the head, face and eyes]
Head coverings should provide protection for ears.

[p35 | prose | Protect the head, face and eyes]
In extremely cold conditions where face protection is worn, eye protection should be separated from the nose and mouth to stop condensation building up on the glasses.

[p35 | prose | Keep hands and fingers warm]
Keep hands and fingers warm

[p35 | prose | Keep hands and fingers warm]
If fine manual dexterity is not required, gloves should be worn for:

[p35 | prose | Keep hands and fingers warm]
– sedentary work under 10°C

[p35 | prose | Keep hands and fingers warm]
– light work under 4°C

[p35 | prose | Keep hands and fingers warm]
– moderate work less than -7°C.

[p35 | prose | Keep hands and fingers warm]
Gloves must be the correct size to avoid creating safety risks.

[p35 | prose | Keep hands and fingers warm]
Where the temperature is below -17°C, mittens should be worn where possible. Mittens with all fingers enclosed together and only the thumb is separate provide better thermal insulation than gloves.

[p35 | prose | Keep hands and fingers warm]
If fine work needs to be performed with bare hands for greater than 10–20 minutes at temperatures below 16°C, you should keep workers’ hands warm by providing warm air blowers, and provide insulated handles on tools.

[p35 | prose | Keep feet warm and dry]
Keep feet warm and dry

[p35 | prose | Keep feet warm and dry]
Make sure footwear is kept dry, and replace footwear when it becomes wet. Wet feet lose heat 25 times faster than dry feet.

[p35 | prose | Keep feet warm and dry]
Felt-lined, rubber-bottomed, leather-topped boots with removable thermal insoles are recommended for work in cold environments.

[p35 | prose | Keep feet warm and dry]
Thick socks are good for providing insulation, but make sure the thickness of socks does not make footwear too tight. Tight socks or footwear can restrict blood flow which will increase the chances of developing trenchfoot or frost bite.

[p35 | prose | Keep feet warm and dry]
If work involves standing in water or slush (for example, firefighting, farming), waterproof boots should be worn.

[p36 | prose | 3.4 Acclimatisation to cold environments]
3.4 Acclimatisation to cold environments

[p36 | prose | 3.4 Acclimatisation to cold environments]
Acclimatisation is when a worker’s body gradually adapts to working in an extreme environment.

[p36 | prose | 3.4 Acclimatisation to cold environments]
The human body does not acclimatise well to cold environments. However, frequently exposed body parts such as the hands can develop some tolerance to cold. The body can get used to maintaining blood flow to fingers in conditions that would otherwise normally result in a reduction in blood flow.

[p36 | prose | 3.4 Acclimatisation to cold environments]
You should not rely on acclimatisation as a control measure.

[p36 | prose | 3.4 Acclimatisation to cold environments]
New workers should be provided with insulated personal protective clothing to wear before work starts (and after all other control measures are in in place).

4.1 Exposure monitoring for extreme temperatures

[p38 | prose | 4.1 Exposure monitoring for extreme temperatures]
What is exposure monitoring?

[p38 | prose | 4.1 Exposure monitoring for extreme temperatures]
Exposure monitoring is the measurement and evaluation of exposure to a health hazard experienced by a person at work. This can include exposure to extremely high or low temperatures at work. It can help to determine what control measures are needed, or if existing control measures are working.

[p38 | prose | 4.1 Exposure monitoring for extreme temperatures]
The working environment should be monitored to make sure thermal conditions do not change to levels that threaten worker safety (as determined in a heat or cold management plan).

[p38 | prose | 4.1 Exposure monitoring for extreme temperatures]
It includes measuring and monitoring the thermal conditions in the work environment. For example:

[p38 | prose | 4.1 Exposure monitoring for extreme temperatures]
– air temperature

[p38 | prose | 4.1 Exposure monitoring for extreme temperatures]
– humidity/water exposure

[p38 | prose | 4.1 Exposure monitoring for extreme temperatures]
– radiant heat sources

[p38 | prose | 4.1 Exposure monitoring for extreme temperatures]
– air speed

[p38 | prose | 4.1 Exposure monitoring for extreme temperatures]
– length of exposure.

[p38 | prose | 4.1 Exposure monitoring for extreme temperatures]
Monitoring should also take into consideration workers’ level of physical activity and what clothes and/or PPE they may be required to wear.

[p38 | prose | 4.1 Exposure monitoring for extreme temperatures]
Monitoring does not control risk and does not replace the need for control measures to eliminate or minimise worker exposure to high or low temperatures. It can help confirm if control measures are required or if existing control measures are working.

[p38 | prose | Who can carry out the exposure monitoring?]
Who can carry out the exposure monitoring?

[p38 | prose | Who can carry out the exposure monitoring?]
Monitoring should be carried out by a competent person such as an occupational hygienist who has the right knowledge, skills and experience in appropriate techniques and procedures, including interpreting results.

[p38 | prose | Who can carry out the exposure monitoring?]
You can find a list of workplace health and safety professionals on the HASANZ website register: www.hasanz.org.nz or contact the New Zealand Occupational Hygiene Society: www.nzohs.org.nz

[p39 | prose | Who can carry out the exposure monitoring?]
How often should exposure monitoring be carried out?

[p39 | prose | Who can carry out the exposure monitoring?]
For work environments where extreme temperatures are a constant risk, monitoring will need to be carried out at regular intervals and when there are significant changes in the work environment which may affect temperature.

[p39 | prose | Who can carry out the exposure monitoring?]
For work environments where extreme temperatures are only a risk some of the time, a plan should be put in place to trigger when monitoring should be done.

[p39 | prose | Who can carry out the exposure monitoring?]
This may mean getting monitoring done at different times of the year across the seasons. A health and safety professional can provide you with a customised monitoring schedule to suit your work environment.

[p39 | prose | Who can carry out the exposure monitoring?]
For more information on workplace exposure monitoring see:

[p39 | prose | Who can carry out the exposure monitoring?]
– WorkSafe’s fact sheet: Exposure Monitoring under the Health and Safety at Work (General Risk and Workplace Management) Regulations 2016

[p39 | prose | Who can carry out the exposure monitoring?]
If monitoring shows there are still unacceptable thermal conditions in the work environment, or workers are still at risk of heat-related or cold-related illnesses, further control measures should be put in place, or work should stop until the environment is safe again.

[p57 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
What does reasonably practicable mean?

[p57 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
Health and safety duties need to be managed so far as is reasonably practicable. There are two parts to ‘reasonably practicable’. First consider what is possible in your circumstances to ensure health and safety. Then consider, of these possible actions, what is reasonable to do in your circumstances.

[p57 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
When deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’, consider:

[p57 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
– How likely is the risk and how severe is the illness or injury that might result?

[p57 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
– What do you know, or what you ought reasonably to know, about the hazard or risk and the ways of eliminating or minimising the risk?

[p57 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
– What is the availability of the control measures, and how suitable are they for the specific risk?

[p57 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
– As a final step, what are the costs of the control measure and are the costs grossly disproportionate to the risk?

[p57 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
For more information, see WorkSafe’s fact sheet: Reasonably practicable

[p58 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working in extreme temperatures]
– giving workers reasonable opportunities to have a say about extreme temperature health and safety matters

[p58 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working in extreme temperatures]
– listening to and considering what workers have to say at each step of the risk management process for extreme temperatures

[p58 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working in extreme temperatures]
– considering workers’ views when decisions are being made

[p58 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working in extreme temperatures]
– updating workers about what decisions have been made.

[p58 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working in extreme temperatures]
