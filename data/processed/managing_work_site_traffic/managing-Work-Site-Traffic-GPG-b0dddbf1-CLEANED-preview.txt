1.1 Who should use these guidelines?

[p10 | prose | 1.1 Who should use these guidelines?]
These guidelines are for any person conducting a business or undertaking (PCBU) who manages a work site where there are vehicles or mobile plant operating, or whose workers carry out work at a work site where there are vehicles or mobile plant operating.

[p10 | prose | 1.1 Who should use these guidelines?]
It may also be useful for workers, health and safety professionals, or traffic management consultants who are providing traffic management advice to PCBUs.

[p10 | prose | 1.2 What are these guidelines about?]

Where more than one business shares a work site, they must work together to manage the risks related to work site traffic.

[p14 | prose | Where more than one business shares a work site, they must work together to manage the risks related to work site traffic.]
Often there will be more than one PCBU operating at a work site, or PCBUs will be working within a contracting chain with other PCBUs. In these situations the PCBUs will likely have health and safety duties for the same risks. This is known as overlapping duties.

[p14 | prose | Where more than one business shares a work site, they must work together to manage the risks related to work site traffic.]
PCBUs that share the same duties at a work site (or through a contracting chain), must consult, cooperate and coordinate with each other to manage the shared risk so far as is reasonably practicable.

[p14 | prose | Where more than one business shares a work site, they must work together to manage the risks related to work site traffic.]
One PCBU cannot push the responsibility to manage risk on to another PBCU and they cannot contract out of their responsibilities to manage risk.

[p14 | prose | Where more than one business shares a work site, they must work together to manage the risks related to work site traffic.]
Shared work sites can include:

[p14 | prose | Where more than one business shares a work site, they must work together to manage the risks related to work site traffic.]
– work sites where there is a main PCBU present and vehicles operated by other PCBUs come and go with pick-ups and deliveries. For example, factories and warehouses

[p14 | prose | Where more than one business shares a work site, they must work together to manage the risks related to work site traffic.]
– work sites where a PCBU may be operating on land leased from another PCBU. The PCBU leasing the site and the landlord both have a responsibility to make sure the work site is safe

[p14 | prose | Where more than one business shares a work site, they must work together to manage the risks related to work site traffic.]
– work sites where there is a main PCBU with other PBCUs contracted to perform certain tasks at the work site. For example, construction sites

[p14 | prose | Where more than one business shares a work site, they must work together to manage the risks related to work site traffic.]
– work sites where the facilities are owned by one PCBU and several PCBUs work independently of each other but share some aspects of the work site (such as shared vehicle entry and exits)

[p14 | prose | Where more than one business shares a work site, they must work together to manage the risks related to work site traffic.]
– vehicles where workers of more than one PCBU are at work. For example, supermarket workers unloading a trailer owned by a transport operator.

[p15 | prose | Where more than one business shares a work site, they must work together to manage the risks related to work site traffic.]
PCBUs should decide together how the risks will be managed. For example, they could agree that the PCBU that manages the work site focuses on control measures related to their work site layout, while the PCBU whose vehicles visit the work site focuses on control measures related to their vehicles and drivers. Both should work together to establish safe practices for activities that involve workers from both PCBUs, such as loading and unloading, and spotting reversing vehicles.

[p15 | prose | 2.1 Dynamic work site example]
2.1 Dynamic work site example

[p15 | prose | 2.1 Dynamic work site example]
A large construction site needed to change its layout to accommodate the development of the project. As part of redesigning the site layout, the site manager needed to shift the vehicle entry and exit points.

[p15 | prose | 2.1 Dynamic work site example]
As a part of planning the new layout, the site manager consulted with the transport operators that visit the site regularly to make sure that the proposed design was safe for drivers making deliveries and pick-ups. By talking with the other PCBUs first, the site manager was able to make sure the proposed layout was workable, and confirm that it would not introduce new risks for drivers.

[p15 | prose | 2.1 Dynamic work site example]
The site manager and transport operators were then able to coordinate communication so that all workers (site workers and the drivers) were aware of the changes, including the updated traffic management plan, beforehand.

[p15 | prose | 2.2 Static work site example]
2.2 Static work site example

[p15 | prose | 2.2 Static work site example]
A large commercial warehouse, is divided into several sections. Each section is leased to a separate PCBU, who all work independently of each other. The PCBUs share a common street entrance and main thoroughfare around the building, and a visitor car park.

[p15 | prose | 2.2 Static work site example]
The PCBUs and the warehouse landlord met together, and agreed on how to manage the traffic risks in the shared areas of the work site. This included the landlord agreeing to install barriers to protect pedestrian walkways.

[p15 | prose | 2.2 Static work site example]
The landlord and PCBUs agreed to set up a traffic management committee to write a traffic management plan covering the shared areas. They agreed to common work site rules/expectations for the shared areas.

Using a risk management approach can help you to identify and manage traffic related dangers at a work site.

[p17 | prose | Using a risk management approach can help you to identify and manage traffic related dangers at a work site.]
Risks to health and safety arise from people being exposed to a hazard (a source or cause of harm).

[p17 | prose | Using a risk management approach can help you to identify and manage traffic related dangers at a work site.]
As a PCBU, you must manage risks so that workers and other peoples’ health and safety is not put at risk by the work that you do. Risk management is about identifying hazards and assessing risks, applying control measures to eliminate or minimise those risks, and regularly reviewing control measures.

[p17 | prose | Using a risk management approach can help you to identify and manage traffic related dangers at a work site.]
You must consult with your workers and their representatives at all steps of the risk management process. For more information see Appendix C: Worker engagement and participation

[p17 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
3.1 Identify potential hazards

[p17 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
With your workers, identify potential hazards at your work site.

[p17 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
Potential hazards include those that can cause acute injuries and those that can harm people’s long-term health. The following suggestions can help you identify potential hazards (sources or causes of harm) related to work site traffic:

[p17 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
– Look around your work site. Are there areas where people and vehicles are in close proximity to each other?

[p17 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
– Look at your floor plan or layout of your work site:

[p17 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
- Are there blind spots, areas where sunstrike might occur, tight corners, low light areas, or other areas of poor visibility?

[p17 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
- Look for areas where space is tight, restricting the manoeuvrability of vehicles.

[p17 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
– Look at the driving surfaces at your work site, are there steep slopes or uneven terrain?

[p17 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
– Are there any natural hazards near vehicle operating areas such as bodies or water or steep drop-offs?

[p17 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
– Think about hazards resulting from the weather, or different seasons. For example, excess surface water from rain, ice in winter, strong winds, or fog.

[p17 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
– Is there tall racking or goods stacked high near vehicle operating areas?

[p17 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
– Could vehicles be used outside their limits or capabilities or be operated at potentially unsafe speeds?

[p18 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
– Are there vehicles (including visiting vehicles) that have driver blind spots?

[p18 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
– Are there vehicles (including visiting vehicles) that produce excessive fumes, vibration, or noise?

[p18 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
– Could workers/drivers be affected by drugs, alcohol or fatigue?

[p18 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
– Are there potential worker/driver distractions (such as cell-phones, work pressures, home pressures)?

[p18 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
The above list is just some suggestions of what to consider or look out for. Every work site is different. Looking around your work site and thinking ‘what could go wrong’ may help you to identify more hazards.

[p18 | prose | 3.1 Identify potential hazards]
You should also ask other people who frequently visit your work site about what hazards they may have encountered. Reviewing your incident and injury records (including near misses) may also help identify hazards.

[p18 | prose | 3.2 Assess the risk]
3.2 Assess the risk

[p18 | prose | 3.2 Assess the risk]
With your workers, you will need to carry out a risk assessment for each hazard you have identified. Things you should consider include:

[p18 | prose | Who might be exposed to the hazard?]
Who might be exposed to the hazard?

[p18 | prose | Who might be exposed to the hazard?]
– Think about who is at your work site, for example, workers, contractors, drivers, customers, children, or other visitors.

[p18 | prose | Who might be exposed to the hazard?]
– Does the hazard have the potential to harm many people at once?

[p18 | prose | Who might be exposed to the hazard?]
How could workers or others be harmed? For example:

[p18 | prose | Who might be exposed to the hazard?]
– being hit by a vehicle

[p18 | prose | Who might be exposed to the hazard?]
– being trapped between a vehicle and a structure

[p18 | prose | Who might be exposed to the hazard?]
– vehicles colliding with each other or a structure

[p18 | prose | Who might be exposed to the hazard?]
– falling from a vehicle

[p18 | prose | Who might be exposed to the hazard?]
– being hit by items falling off a vehicle (unsecured or unstable loads)

[p18 | prose | Who might be exposed to the hazard?]
– injured as a result of improper use of a vehicle

[p18 | prose | Who might be exposed to the hazard?]
– exposure to unsafe levels of fumes or excessive noise from vehicles.

[p18 | prose | How severe could the harm be?]
How severe could the harm be?

[p18 | prose | How severe could the harm be?]
Could the hazard result in serious harm or a fatality?

[p18 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?

[p18 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– Are there certain times of the day that are busier than others? For example, store open hours or the beginning and end of shifts.

[p18 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– Are there certain times of the year that are busier than others?

[p18 | prose | How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk?]
– Are there likely to be seasonal variations to the level of risk?

[p18 | prose | How could people’s health be affected?]
How could people’s health be affected?

[p18 | prose | How could people’s health be affected?]
Could the hazard cause short or long term health problems for the affected person/people?

[p18 | prose | How likely are these consequences?]
How likely are these consequences?

[p18 | prose | How likely are these consequences?]
What is the likelihood of somebody being harmed by the hazard?

[p18 | prose | How likely are these consequences?]
Decide which risks to deal with first. For example, risks with potentially significant consequences such as serious injury or death, chronic ill-health, or those with a high chance of happening should be prioritised.

[p19 | prose | 3.3 Manage the risk]
3.3 Manage the risk

[p19 | prose | 3.3 Manage the risk]
You must take all reasonably practicable actions to eliminate or minimise the risks you and your workers have identified.

[p19 | prose | 3.3 Manage the risk]
The ways of controlling risks can be ranked from the highest level of protection and reliability to the lowest. This ranking is known as the hierarchy of control measures. Using the hierarchy of control measures to manage risks will help you to make sure you are using the most effective control measures first.

3.4 Review control measures

[p19 | prose | 3.4 Review control measures]
Check your control measures regularly to make sure they are working as planned. Control measures need to be regularly reviewed in consultation with your workers or their representatives, to make sure they remain effective. For more information, see Appendix C: Worker engagement and participation

[p19 | prose | 3.4 Review control measures]
If your work site or work activities change, you need to check that your existing control measures are still the most appropriate ones to use.

[p20 | prose | 3.4 Review control measures]

A well planned and designed work site can reduce the risks to people working near work site traffic.

This section provides examples of ways you can control work site traffic risks from a site design or layout perspective. This may be particularly useful when planning or setting up a new work site, but many control measures can also be added to existing work sites as well (such as adding barriers and signage). As a PCBU, you will need to assess your individual situation to decide what control measures will be the most effective, and reasonably practicable for your situation. This may mean adopting a combination of control measures to manage the risk. When planning a new work site, consideration also needs to be given to any resource consent or building consent requirements, including any traffic management requirements.

[p22 | prose | 4.1 Keep pedestrians and vehicles apart]
4.1 Keep pedestrians and vehicles apart

[p22 | prose | 4.1 Keep pedestrians and vehicles apart]
The main aim of any work site design should be the separation of vehicles and pedestrians as much as possible. The most effective way of making sure pedestrians and vehicles can move safely around a work site is to provide separate pedestrian and vehicle traffic routes. You could eliminate just about all interaction by using overhead walkways (see Figure 3).

[p23 | prose | 4.1 Keep pedestrians and vehicles apart]
However, historical work site layouts, lack of space, or other constraints, may mean it is not reasonably practicable to install overhead walkways. If it is not reasonably practicable to install overhead walkways to keep pedestrians separated from vehicles, you should consider the following control measures:

[p26 | prose | FIGURE 9:]

[p26 | prose | FIGURE 9:]
For static work sites, barriers and guard rails should be suitable for what they are providing protection from. High impact barriers could be used to stop vehicles from entering pedestrian walkways. Low impact barriers could be used to keep pedestrians within pedestrian routes.

[p26 | prose | FIGURE 9:]
When planning pedestrian routes, create paths pedestrians would naturally follow so they will be more likely to stay on the path and not take shortcuts.

[p26 | prose | FIGURE 9:]
If possible, schedule work so vehicles and pedestrians are not in the same area at the same time. For example, only accepting deliveries during certain hours, when there are no pedestrians about.

[p26 | prose | 4.2 Have designated pedestrian crossings]
4.2 Have designated pedestrian crossings

[p26 | prose | 4.2 Have designated pedestrian crossings]
Where pedestrian and vehicle traffic routes cross, there should be clear ground markings, lights and signs to show that it is a crossing area. Typically vehicles will have the right of way unless the pedestrian is already on the crossing when a vehicle approaches.

[p26 | prose | 4.2 Have designated pedestrian crossings]
Pedestrians should not cross unless all vehicles are well clear of the crossing, or the vehicle has stopped and the pedestrian and driver have positively acknowledged each other’s presence and made confirmed eye contact. Any vehicle that has stopped at a pedestrian crossing should wait until the pedestrian is well clear of the crossing before moving again.

[p26 | prose | 4.2 Have designated pedestrian crossings]
Consider control measures to stop pedestrians from accidentally stepping out into traffic areas without stopping and checking first (see Figure 10).

Check there are no slip or trip hazards, especially near pedestrian crossing areas.

[p28 | prose | 4.3 Plan vehicle routes]
4.3 Plan vehicle routes

[p28 | prose | 4.3 Plan vehicle routes]
Ideally work sites should have a one-way system with separate entry and exit points. This removes the need for vehicles to reverse.

[p28 | prose | 4.3 Plan vehicle routes]
Where it is not possible to have a one-way system, traffic lanes should be clearly marked:

[p28 | prose | 4.3 Plan vehicle routes]
– make allowances for stopping distances

[p28 | prose | 4.3 Plan vehicle routes]
– traffic lanes should be wide enough for stationary vehicles to be passed safely and overhead clearance high enough for the largest vehicle that may use them

[p28 | prose | 4.3 Plan vehicle routes]
– create designated turning areas that are wide enough for vehicles to reverse/ turn without entering pedestrian or loading/unloading areas. See Section 5.2 Reversing vehicles

[p28 | prose | 4.4 Keep vehicle routes clear of known hazard areas]
4.4 Keep vehicle routes clear of known hazard areas

[p28 | prose | 4.4 Keep vehicle routes clear of known hazard areas]
If vehicle routes come close to building columns, pipework, storage racking or cables, install barriers to protect them from vehicle impacts.

[p28 | prose | 4.5 Avoid vehicle routes that cross public parking and pedestrian areas]
4.5 Avoid vehicle routes that cross public parking and pedestrian areas

[p28 | prose | 4.5 Avoid vehicle routes that cross public parking and pedestrian areas]
Where relevant, work site traffic should have separate entry and exit points from the general public. When planning or setting up new work sites, avoid creating vehicle routes that require delivery vehicles to travel through public car parks to access loading/unloading bays.

[p28 | prose | 4.5 Avoid vehicle routes that cross public parking and pedestrian areas]
Where historical layouts require drivers to navigate public car parks or park on public roads to unload, the additional risks this creates should be clearly addressed in a work site traffic management plan. Control measures may include:

[p28 | prose | 4.5 Avoid vehicle routes that cross public parking and pedestrian areas]
– only accepting smaller delivery vehicles at that work site

[p28 | prose | 4.5 Avoid vehicle routes that cross public parking and pedestrian areas]
– staggering delivery times so only one vehicle is present at a time

[p28 | prose | 4.5 Avoid vehicle routes that cross public parking and pedestrian areas]
– setting delivery hours outside of shop hours

[p28 | prose | 4.5 Avoid vehicle routes that cross public parking and pedestrian areas]
– using a spotter to help guide the driver.

[p28 | prose | Temporary traffic routes]
Temporary traffic routes

[p28 | prose | Temporary traffic routes]
Dynamic work sites, for example construction and forestry sites, often have routes for vehicles and pedestrians that change as work progresses. Where possible, these routes should follow the same basic standards as for the static traffic routes listed above. [p42 | prose | 6.5 Consider technological safety controls]
Elimination and engineering control measures are considered the most effective control measures. However, technological safety control measures can also contribute to making a work site safer by helping drivers avoid incidents. For example:

[p42 | prose | 6.5 Consider technological safety controls]

[p43 | prose | 6.5 Consider technological safety controls]

[p45 | prose | 7.1 Use competent drivers]
– has had sufficient training and supervision on the operation of the vehicle. This includes:

[p45 | prose | 7.1 Use competent drivers]
the practical mechanics of operating the vehicle, and

[p45 | prose | 7.1 Use competent drivers]
the knowledge of how to operate it in a safe way for themselves, the environment and the people around them

[p45 | prose | 7.1 Use competent drivers]
– has the required licences and certifications to safely operate the vehicle

[p45 | prose | 7.1 Use competent drivers]
– has the right level of fitness and general abilities to operate the vehicle

[p46 | prose | 7.2 Provide training and certifications]
7.2 Provide training and certifications

[p46 | prose | 7.2 Provide training and certifications]
Training requirements will depend on the worker’s experience and the training they have previously received. Consider these factors when deciding the level and amount of training a worker needs. When considering training needs for a worker, you should not only consider what vehicle type training they have received, but also if they may need training for working safely in certain environments. For example, a worker may have training and experience in operating a digger at a work site where the digger is the only vehicle present, but have no experience or training on operating a digger on a busy construction site with many other vehicles present. Make sure all workers are competent before starting unsupervised work. They should be monitored on-site to establish their actual level of competence and any extra training needs (regardless of what licences or certifications they may hold). Existing workers should continue to have training, such as: - when the work changes and the task needs to be done differently - when new vehicles or new features are introduced - refresher training (when the need is identified). Training should be provided by a competent person. Simulator training (if available) should also be considered.

[p46 | prose | 7.3 Keep evidence of training and certifications]
7.3 Keep evidence of training and certifications

[p46 | prose | 7.3 Keep evidence of training and certifications]
For each driver, keep records of all completed training and certifications and licences that they hold. Records should also include when refresher training or recertification is due. This will help make sure the right worker is allocated a particular task and identify workers who need refresher training.

[p46 | prose | 7.4 Provide personal protective equipment (PPE)]
7.4 Provide personal protective equipment (PPE)

[p46 | prose | 7.4 Provide personal protective equipment (PPE)]
PPE is generally considered the last resort when protecting workers from harm. PPE should be used if there is still risk remaining after all other reasonably practicable control measures have been put in place. On a work site with traffic risks, this is most likely going to mean the use of hi-visibility clothing (for example, reflective vests), sturdy footwear, and possibly personal proximity warning devices. Beware of the risks that some PPE may create when working in and around vehicle traffic. For example, hearing protection may limit a person’s ability to hear approaching vehicles, or tinted eye wear may limit the ability for drivers to make confirmed eye contact with pedestrians. You may need to consider alternative control measures to manage these risks.

[p47 | prose | 7.5 Keep visitors safe]
7.5 Keep visitors safe

[p47 | prose | 7.5 Keep visitors safe]
You should have specific car parks set aside for visitors. Upon arrival visitors should report to the reception area, site office, or site manager. They should be given information on the safety procedures and expectations for the work site. A pre-prepared visitor work site induction is useful for this. For more information, see Section 8: Work site induction and traffic management plans

[p47 | prose | 7.5 Keep visitors safe]
This may include providing visitors with PPE.

[p47 | prose | 7.6 Keep customers safe near work site traffic]
7.6 Keep customers safe near work site traffic

[p47 | prose | 7.6 Keep customers safe near work site traffic]
PCBUs must make sure that the health and safety of other people is not put at risk from their work or from anything else at the work site (so far as is reasonably practicable). Customers are considered ‘other people at a workplace’. In a retail situation, this means doing what is reasonably practicable to keep your customers safe when they are at your business.

[p47 | prose | 7.6 Keep customers safe near work site traffic]
Where work site vehicles are in operation near customers, you should consider:

[p47 | prose | 7.6 Keep customers safe near work site traffic]
– creating a safe zone for customers

[p47 | prose | 7.6 Keep customers safe near work site traffic]
this can be done with barriers, signage and a store layout that keeps customers away from high traffic areas unless they have a specific purpose to be there

[p47 | prose | 7.6 Keep customers safe near work site traffic]
– installing clear signs and markings that show where customers should or should not park vehicles when they arrive at your work site

[p47 | prose | 7.6 Keep customers safe near work site traffic]
– where relevant, a policy of asking customers to stay in their vehicles until loading or unloading is complete

[p47 | prose | 7.6 Keep customers safe near work site traffic]
– making sure children are not permitted into high traffic areas. For example, in a building supplies yard, children should always remain inside vehicles.

[p47 | prose | 7.6 Keep customers safe near work site traffic]
Provide training for your workers to help them communicate with customers about where they need to be when vehicles are operating on site.

[p47 | prose | 7.7 Promote work health and safety]
7.7 Promote work health and safety

[p47 | prose | 7.7 Promote work health and safety]
You should promote a work environment that prioritises health and safety and worker well-being. This can help boost worker morale and encourage workers to follow safe work practices. This will reduce the likelihood of workers taking shortcuts and putting themselves and others at risk.

[p47 | prose | 7.7 Promote work health and safety]
Some steps towards creating health and safety-focused work environment include:

[p47 | prose | 7.7 Promote work health and safety]
– getting workers, contractors and their representatives actively involved in decision-making around health and safety. For more information see Appendix C: Worker engagement and participation

[p47 | prose | 7.7 Promote work health and safety]
– encouraging workers to look out for each other as well as themselves

[p47 | prose | 7.7 Promote work health and safety]
– promoting open and honest communication between workers and management – encouraging everyone to report incidents, hazards and near-misses

[p47 | prose | 7.7 Promote work health and safety]
– making sure incidents and hazards are investigated, and improvements are made where the opportunity or need is identified.

[p49 | prose | Creating work site inductions and traffic management plans in consultation with other PCBUs and your workers is a useful way to manage shared risks.]
Creating work site inductions and traffic management plans in consultation with other PCBUs and your workers is a useful way to manage shared risks.

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
8.1 Work site inductions

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
Work site inductions are one way to make sure new workers and visitors entering a work site know and understand the requirements and expectations for that work site.

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
Work site inductions are usually overseen by the site manager or a designated representative.

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
Work site inductions should include site-specific requirements for:

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– entering and exiting the work site

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– speed limits

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– work site layout – including:

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
- vehicle and pedestrian routes

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
- vehicle parking

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
- delivery areas

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– schedules for loading and unloading

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– safe practices for loading and unloading

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– safe vehicle turning and reversing practices

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– locations of facilities, including first aid

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– emergency procedures/evacuation procedures/location of assembly points (which should be located away from traffic areas)

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– a list of known hazards at the work site and the control measures for managing the risks they create

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– how to report an incident or hazard

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– PPE

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– safety documents, policies and plans specific to the work site such as a traffic management plan.

[p49 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
Work site requirements that follow industry-wide standards and conventions can make it easier for workers (such as delivery drivers) who visit many work sites in a day to remember and follow work site requirements. For example, use the same colour codes for loading zones or pedestrian zones.

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
When writing a work site induction document, workers and other PCBUs that regularly visit the work site should be consulted. You should also aim to:

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– keep it as brief as possible

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– use familiar, straightforward language

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– use drawings, diagrams and pictograms where possible (pictures can sometimes convey the message much more effectively than words)

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– consider the range of literacy, numeracy, and language skills of workers that may need to read and understand it

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– consider the range of skills, knowledge and experience of new workers and visitors to the work site (avoid using jargon or acronyms without explaining what they mean)

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– consider providing your work site induction documents in other languages

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– consider including a work site walkthrough (keeping to safe areas) to show the work site layout (rather than relying on people translating maps and diagrams).

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
All PCBUs on site should be fully aware of their roles and responsibilities before any activity is started. A work site induction should be completed for every new worker or visitor to a work site.

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
In addition, the site manager (or designated representative) should:

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– review the induction document regularly. Reviews should include considering any feedback from workers

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– monitor all workers on site to make sure they are working safely and following the work site requirements

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– investigate incidents and near misses to identify their causes and what needs to change to stop them from happening again

[p50 | prose | 8.1 Work site inductions]
– consider how to check for understanding of the information. This may as simple as asking some open-ended questions about key points from the induction information.

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
8.2 Traffic management plans

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
Work sites that have a lot of traffic activity or pedestrian interaction should have a traffic management plan. Traffic management plans help communicate how traffic risks will be managed in greater detail.

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
A traffic management plan should include details of:

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
– the desired flow of pedestrian and vehicle movements

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
– rules for when and how drivers are to give way or stop for pedestrians or other vehicles

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
– the expected frequency of interaction between vehicles and pedestrians

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
– diagrams of the layout of barriers, speed bumps, speed limits, walkways, signs and general arrangements to warn and guide traffic around, past, or through a work site

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
– how short term, mobile work and complex traffic situations will be managed

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
– any restrictions or considerations due to other regulatory requirements (such as noise restrictions or traffic volume restrictions).

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
A traffic management plan could also include details of:

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
– the roles and responsibilities of workers managing traffic at the work site

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
– the roles and responsibilities of workers who work in or near vehicles at the work site

[p50 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
– the procedures for controlling traffic during an emergency.

[p51 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
Workers should be familiar with the traffic management plan. They should be provided information, instruction and training on how to apply the traffic management plan when working as part of their overall induction.

[p51 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
Traffic management plans should be regularly monitored and reviewed to make sure they remain effective and take into consideration any changes at the work site. They should always be reviewed after an incident (including near-misses). Any changes to a traffic management plan should be communicated to workers as soon as possible.

[p51 | prose | 8.2 Traffic management plans]
A traffic management consultant (such as a traffic planner or traffic engineer) may be able to provide you with advice for creating a traffic management plan that suits the specific needs of your work site, vehicles, and work site activity.

[p51 | prose | 8.3 Traffic management committees]
8.3 Traffic management committees

[p51 | prose | 8.3 Traffic management committees]
Work sites with more than one PCBU working on site should consider having a traffic management committee. Traffic management committees should be made up of representatives from all PCBUs involved at the work site (where possible), Health and Safety Representatives, driver representatives, supervisors and any other group sharing the traffic routes and vehicle working areas. Participating in traffic management committees is one way PCBUs can work together to manage their overlapping duties for work site traffic risks.

[p51 | prose | 8.3 Traffic management committees]
Traffic management committees can carry out the monitoring and review of the traffic management plan.

Workers also have a duty to look after their own health and safety and that of anyone who might be affected by their work. |

[p58 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
What does reasonably practicable mean?

[p58 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
Health and safety duties need to be managed so far as is reasonably practicable. There are two parts to ‘reasonably practicable’. First consider what is possible in your circumstances to ensure health and safety. Then consider, of these possible actions, what is reasonable to do in your circumstances.

[p58 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
When deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’, consider:

[p58 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
– How likely is the risk and how severe is the illness or injury that might result?

[p58 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
– What do you know, or what you ought reasonably to know, about the hazard or risk and the ways of eliminating or minimising the risk?

[p58 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
– What is the availability of the control measures, and how suitable are they for the specific risk?

[p58 | prose | What does reasonably practicable mean?]
– As a final step, what are the costs of the control measure and are the costs grossly disproportionate to the risk?

[p59 | appendix | Appendix C: Worker engagement, participation and representation]
Appendix C: Worker engagement, participation and representation

[p59 | appendix | Appendix C: Worker engagement, participation and representation]
PCBUs have two duties under HSWA related to worker engagement and participation:

[p59 | appendix | Appendix C: Worker engagement, participation and representation]
– to engage with workers on health and safety matters that affect or are likely to affect them, so far as is reasonably practicable; and

[p59 | appendix | Appendix C: Worker engagement, participation and representation]
– to have practices that give workers reasonable opportunities to participate effectively in the ongoing improvement of work health and safety.

[p59 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working around vehicles]
Engage with your workers about the risks of working around vehicles

[p59 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working around vehicles]
You must engage and consult with your workers on work health and safety matters, so far as is reasonably practicable. This includes when identifying, controlling and monitoring risks related to working near vehicles, as outlined in this guide.

[p59 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working around vehicles]
You can engage with workers by:

[p59 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working around vehicles]
– sharing information about the dangers of working around vehicles so that workers are well-informed, know what is going on and can contribute to decision-making

[p59 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working around vehicles]
– giving workers reasonable opportunities to have a say about health and safety matters

[p59 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working around vehicles]
– listening to and considering what workers have to say at each step of the risk management process for working around vehicles

[p59 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working around vehicles]
– considering workers’ views when decisions are being made

[p59 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working around vehicles]
– updating workers about what decisions have been made.

[p59 | prose | Engage with your workers about the risks of working around vehicles]
If workers are represented by a Health and Safety Representative (HSR), engagement must also involve that representative.
