[p13 | prose | 1.5 PCBU duties]
1.5 PCBU duties

[p13 | prose | 1.5 PCBU duties]
Under HSWA, you have a duty to ensure the health and safety of your workers and other people, as far as is reasonably practicable.

[p13 | prose | 1.5 PCBU duties]
This duty covers:

[p13 | prose | 1.5 PCBU duties]
– any workers that are influenced by your business (for example, your workers, contractors, subcontractors, and apprentices)

[p13 | prose | 1.5 PCBU duties]
– other people that could be put at risk by your work (for example, tenants, visitors, customers, and passers-by).

[p13 | prose | 1.5 PCBU duties]
Under the Asbestos Regulations, you must:

[p13 | prose | 1.5 PCBU duties]
– make sure the risk of people being exposed to airborne asbestos at the workplace is eliminated, so far as is reasonably practicable, or

[p13 | prose | 1.5 PCBU duties]
– if it not reasonably practicable to eliminate the risk, then minimise the risk so far as is reasonably practicable.

[p13 | prose | 1.5 PCBU duties]
You must also make sure that the airborne contamination standard for asbestos is not exceeded at the workplace. The airborne contamination standard for asbestos is 0.1 fibres per millilitre of air over an eight-hour period. This means that for asbestos fibres in the air, the average concentration must not go above 0.1 fibres per millilitre of air.

[p13 | prose | 1.5 PCBU duties]
The airborne contamination standard for asbestos is a control limit for the workplace. It does not set an acceptable limit for personal exposure. If asbestos is detected at a concentration above the airborne contamination standard, you must take steps to eliminate the risk of exposure to asbestos fibres. If it is not reasonably practicable to eliminate the risk, you must take steps to minimise the risk of exposure to asbestos fibres.

[p13 | prose | 1.6 Risk management]
1.6 Risk management

[p13 | prose | 1.6 Risk management]
You must manage risks so that the health and safety of workers and other people is not put at risk by the work you do.

[p13 | prose | 1.6 Risk management]
Risk management is about:

[p13 | prose | 1.6 Risk management]
– identifying hazards and assessing risks

[p13 | prose | 1.6 Risk management]
– applying control measures to eliminate or minimise risks

[p13 | prose | 1.6 Risk management]
– regularly reviewing control measures.

[p13 | prose | 1.6 Risk management]
You can read more about the Health and Safety at Work (General Risk and Workplace Management) Regulations 2016 on the WorkSafe website General risk and workplace management

[p13 | prose | 1.6 Risk management]
You must engage with your workers and their representatives, so far as is reasonably practicable, at all steps of the risk management process.

[p13 | prose | 1.6 Risk management]
You can read more about worker engagement, participation, and representation on the WorkSafe website Good practice for worker engagement, participation and representation

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
When more than one PCBU has the same health and safety duty in an asbestos matter, all PCBUs involved must, so far as is reasonably practicable:

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– consult each other

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– cooperate with each other

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– coordinate their activities.

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
If there is more than one PCBU involved with managing asbestos risks in a building or workplace, all PCBUs should:

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– discuss what work activities are being, or going to be, carried out

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– agree on the degree of influence and control each PCBU has

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– agree on who will manage what and how it will be managed

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– agree on the use of shared facilities, if applicable

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– monitor and check how things are going on an ongoing basis.

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
Examples of where PCBU duties are likely to overlap include:

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– when a body corporate employs a property manager

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– when a residential landlord employs a property manager

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– when a commercial business leases a unit from a property owner.

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
Each business’s responsibility to carry out their duties will most likely be different. This will depend on the ability of the business to influence and control the health and safety matter.

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
The extent of the duty to manage asbestos risks depends on the influence and control of each PCBU. The more influence and control a PCBU has over a building, workplace, work activities, or workers, the more responsibility it is likely to have.

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
You must manage any overlaps and risks that are appropriate for you to control. The nature of that control will reflect how much influence and control you have, and what is reasonably practicable in the circumstances. Any arrangements you make with other PCBUs to manage risks (for example, arrangements to monitor control measures) should be sensible and proportionate.

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
You cannot contract out of your duties, but you can make reasonable agreements with other PCBUs to meet your duties. Each PCBU is responsible for making sure their own duties are met.

[p14 | prose | 1.7 Overlapping PCBU duties]
You can read more about overlapping PCBU duties on the WorkSafe website Overlapping duties – quick guide

[p15 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Example scenario: Overlapping duties

[p15 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Hemi is a landlord of a residential property that was built in the late 1980s. He rents the property out to the Thompson family. The Thompsons contact Hemi to let him know that there is minor damage to the dividing wall between the living room and kitchen that needs to be repaired.

[p15 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Hemi checks the building report for the property for information about the damaged wall. He discovers that the wall is made from asbestos cement board.

[p15 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
From maintenance work he has had done on his own home, Hemi knows that asbestos materials are generally safe if they are in good condition and are not disturbed or damaged. However, he is also aware that maintenance or refurbishment work could lead to the release of asbestos fibres, which could expose the Thompson family and tradespeople working in the house to harm.

[p15 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Hemi decides to contact an asbestos surveyor for advice. The asbestos surveyor assesses the damage and recommends a local contractor that is experienced in repairs on asbestos materials to carry out the work.

[p15 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
As work on Hemi’s property is being planned, he prepares an asbestos management plan with the help of the contractor, Liv, before the work starts. He makes sure that the asbestos management plan contains details about the planned work and includes photographs of the damaged wall.

[p15 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Liv carries out a risk assessment on the damaged asbestos material. She finds that the work can be completed as a minor repair.

[p15 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Before the repair starts, Hemi, Liv, and the Thompson family have a meeting. Hemi tells the Thompsons about the presence of asbestos in the wall and explains that all necessary precautions will be taken during the repair. Liv explains the control measures that will be in place to minimise the release of asbestos fibres into the air.

[p15 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
The Thompsons decide that they will stay with friends while the work is being done.

[p15 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Liv’s work plan for the repair includes using a shadow vacuum technique to capture any released asbestos fibres, wearing appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE, including respiratory protective equipment), and sealing off the area to prevent any potential spread of asbestos fibres to other parts of the house.

[p15 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Throughout the process, Hemi keeps in regular contact with Liv, making sure that health and safety procedures are followed closely and that the work is going to plan. Once the repair is complete, Hemi checks the quality of the work and takes some more photos to include in his asbestos management plan, which he updates.

[p16 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Example scenario: Overlapping duties

[p16 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
David owns a busy retail plaza in the heart of the city. The plaza houses a variety of shops, including a popular café run by Chiara and her family. David has hired Selphie Properties, a property management company, to oversee the maintenance of the building. Ingrid, an experienced property manager from Selphie Properties, has been managing the retail plaza for the past three years.

[p16 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
One day, Chiara calls Ingrid to report damage to a wall in the café caused by some rowdy customers. Chiara remembers that some work had been done in that area of the building several years ago, which required the café to close temporarily.

[p16 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Ingrid immediately contacts David, the building owner, to discuss the issue. Remembering the previous work in the building, David authorises Ingrid to hire an asbestos surveyor to inspect the damaged area. The asbestos survey confirmed that the damaged wall had been constructed to enclose asbestos material, and that the asbestos material behind the wall had been disturbed by the incident.

[p16 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Following the advice of the asbestos surveyor, Ingrid hires a licensed asbestos removalist to safely remove the asbestos material and repair the enclosing wall, and a licensed asbestos assessor to inspect the work. She takes care to check that removalists and assessors are from different businesses so can work independently.

[p16 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
The licensed asbestos removalist that Ingrid hired prepares an asbestos removal control plan outlining the procedures for safely removing the asbestos and repairing the wall.

[p16 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Before work starts, Ingrid organises a meeting with David, Chiara, and the asbestos removalist to establish each business’s health and safety duties. They decide the following:

[p16 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
– The asbestos removalist has the most influence and control over the workplace while the asbestos removal work is being carried out, so they will be responsible for managing risks to workers and the public created by the asbestos removal work.

[p16 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
– David and Ingrid have little control over the work or work site but do have control over the area around the work site, including any risks from the other businesses working in the same area. They will be responsible for managing those risks (for example, by communicating with others about the asbestos removal work).

[p16 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
– Chiara has little control over the work or work site but does have influence over her workers. She will be responsible for making sure her workers are informed about the asbestos removal work, and stay away from the café while the asbestos removal work is being carried out.

[p16 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Ingrid and David make sure the businesses in the retail plaza are told about the planned work and the steps that will be taken to manage the asbestos material. Chiara’s café is temporarily closed to keep staff and customers safe while the work is carried out.

[p16 | prose | Example scenario: Overlapping duties]
Ingrid coordinates the repair work, and David makes regular visits to the building to check the progress. After the asbestos material is removed and the wall repaired, the licensed asbestos assessor checks that the area is safe to re-enter and provides Ingrid with a clearance certificate. Ingrid and David work with Chiara to update the asbestos management plan before Chiara’s café reopens.

1.8 Managing asbestos risks across a property portfolio

[p17 | prose | 1.8 Managing asbestos risks across a property portfolio]
Managing asbestos risks across a property portfolio can be challenging. Each property may:

[p17 | prose | 1.8 Managing asbestos risks across a property portfolio]
– be in a different condition or state of repair

[p17 | prose | 1.8 Managing asbestos risks across a property portfolio]
– have a different budget

[p17 | prose | 1.8 Managing asbestos risks across a property portfolio]
– be occupied by a different PCBU

[p17 | prose | 1.8 Managing asbestos risks across a property portfolio]

[p17 | prose | 1.8 Managing asbestos risks across a property portfolio]

If you own or manage a portfolio of properties, you will need to consider how you manage asbestos risks for each property. [p17 | prose | 1.8 Managing asbestos risks across a property portfolio]
– how you will monitor identified asbestos material for signs of deterioration

[p17 | prose | 1.8 Managing asbestos risks across a property portfolio]
– how you will communicate and work with other PCBUs involved with the management of asbestos in your properties (for example, commercial tenants and asbestos contractors)

[p17 | prose | 1.8 Managing asbestos risks across a property portfolio]

[p17 | prose | 1.8 Managing asbestos risks across a property portfolio]
– how you will make sure that your asbestos records are easily accessible when required.

[p21 | prose | 2.1 What is asbestos?]
2.1 What is asbestos?

[p21 | prose | 2.1 What is asbestos?]
Asbestos is a heat-resistant, fire-resistant, and insulating mineral that was commonly used in building materials.

[p21 | prose | 2.1 What is asbestos?]
Asbestos is made up of tiny fibres. When asbestos is disturbed or breaks down, asbestos fibres can be released into the air.

[p21 | prose | 2.1 What is asbestos?]
All forms of asbestos are harmful to humans.

[p21 | prose | 2.2 Why is asbestos still a problem in New Zealand?]
2.2 Why is asbestos still a problem in New Zealand?

[p21 | prose | 2.2 Why is asbestos still a problem in New Zealand?]
Even though asbestos has been banned, it still causes problems today. This is because many buildings built before 1 January 2000 still have asbestos material in them.

[p21 | prose | 2.2 Why is asbestos still a problem in New Zealand?]
As these buildings get older, they need to be repaired, renovated, or in some cases, demolished. These activities can disturb asbestos, creating a health risk for workers and other people in the workplace.

[p21 | prose | 2.2 Why is asbestos still a problem in New Zealand?]
For buildings built after 1 January 2000, the risk of asbestos material being present is lower.

[p21 | prose | 2.3 What problems can asbestos cause?]
2.3 What problems can asbestos cause?

[p21 | prose | 2.3 What problems can asbestos cause?]
Asbestos is not dangerous if it is in good condition, and is left alone and managed correctly. If asbestos material is damaged or disturbed, it can release fibres into the air.

[p21 | prose | 2.3 What problems can asbestos cause?]
When asbestos fibres are released into the air, they can be breathed in easily. Breathing in asbestos fibres can cause them to get trapped in the lungs, leading to serious health issues.

[p21 | prose | 2.3 What problems can asbestos cause?]
Asbestos fibres in the lungs cause several diseases, including:

[p21 | prose | 2.3 What problems can asbestos cause?]
– cancers (for example, lung cancer and mesothelioma)

[p21 | prose | 2.3 What problems can asbestos cause?]
– serious long-term lung conditions (for example, asbestosis).

[p21 | prose | 2.3 What problems can asbestos cause?]
Diseases caused by asbestos often cannot be cured. They can cause severe symptoms and can be life-threatening.

[p21 | prose | 2.3 What problems can asbestos cause?]
You can read more about the health problems caused by asbestos on the WorkSafe website Asbestos in Aotearoa New Zealand

[p21 | prose | 2.4 Categories of asbestos]
2.4 Categories of asbestos

[p21 | prose | 2.4 Categories of asbestos]
Asbestos can be classified into two categories based on its condition:

[p21 | prose | 2.4 Categories of asbestos]
– Friable asbestos is flaky or powdery. It can be crumbled or reduced to a powder without much pressure. Friable asbestos can easily release fibres into the air if it is disturbed. Friable asbestos is often called Class A asbestos.

[p21 | prose | 2.4 Categories of asbestos]
– Non-friable asbestos usually has asbestos fibres bonded into another material such as cement or vinyl. Non-friable asbestos is less likely to release asbestos fibres into the air unless it is disturbed or has started to deteriorate. Non-friable asbestos is often called Class B asbestos.

[p21 | prose | 2.4 Categories of asbestos]
The risk of harm from asbestos depends on its condition and how easily fibres are released into the air. Asbestos fibres are more likely to be released into the air if asbestos materials are:

[p21 | prose | 2.4 Categories of asbestos]
– friable (flaky, powdery, or easy to crumble)

[p21 | prose | 2.4 Categories of asbestos]
– in poor condition (for example, if they are flaking, peeling, or crumbling)

[p21 | prose | 2.4 Categories of asbestos]
– disturbed in any way (for example, sanded, drilled, cut, or water blasted).

[p21 | prose | 2.4 Categories of asbestos]
Airborne asbestos fibres are often too small to be seen with the naked eye. Even if you cannot see any dust particles in the air, asbestos fibres may still be present.

[p21 | prose | 2.4 Categories of asbestos]
Non-friable (or bonded) asbestos materials generally do not release asbestos fibres if they are in good condition and are not disturbed.

[p27 | prose | 3.3 Contractor independence]
3.3 Contractor independence

[p27 | prose | 3.3 Contractor independence]
To be independent, an asbestos assessor should have no conflict of interest when they carry out their assessor work.

[p27 | prose | 3.3 Contractor independence]
This means that the assessor should not be from the same business as the removalist for that specific job. Being from the same business could create a conflict of interest, as the assessor could be influenced by their connection to the company doing the asbestos removal work. This could lead to biased reporting or inadequate inspections that put health or safety at risk.

[p27 | prose | 3.3 Contractor independence]
Making sure asbestos contractors work independently helps to make sure that your asbestos identification and management process is carried out to the highest standard. You should encourage open communication between any contractors you hire to help you manage asbestos material to avoid any conflicts of interest.

[p39 | prose | 5.12 Sample analysis]
5.12 Sample analysis

[p39 | prose | 5.12 Sample analysis]
It is very difficult to tell the difference between materials that contain asbestos and materials that do not. The only way to confirm that something is asbestos is to test a sample of it.

[p39 | prose | 5.12 Sample analysis]
If the suspected asbestos material is stable, non-friable and will not be disturbed, it may be more practicable to assume it is asbestos, rather than test it.

6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
When you have identified the asbestos in your building or workplace, you will need to make a plan to assess the risks and manage them.

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
To assess the risk from asbestos material in your building or workplace, think about:

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
– who might be exposed to the asbestos material

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
– how often the asbestos material is likely to cause a risk (for example, whether the asbestos material is in an area that is used often or an area that is sealed off)

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
– what the possible consequences of asbestos exposure are

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
– how likely are the consequences of exposure to the asbestos material.

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
Involve your workers and their representatives in the risk assessment process. They will have operational day-to-day knowledge that will help you make sure that risks are fully assessed.

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
Keep in mind that it can be easy to underestimate how likely a risk is, or how severe its consequences might be if it happens.

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
In your assessment, think about:

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
– the condition of the asbestos material

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
– whether the asbestos material is likely to be damaged or disturbed

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
– whether the asbestos material is likely to deteriorate

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
– the quantity of airborne asbestos fibres that could be released

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
– whether the asbestos material is in an area where workers or other people could be exposed to it

[p41 | prose | 6.2 Assess the risk of asbestos exposure]
– potential routes for exposure (for example, through vents or ducts).

[p42 | prose | Remember]
Remember

[p42 | prose | Remember]
If asbestos material is in good condition and has not been disturbed, it may be unlikely to release fibres into the air. It may be safer to leave it alone and review its condition over time.

[p42 | prose | Remember]
There is a higher risk that asbestos fibres could be released into the air if:

[p42 | prose | Remember]
– the asbestos material is in poor condition

[p42 | prose | Remember]
– the asbestos material has been disturbed

[p42 | prose | Remember]
– if there is asbestos contaminated dust present (because this may be disturbed later).

[p42 | prose | 6.3 What is an asbestos management plan?]
6.3 What is an asbestos management plan?

[p42 | prose | 6.3 What is an asbestos management plan?]
An asbestos management plan is a document that sets out where any identified asbestos material is present and how it will be managed.

[p42 | prose | 6.3 What is an asbestos management plan?]
You must have an asbestos management plan in place for:

[p42 | prose | 6.3 What is an asbestos management plan?]
– any structure that has (or is likely to have) asbestos material

[p42 | prose | 6.3 What is an asbestos management plan?]
– any identified asbestos material in soil at your building or workplace

[p42 | prose | 6.3 What is an asbestos management plan?]
– any naturally occurring asbestos material at your building or workplace

[p42 | prose | 6.3 What is an asbestos management plan?]
– any asbestos material that is likely to be at your building or workplace from time to time.

[p42 | prose | 6.3 What is an asbestos management plan?]
Your asbestos management plan can help you to prioritise and manage asbestos risks if people at your building or workplace could be exposed to asbestos fibres.

[p42 | prose | 6.4 What information is in an asbestos management plan?]
6.4 What information is in an asbestos management plan?

[p42 | prose | 6.4 What information is in an asbestos management plan?]

[p42 | prose | 6.4 What information is in an asbestos management plan?]

[p42 | prose | 6.4 What information is in an asbestos management plan?]

[p42 | prose | 6.4 What information is in an asbestos management plan?]

You may also include any other information that describes how you plan to manage asbestos material in your building or workplace. This might include:

[p42 | prose | 6.4 What information is in an asbestos management plan?]

[p42 | prose | 6.4 What information is in an asbestos management plan?]

[p42 | prose | 6.4 What information is in an asbestos management plan?]

[p42 | prose | 6.4 What information is in an asbestos management plan?]

7.0 Manage asbestos material

[p48 | prose | 7.1 Introduction]
7.1 Introduction

[p48 | prose | 7.1 Introduction]
When you have assessed the risks from the asbestos material you have identified or assumed to be present, you will need to manage them. Managing the asbestos material in your building or workplace is the fourth step of the asbestos identification and management process (Figure 11).

[p48 | prose | 7.2 Hierarchy of control measures]
7.2 Hierarchy of control measures

[p48 | prose | 7.2 Hierarchy of control measures]
The ways of managing risks are ranked from the highest level of protection and reliability to the lowest. This ranking is known as the hierarchy of control measures (Figure 12).

[p48 | prose | 7.2 Hierarchy of control measures]
Using the hierarchy of control to manage risks will help you make sure you are using the most effective control measures first. Table 6 below provides examples of control measures that might be used to manage asbestos risks.

[p49 | table | 7.2 Hierarchy of control measures]
|  | ACTION | WHAT IS THIS? |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  | Elimination | Removing the source of harm, for example arranging for asbestos to be removed from the building or workplace. |
|  | Substituting (wholly or partly) | Swapping the hazard with something that has a lower risk. |
| MINIMISATION | Isolating or preventing contact | Separating people from the source of harm, for example enclosing asbestos material to minimise the risk of exposure. |
| Using engineering control measures | Using physical control measures to reduce exposure to a hazard, for example using equipment to minimise the release of asbestos fibres. |  |
| Using administrative control measures | Using safe methods of work, procedures, or processes, for example using signs and labels to identify areas where there is asbestos. |  |
| Using personal protective equipment (PPE) | Using or wearing items to minimise risks to personal health and safety, for example providing your workers with respiratory protective equipment. |  |

[p49 | prose | 7.3 Managing the risk of asbestos exposure]
7.3 Managing the risk of asbestos exposure

[p49 | prose | 7.3 Managing the risk of asbestos exposure]
You must take reasonably practicable steps to eliminate the risk of asbestos exposure.

[p49 | prose | 7.3 Managing the risk of asbestos exposure]
If it is not reasonably practicable to eliminate the risk, you must minimise the risk so far as is reasonably practicable. This means using control measures to:

[p49 | prose | 7.3 Managing the risk of asbestos exposure]
– reduce the chance of people being exposed to asbestos

[p49 | prose | 7.3 Managing the risk of asbestos exposure]
– reduce how severe the harm is if people are exposed to asbestos.

[p49 | prose | 7.3 Managing the risk of asbestos exposure]
Control measures to minimise risk should be used in combination to reduce the risk of asbestos exposure.

[p49 | prose | 7.3 Managing the risk of asbestos exposure]
Ideally, the control measures you use should reduce the severity of it and the chance of it happening.

[p49 | prose | 7.4 Prioritising risks]
7.4 Prioritising risks

[p49 | prose | 7.4 Prioritising risks]
You will need to decide which asbestos risks to deal with first.

[p49 | prose | 7.4 Prioritising risks]
Your asbestos management plan can help you determine which risks could be most likely to result in exposure to asbestos.

[p49 | prose | 7.4 Prioritising risks]
Prioritise managing these risks first, then move on to managing risks that are less likely to result in exposure to asbestos.

[p49 | prose | 7.5 Control measures for managing asbestos risks]
7.5 Control measures for managing asbestos risks

[p49 | prose | 7.5 Control measures for managing asbestos risks]
You will need to weigh up the benefits of using a control measure against any risks that it could create. This is likely to include thinking about:

[p49 | prose | 7.5 Control measures for managing asbestos risks]
– whether the asbestos material is friable or non-friable

[p49 | prose | 7.5 Control measures for managing asbestos risks]
– the condition of the asbestos material

[p49 | prose | 7.5 Control measures for managing asbestos risks]
– the location and accessibility of the asbestos material

[p49 | prose | 7.5 Control measures for managing asbestos risks]
– what effect a control measure might have on the occupants of your building or workplace.

[p52 | prose | 7.7 When is asbestos removal appropriate?]
7.7 When is asbestos removal appropriate?

[p52 | prose | 7.7 When is asbestos removal appropriate?]
Asbestos removal may be appropriate when:

[p52 | prose | 7.7 When is asbestos removal appropriate?]
– the surface of the asbestos material is friable

[p52 | prose | 7.7 When is asbestos removal appropriate?]
– the asbestos material is badly damaged (for example, water damage or lichen growth)

[p52 | prose | 7.7 When is asbestos removal appropriate?]
– the asbestos material is in air conditioning ducts

[p52 | prose | 7.7 When is asbestos removal appropriate?]
– airborne asbestos levels are above the legal limit

[p52 | prose | 7.7 When is asbestos removal appropriate?]
– other control measures are not appropriate.

[p52 | prose | 7.7 When is asbestos removal appropriate?]
Asbestos removal may not be appropriate when:

[p52 | prose | 7.7 When is asbestos removal appropriate?]
– the asbestos material is located in a complex or dangerous area of the building or workplace

[p52 | prose | 7.7 When is asbestos removal appropriate?]
– the asbestos material is in an area that cannot be easily accessed

[p52 | prose | 7.7 When is asbestos removal appropriate?]
– removal would be extremely difficult and another control measure would manage the risk.

7.10 Enclosing asbestos material

[p54 | prose | 7.10 Enclosing asbestos material]
Enclosing involves placing a physical barrier between the asbestos material and the surrounding area to:

[p54 | prose | 7.10 Enclosing asbestos material]
– minimise the risk of exposure to asbestos fibres

[p54 | prose | 7.10 Enclosing asbestos material]
– minimise the risk of the asbestos material being damaged or disturbed.

[p54 | prose | 7.10 Enclosing asbestos material]
Enclosing asbestos material should be considered if removal is not reasonably practicable or cannot be done immediately.

[p54 | prose | 7.10 Enclosing asbestos material]
The enclosure needs to be inspected and maintained regularly to make sure it is still providing an effective barrier. If there is any sign of damage or disturbance to the enclosure or to the asbestos material inside, you should arrange for:

[p54 | prose | 7.10 Enclosing asbestos material]
– urgent repair

[p54 | prose | 7.10 Enclosing asbestos material]
– further enclosure, or

[p54 | prose | 7.10 Enclosing asbestos material]
– asbestos removal.

[p54 | prose | 7.11 When is enclosing asbestos material appropriate?]
7.11 When is enclosing asbestos material appropriate?

[p54 | prose | 7.11 When is enclosing asbestos material appropriate?]
Enclosing asbestos material may be appropriate when:

[p54 | prose | 7.11 When is enclosing asbestos material appropriate?]
– removing the asbestos material would be extremely difficult or dangerous

[p54 | prose | 7.11 When is enclosing asbestos material appropriate?]
– asbestos fibres can be fully contained within the enclosure

[p54 | prose | 7.11 When is enclosing asbestos material appropriate?]
– most of the surface of the asbestos material cannot be accessed

[p54 | prose | 7.11 When is enclosing asbestos material appropriate?]
– it is unlikely that the asbestos material and enclosure will be disturbed.

[p54 | prose | 7.11 When is enclosing asbestos material appropriate?]
Enclosing asbestos material may not be appropriate if:

[p54 | prose | 7.11 When is enclosing asbestos material appropriate?]
– there is a possibility that the enclosure could be damaged (for example, water damage)

[p54 | prose | 7.11 When is enclosing asbestos material appropriate?]
– the asbestos material cannot be fully enclosed.

[p54 | prose | 7.12 Encapsulating asbestos material]
7.12 Encapsulating asbestos material

[p54 | prose | 7.12 Encapsulating asbestos material]
Encapsulating asbestos material involves using a liquid product (an encapsulant) that penetrates the surface of the asbestos material. When the liquid dries, it binds the asbestos fibres together, minimising the risk of fibres being released into the air.

[p54 | prose | 7.12 Encapsulating asbestos material]
Encapsulating asbestos material should be considered if removal is not reasonably practicable or cannot be done immediately.

[p54 | prose | 7.12 Encapsulating asbestos material]
Asbestos material that has been encapsulated needs to be checked regularly for signs of deterioration or damage. If there is any sign of damage or disturbance to the asbestos material, you should arrange for:

[p54 | prose | 7.12 Encapsulating asbestos material]
– urgent repair (for example, reapplication of the encapsulant), or

[p54 | prose | 7.12 Encapsulating asbestos material]
– another control measure to be used (for example, asbestos removal).

[p54 | prose | 7.13 When is encapsulating asbestos material appropriate?]
7.13 When is encapsulating asbestos material appropriate?

[p54 | prose | 7.13 When is encapsulating asbestos material appropriate?]
Encapsulating asbestos material may be appropriate when:

[p54 | prose | 7.13 When is encapsulating asbestos material appropriate?]
– removing the asbestos material would be extremely difficult or dangerous

[p54 | prose | 7.13 When is encapsulating asbestos material appropriate?]
– the asbestos material is unlikely to be damaged

[p54 | prose | 7.13 When is encapsulating asbestos material appropriate?]
– the asbestos material can be easily inspected.

[p55 | prose | 7.13 When is encapsulating asbestos material appropriate?]
Encapsulating asbestos material may not be appropriate if:

[p55 | prose | 7.13 When is encapsulating asbestos material appropriate?]
– the asbestos material is deteriorating

[p55 | prose | 7.13 When is encapsulating asbestos material appropriate?]
– the asbestos material is water-damaged.

[p55 | prose | 7.14 Sealing asbestos material]
7.14 Sealing asbestos material

[p55 | prose | 7.14 Sealing asbestos material]
Sealing asbestos material involves applying a sealant to the surface of the asbestos material. When the sealant dries, it creates a protective layer that physically traps asbestos fibres. This reduces the risk that the fibres will be released into the air.

[p55 | prose | 7.14 Sealing asbestos material]
Sealing asbestos material should be considered if removal is not reasonably practicable or cannot be done immediately.

[p55 | prose | 7.14 Sealing asbestos material]
Asbestos material that has been sealed needs to be checked regularly for signs of deterioration or damage. If there is any sign of damage or disturbance to the asbestos material, you should arrange for:

[p55 | prose | 7.14 Sealing asbestos material]
– urgent repair (for example, reapplication of the sealant), or

[p55 | prose | 7.14 Sealing asbestos material]
– another control measure to be used (for example, asbestos removal).

[p55 | prose | 7.15 When is sealing asbestos material appropriate?]
7.15 When is sealing asbestos material appropriate?

[p55 | prose | 7.15 When is sealing asbestos material appropriate?]
Sealing asbestos material may be appropriate when:

[p55 | prose | 7.15 When is sealing asbestos material appropriate?]
– removing the asbestos material would be extremely difficult or dangerous

[p55 | prose | 7.15 When is sealing asbestos material appropriate?]
– the asbestos material is unlikely to be damaged

[p55 | prose | 7.15 When is sealing asbestos material appropriate?]
– the asbestos material can be easily inspected.

[p55 | prose | 7.15 When is sealing asbestos material appropriate?]
Sealing asbestos material may not be appropriate if:

[p55 | prose | 7.15 When is sealing asbestos material appropriate?]
– the asbestos material is deteriorating

[p55 | prose | 7.15 When is sealing asbestos material appropriate?]
– the asbestos material is water-damaged

[p55 | prose | 7.15 When is sealing asbestos material appropriate?]

[p55 | prose | 7.16 When is it appropriate to leave asbestos material alone?]
7.16 When is it appropriate to leave asbestos material alone?

[p55 | prose | 7.16 When is it appropriate to leave asbestos material alone?]
In some cases, leaving asbestos material alone may be the safest reasonably practicable action to take.

[p55 | prose | 7.16 When is it appropriate to leave asbestos material alone?]
It may be appropriate to leave asbestos material alone if:

[p55 | prose | 7.16 When is it appropriate to leave asbestos material alone?]
– the risk of exposure is very low

[p55 | prose | 7.16 When is it appropriate to leave asbestos material alone?]
– the asbestos material is in good condition, unlikely to be damaged, and is fully contained or cannot be accessed.

[p55 | prose | 7.17 Demolition and refurbishment]
7.17 Demolition and refurbishment

[p55 | prose | 7.17 Demolition and refurbishment]
If your building or workplace contains or might contain asbestos material and you are planning demolition or refurbishment work, before work starts you must engage a competent person to:

[p55 | prose | 7.17 Demolition and refurbishment]
– inspect the areas of your building or workplace (including inaccessible areas such as wall cavities)

[p55 | prose | 7.17 Demolition and refurbishment]
– identify any asbestos material likely to be disturbed by the planned work.

[p56 | prose | 7.17 Demolition and refurbishment]
You must assume that asbestos is present if it is not reasonably practicable to determine if there is asbestos or asbestos material installed in the building, structure, or plant.

[p56 | prose | 7.17 Demolition and refurbishment]
Before any demolition or refurbishment work starts, you must make sure that any asbestos material is removed so far as is reasonably practicable (unless the asbestos material can only be accessed by demolition). Minor or routine maintenance work does not trigger this requirement.

[p56 | prose | Demolition, refurbishment, or maintenance?]
Demolition, refurbishment, or maintenance?

[p56 | prose | Demolition, refurbishment, or maintenance?]
Demolition means work to demolish or dismantle a structure, or part of a structure that is loadbearing or otherwise related to the physical integrity of the structure.

[p56 | prose | Demolition, refurbishment, or maintenance?]
Refurbishment means carrying out work in a building or structure with an emphasis on changing or upgrading it.

[p56 | prose | Demolition, refurbishment, or maintenance?]
Maintenance means care or upkeep that is planned, routine, or urgent work that keeps the building or structure in a proper condition or working order. Maintenance is incidental work that can be done quickly and safely with minimal control measures needed to ensure health and safety.

[p56 | prose | Demolition, refurbishment, or maintenance?]
When you are thinking about whether a job is maintenance or refurbishment, ask yourself what the primary purpose of the work is. Refurbishment is about making a change or an upgrade, whereas maintenance is about maintaining what you already have.

[p56 | prose | Demolition, refurbishment, or maintenance?]
For example, minor work on a kitchen such as fixing a plumbing fixture would be maintenance. Removing cabinetry or flooring for replacement would be refurbishment.

[p56 | prose | Demolition, refurbishment, or maintenance?]
You can read more about demolition, refurbishment, and maintenance on the WorkSafe website Demolition and refurbishment

Auron Developments has been contracted to carry out a routine flooring maintenance job in Goldside Community Hall. The hall was built in the late 1950s and was well used by the community. 8.2 Maintain effective control measures

[p60 | prose | 8.2 Maintain effective control measures]
If you put a control measure in place to manage the risk of asbestos exposure, you must make sure it is effective and maintained so that it stays effective.

[p60 | prose | 8.2 Maintain effective control measures]
This means that control measures must be regularly monitored and checked to ensure that they are still managing the risk effectively. This should occur on an ongoing basis – not just when the control measure is first put in place.

[p60 | prose | 8.2 Maintain effective control measures]
You should regularly inspect areas of your building or workplace that contain or are assumed to contain asbestos material. Inspections should assess:

[p60 | prose | 8.2 Maintain effective control measures]
– the condition of asbestos material

[p60 | prose | 8.2 Maintain effective control measures]
– any signs of deterioration of asbestos material

[p60 | prose | 8.2 Maintain effective control measures]
– the effectiveness of encapsulation, sealing, or enclosure.

[p60 | prose | 8.2 Maintain effective control measures]
If you discover that a control measure is no longer effective or needs to be changed, you must update your asbestos management plan.

Monitoring how well your asbestos control measures are performing can help to show if your control measures are still effective.

[p61 | prose | 8.4 Health monitoring]
8.4 Health monitoring

[p61 | prose | 8.4 Health monitoring]
Health monitoring means monitoring a person to identify any changes in their health status because of exposure to certain health hazards arising from work carried out by a business or undertaking.

[p61 | prose | 8.4 Health monitoring]
Health monitoring is a way to check if the health of workers is being harmed from exposure to hazards while carrying out work. It aims to detect early signs of ill-health or disease.

[p61 | prose | 8.4 Health monitoring]
HSWA requires that PCBUs monitor the health of their workers to make sure they are not being harmed from the work that they do for the PCBU. The Health and Safety at Work (General Risk and Workplace Management) Regulations provide more specific requirements for how to do health monitoring. You can read more about these requirements on the WorkSafe website Exposure monitoring and health monitoring – guidance for businesses

[p61 | prose | 8.4 Health monitoring]
If you have work that involves asbestos taking place at a building or workplace that you own or manage, you must make sure that appropriate health monitoring is in place for the workers carrying out the work.

[p62 | prose | 8.4 Health monitoring]
The duty to provide health monitoring is shared between relevant PCBUs. You may not be providing health monitoring to workers, but you must check that health monitoring is being provided to workers by another PCBU.

[p62 | prose | What health monitoring requirements do you need to check?]
What health monitoring requirements do you need to check?

[p62 | prose | What health monitoring requirements do you need to check?]
Before you have work involving asbestos carried out at your building or workplace, you must check that:

[p62 | prose | What health monitoring requirements do you need to check?]
– health monitoring is provided to workers at risk of exposure to asbestos

[p62 | prose | What health monitoring requirements do you need to check?]
– the health monitoring considers the demographic, medical, and occupational history of the worker, as well as records of the worker’s personal exposure to asbestos

[p62 | prose | What health monitoring requirements do you need to check?]
– the health monitoring includes a physical examination of the worker

[p62 | prose | What health monitoring requirements do you need to check?]
– the health monitoring starts or started within four weeks of a worker beginning asbestos removal work.

[p62 | prose | What health monitoring requirements do you need to check?]
Health monitoring is not required for workers of Class B asbestos removal licence-holders who carry out Class B asbestos removal work for less than four weeks in any 12-month period.

[p62 | prose | What health monitoring requirements do you need to check?]
Workers may also need other types of health monitoring depending on the type of work they do. You should check that the health monitoring in place is suitable for the work that will be carried out (for example, hearing tests for workers working in noisy environments).

8. Worker health monitoring

[p81 | prose | 8. Worker health monitoring]
Note: The requirement for worker health monitoring applies only to certain workplaces. Health monitoring must be provided for workers who may be exposed to asbestos while carrying out certain types of asbestos-related work.

[p81 | prose | 8. Worker health monitoring]
Get advice about whether health monitoring is required for workers, taking into account:

[p81 | prose | 8. Worker health monitoring]
– their exposure to asbestos/ACM

[p81 | prose | 8. Worker health monitoring]
– how long they have been exposed to it

[p81 | prose | 8. Worker health monitoring]
– the type of work that they do

[p81 | prose | 8. Worker health monitoring]
– the level of risk or potential risk to health and safety

[p81 | prose | 8. Worker health monitoring]
– whether respiratory protective equipment (RPE) is being used to manage risk.

[p81 | prose | 8. Worker health monitoring]
An occupational hygienist on the HASANZ Register can provide advice.
