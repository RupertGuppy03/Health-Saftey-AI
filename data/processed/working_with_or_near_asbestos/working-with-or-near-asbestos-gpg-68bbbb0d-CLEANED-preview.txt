[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
1.1 What are these guidelines about?

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
Tradespeople will often come across asbestos or asbestos-containing materials (ACMs) while doing their work. When asbestos is disturbed, tiny fibres can be released. Breathing them in can cause lung cancer, asbestosis, mesothelioma or other serious lung diseases.

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
Tradespeople have a duty to manage the risk of asbestos exposure to themselves, their workers, and others when working with or near asbestos or ACMs (referred to as asbestos-related work in these guidelines).

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
These guidelines provide practical information on how you can manage the risk of asbestos exposure and meet your duties under:

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
– Health and Safety at Work (Asbestos) Regulations 2016 (Asbestos Regulations) – Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
– Health and Safety at Work (General Risk and Workplace Management) Regulations 2016 (GRWM Regulations).

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
These guidelines cover general good practice principles that will apply to most asbestos-related work situations. They reflect a prescribed risk management process.

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
Detailed information sheets are also available that outline step-by-step instructions for doing specific asbestos-related tasks (for example, how to safely drill into asbestos cement board).

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
See Section 8 for a list of the available information sheets for specific asbestos- related work tasks. Where relevant, the information sheets should be used as well as these guidelines.

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
These guidelines do not cover:

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
– building refurbishment and/or demolition work (see Section 1.3 below for more information)

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
– licensed asbestos removal work

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
– unlicenced asbestos removal work. For example, removing less than 10m2 of non-friable asbestos as part of other work.

[p9 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
For information on asbestos removal work see Asbestos removal – good practice guidelines

[p10 | prose | 1.3 What is asbestos-related work?]
1.3 What is asbestos-related work?

[p10 | prose | 1.3 What is asbestos-related work?]
For the purpose of these guidelines asbestos-related work means maintenance activities and servicing work (for example minor repairs or alterations, encapsulation or sealing), that may disturb ACM or cause asbestos fibres to be released into the air. This does not include any work related to refurbishment, demolition, or removal of asbestos/ACM.

[p10 | prose | 1.3 What is asbestos-related work?]
Maintenance means care and/or upkeep that is planned, routine or urgent that keeps the building or structure in a proper condition or working order. It is incidental work that can be done quickly and safely with minimal control measures required to ensure safety.

[p10 | prose | 1.3 What is asbestos-related work?]
Refurbishment means carrying out work in a building or structure with the emphasis on changing and/or upgrading it.

[p10 | prose | 1.3 What is asbestos-related work?]
Demolition means work to demolish or dismantle a structure, or part of a structure, or that is loadbearing or otherwise related to the physical integrity of the structure.

[p10 | prose | 1.3 What is asbestos-related work?]
Removal work means the complete removal or replacement of asbestos, ACM or asbestos-contaminated dust or debris, regardless of the quantity or whether it is friable or non-friable.

[p10 | prose | 1.3 What is asbestos-related work?]
Before a building can be refurbished or demolished all asbestos must be positively identified, removed, and the building given clearance as required by the Asbestos Regulations. For more information see:

[p10 | prose | 1.3 What is asbestos-related work?]
– Conducting asbestos surveys – good practice guidelines

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
When more than one PCBU is working at the same worksite you must all work together to make sure any risks of asbestos exposure are managed appropriately. All PCBUs involved (such as the building owner and all other tradespeople that will be present) must, so far as is reasonably practicable:

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– consult each other

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– cooperate with each other

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– coordinate their activities.

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
This can be done by:

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– making sure everyone is aware of the locations of any asbestos at the worksite

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– discussing what work activities are going to be carried out that may disturb asbestos

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– agreeing on the degree of influence can control each PCBU has

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– agreeing on who will manage the risk of asbestos disturbance, and how it will be managed

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
– monitoring and checking that agreed safe work practices are being followed as agreed.

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
Use the asbestos management plan (if there is one) as the starting point for these conversations.

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
Any arrangements you make with other PCBUs on how the risks will be managed (for example, arrangements to monitor control measures) should be sensible and proportionate so far as is reasonably practicable.

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
You cannot contract out of your duties, but you can make reasonable agreements with other PCBUs to meet your duties. Each PCBU is responsible for making sure their own duties are met.

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
You can read more about overlapping PCBU duties in Overlapping duties – quick guide

[p12 | prose | 1.5 Overlapping PCBU duties]
See Section 12.1 Scenario one: managing overlapping duties for a worked example of managing overlapping duties related to managing the risk of asbestos exposure during asbestos-related work.

[p14 | prose | 2.1 How can workers be harmed?]
2.1 How can workers be harmed?

[p14 | prose | 2.1 How can workers be harmed?]
When asbestos or ACMs are disturbed, tiny fibres can be released. If you breathe in these fibres, they can lodge in your lungs and cause lung cancer, scarring of the lung tissue (asbestosis), cancer of the lung lining or abdomen (mesothelioma) or other serious diseases of the lungs and surrounding membranes.

[p14 | prose | 2.1 How can workers be harmed?]
Symptoms for most asbestos-related diseases take between 10 to 40 years before they start to appear and can include shortness of breath, a persistent cough, and chest pains.

[p14 | prose | 2.1 How can workers be harmed?]
Most asbestos-related diseases are caused by exposure to asbestos fibres at work. Even small jobs can be risky if the right procedures are not followed to minimise the spread of asbestos fibres.

[p14 | prose | 2.1 How can workers be harmed?]
For more information see Asbestos in Aotearoa New Zealand

[p15 | prose | 2.1 How can workers be harmed?]
You must ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health and safety of any workers who work for you or who you influence or direct, and make sure that other persons are not put at risk by the work that you carry out. This is called the ‘primary duty of care’. If you are self-employed, you must also ensure (so far as is reasonably practicable) your own health and safety at work. This includes protecting yourself, your workers, and others from the risk of exposure to asbestos fibres.

[p15 | prose | Others can be harmed too]
Others can be harmed too

[p15 | prose | Others can be harmed too]
If you do not follow safe work practices during asbestos-related work, you risk spreading asbestos fibres and exposing other people to asbestos fibres. For example:

[p15 | prose | Others can be harmed too]
– you may cause contamination of the work area where other people are also working

[p15 | prose | Others can be harmed too]
– you may cause contamination of someone’s home, putting at risk the health of those who live there

[p15 | prose | Others can be harmed too]
– you may contaminate your work vehicle, putting at risk the health of anyone else who gets in that vehicle

[p15 | prose | Others can be harmed too]
– you may contaminate your own home (for example by wearing or taking home contaminated clothing), putting at risk the health of your own family.

4.1 If the place you will be working is a workplace or a rented property

[p20 | prose | 4.1 If the place you will be working is a workplace or a rented property]

[p35 | prose | 8.1 Keep dust down by keeping asbestos or ACM damp]

[p35 | prose | 8.1 Keep dust down by keeping asbestos or ACM damp]

[p35 | prose | 8.1 Keep dust down by keeping asbestos or ACM damp]
– When working with or near electrical sockets and wiring make sure to turn off mains power before misting.

[p35 | prose | 8.1 Keep dust down by keeping asbestos or ACM damp]

Gently wetting

ACM before working on it will reduce the amount of dust produced

[p36 | prose | 8.2 Use an H-class vacuum cleaner to control dust]
8.2 Use an H-class vacuum cleaner to control dust

[p36 | prose | 8.2 Use an H-class vacuum cleaner to control dust]
As some ACMs (such as asbestos boards or sheets) cannot be wetted all the way through, additional methods to control dust such as ‘shadow vacuuming’ may be needed. Shadow vacuuming is where you have the vacuum cleaner nozzle positioned as close as possible to where the work is being done to immediately capture any dust that is produced (for example, when removing a screw from asbestos wall cladding hold the nozzle so that the nozzle sucks dust and debris away as soon as it is created). Dust shrouds that connect to your H-class vacuum cleaner are used to maximise the amount of dust that can be captured (see Figures 9–10).

[p36 | prose | 8.2 Use an H-class vacuum cleaner to control dust]
For any vacuuming involving asbestos or ACM you must use an H-class (high hazard) industrial vacuum cleaner with a suitable high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filter (see Figure 11). H-class vacuums can be used for removal of moist or dampened material, but not soaking wet material or water.

[p36 | prose | 8.2 Use an H-class vacuum cleaner to control dust]

8.3 Do not use certain tools

[p37 | prose | 8.3 Do not use certain tools]
The Asbestos Regulations prohibit the use of power tools, brooms, and any other equipment that can cause asbestos to be released into the air when working with asbestos. Power tools and other equipment (including angle grinders, sanders, saws, drills, brushes, and brooms) may only be used on asbestos if:

[p37 | prose | 8.3 Do not use certain tools]
– the equipment is designed to capture or suppress asbestos fibres and is used according to its design, or

[p37 | prose | 8.3 Do not use certain tools]
– the equipment is used in a way designed to capture or suppress asbestos fibres safely. For example, through engineering control measures such as using a shroud filled with foam or extraction ventilation.

[p39 | prose | Decontaminating tools and equipment]
Use a damp cloth or wet wipe to wipe down tools and surfaces to remove asbestos fibres:

[p39 | prose | Decontaminating tools and equipment]
– Do not re-use the cloth.

[p39 | prose | Decontaminating tools and equipment]
– Fold the cloth so that no part is used twice so as not to spread fibres and cause cross-contamination.

[p39 | prose | Decontaminating tools and equipment]
– Dispose of the cloth as asbestos waste (see Section 10.0).

[p39 | prose | Decontaminating tools and equipment]
Some tools are hard to clean perfectly. For these, wipe them down, then seal them in a labelled bag or container. Only open the sealed bag or container again when you are in the next asbestos work area and wearing PPE and RPE. This stops you from spreading contamination.

[p39 | prose | Decontaminating tools and equipment]
‘Quarantining’ difficult-to-decontaminate items will help to prevent cross- contamination. See How to decontaminate tools safely for an instructional video for workers on how to decontaminate tools.

[p40 | prose | Decontaminating the work area]
Decontaminating the work area

[p40 | prose | Decontaminating the work area]
Use an H-class vacuum cleaner with a suitable HEPA filter, for more information see Industrial vacuums and portable extractors for hazardous dust

[p40 | prose | Decontaminating the work area]
Never use domestic vacuum cleaners to clean up asbestos dust. Do not use a broom to sweep up waste. Using a domestic vacuum cleaner or a broom will spread asbestos fibres into the air.

[p40 | prose | Decontaminating the work area]
Look closely at the work area before leaving the site – has it been cleaned thoroughly? Check under drop sheets and under asbestos waste bags and the waste area. No dust or debris should be visible.

[p40 | prose | Decontaminating the work area]
Decontaminate the H-Class vacuum cleaner with wet wipes last after you have finished using it for decontamination of everything else.

9.2 Decontaminate yourself

[p40 | prose | 9.2 Decontaminate yourself]
Follow the below steps for personal decontamination. This will help limit the spread of any asbestos fibres trapped in your PPE or RPE.

[p40 | prose | 9.2 Decontaminate yourself]
You must decontaminate every time you leave the work area – otherwise you risk spreading asbestos fibres outside the work area.

[p40 | prose | 9.2 Decontaminate yourself]
As a PCBU doing asbestos-related work you must make sure your workers have the right training, facilities, and equipment they need to decontaminate properly.

[p40 | prose | 9.2 Decontaminate yourself]
You must keep RPE on until step 4.

[p40 | prose | Step]

[p40 | prose | Step]

[p49 | prose | 12.2 Scenario two: demonstrating safe work practices]
Kev, an Oamaru plumber, is installing a new vented hot water cylinder in a house. Kev confirms that the old hot water cylinder and asbestos lagging round its pipes and joins has been safely removed by a licensed asbestos removalist. He has seen the clearance certificate for the removal.

[p50 | prose | 12.2 Scenario two: demonstrating safe work practices]

[p50 | prose | 12.2 Scenario two: demonstrating safe work practices]

[p50 | prose | 12.2 Scenario two: demonstrating safe work practices]

– Grant records the results and files them in the AMP.

[p52 | prose | Monitoring and review]
– Ongoing health monitoring is scheduled for all three workers.

[p52 | prose | Monitoring and review]
Because Grant expects similar work will be done again frequently in old community buildings over the next couple of years, he also organises health monitoring for himself, Api, and Jamie. Grant, Api and Jamie get baseline lung function tests and ongoing medical checks.

[p52 | prose | Monitoring and review]
By following these steps Grant should be meeting his duties under the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 and the Health and Safety at Work (Asbestos) Regulations 2016. He protects Api, and Jamie from asbestos exposure, reassures the hall committee, fulfils overlapping duties with all PCBUs and confirms his safe work methods will keep asbestos risks under control on this and future projects.
