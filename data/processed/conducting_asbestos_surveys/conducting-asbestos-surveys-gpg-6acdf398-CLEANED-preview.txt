[p10 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
1.1 What are these guidelines about?

[p10 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
These guidelines provide good practice advice for how to plan, carry out, and report the results of an asbestos survey.

[p10 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
These guidelines will help PCBUs that conduct asbestos surveys to comply with the requirements of the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA) and The Health and Safety at Work (Asbestos) Regulations 2016 (Regulations).

[p10 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
An asbestos survey is where a competent person (referred to as a surveyor in these guidelines) inspects a building or workplace to:

[p10 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
– identify asbestos material or asbestos containing material (ACM)

[p10 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
– provide information on the location and quantity of asbestos present

[p10 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
– report on the condition of the asbestos.

[p10 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
This information may then be used to create a plan on how to manage the asbestos in-situ, or safely remove it prior to renovation or demolition work, depending on the type of survey being done.

[p10 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
For more information on the different types of asbestos surveys, see Section 3.0: Asbestos surveys

[p10 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
For more information on the qualifications and experience needed to qualify as a competent person to provide asbestos surveys, see Section 2.0: Qualifications and experience for surveying

[p10 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
These guidelines do not cover surveying contaminated land. For more information, see New Zealand Guidelines for Assessing and Managing Asbestos in Soil

[p11 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)

[p11 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
HSWA is the primary work health and safety legislation in New Zealand. HSWA applies to all work and workplaces unless specifically excluded.

[p11 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
All PCBUs (including self-employed surveyors and those who employ surveyors) have a primary duty of care under HSWA. The primary duty of care means that a PCBU must ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health and safety of:

[p11 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
– its workers (such as employees, contractors, subcontractors, apprentices)

[p11 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
– any other workers who are influenced or directed by the PCBU, (such as workers of other PCBUs on the same site).

[p11 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
A PCBU must also ensure that the health and safety of other persons is not put at risk by its work (for example, tenants, visitors, customers, and passers-by).

[p12 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
For surveying this includes:

[p12 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
– anyone that may be nearby when a survey or sampling is being done

[p12 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
– the people or businesses who may rely on the information and advice provided in the surveys and reports they produce.

[p12 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
Surveyors should, so far as is reasonably practicable, make sure their survey findings and reports are thorough and accurate enough for other PCBUs to rely on when creating plans for how to safely manage or remove asbestos.

[p12 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
Self-employed persons are also considered PCBUs, and the primary duty of care applies. They must also ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, their own health and safety while at work.

[p12 | prose | 1.5 The Health and Safety at Work (Asbestos) Regulations 2016 (the Regulations)]
1.5 The Health and Safety at Work (Asbestos) Regulations 2016 (the Regulations)

[p12 | prose | 1.5 The Health and Safety at Work (Asbestos) Regulations 2016 (the Regulations)]
The Regulations outline specific legislative requirements for the safe management and removal of asbestos in New Zealand.

[p12 | prose | 1.5 The Health and Safety at Work (Asbestos) Regulations 2016 (the Regulations)]
Surveyors do not have specific duties mentioned in the Regulations.

[p12 | prose | 1.5 The Health and Safety at Work (Asbestos) Regulations 2016 (the Regulations)]
However, surveying for asbestos material is a key step in the asbestos management process. Accurately undertaking asbestos surveys is essential to the safe management and removal of asbestos.

2.1 Competence to carry out asbestos surveys

[p14 | prose | 2.1 Competence to carry out asbestos surveys]
Before carrying out a survey of a building to identify asbestos, the surveyor should be able to demonstrate that they are a competent person.

[p14 | prose | 2.1 Competence to carry out asbestos surveys]
Competence can be demonstrated through a combination of relevant qualifications and practical experience.

[p14 | prose | Competent person]
Competent person

[p14 | prose | Competent person]
A competent person means a person who has the knowledge, experience, skills, and qualifications to carry out a particular task under the Regulations, including any knowledge, experience, skills, and qualifications prescribed in a safe work instrument.

[p14 | prose | Competent person]
To carry out an asbestos survey, a competent person should be able to demonstrate that they are:

[p14 | prose | Competent person]
– qualified and experienced in all aspects of asbestos inspection work, including planning, risk assessment, asbestos material assessment, reporting, and quality control – as outlined in these guidelines

[p14 | prose | Competent person]
– knowledgeable about asbestos products, including their nature, uses, hazards, and sampling techniques

[p14 | prose | Competent person]
– knowledgeable about relevant building types (such as industrial, commercial, and residential buildings)

[p14 | prose | Competent person]
– knowledgeable about building components and structures (such as ducts, eaves, fascia, risers, soffits, and partitions) that may contain asbestos

[p14 | prose | Competent person]
– knowledgeable about relevant construction practices and building design, including past and present practices and design

[p14 | prose | Competent person]
– able to review and understand construction plans and specifications for proposed works and historic construction records

[p14 | prose | Competent person]
– aware of the unexpected places asbestos may be located (and most likely not recorded on building plans). For example, being used in ad hoc ways such as packing around columns and as spacers around windows and door frames

[p14 | prose | Competent person]
– knowledgeable about building systems that may contain asbestos material (such as fire protection systems).

[p15 | prose | Competent person]
PCBUs providing surveying services should be able to provide the commissioning PCBU with evidence that the workers carrying out the work are competent.

[p15 | prose | Competent person]
– qualification records for the workers carrying out the work

[p15 | prose | Competent person]
– the status of any relevant accreditations and certifications for the workers carrying out the work

[p15 | prose | Competent person]
– information about the experience and capabilities of the workers carrying out the work

[p15 | prose | Competent person]
– evidence of recent similar work

[p15 | prose | Competent person]
– evidence the surveyor is employed or engaged by an inspection body accredited to ISO 17020.

There are two main types of asbestos surveys. Choosing which type to do depends on what the PCBU intends to do with the information.

[p19 | prose | 3.1 The purpose of asbestos surveys]
3.1 The purpose of asbestos surveys

[p19 | prose | 3.1 The purpose of asbestos surveys]
The overall purpose of an asbestos survey is to identify asbestos containing materials to manage them effectively in a building or workplace. There are two types of asbestos surveys depending on what the PCBU intends to do with the information: an asbestos management survey and a refurbishment/demolition survey.

[p19 | prose | Asbestos management survey]
Asbestos management survey

[p19 | prose | Asbestos management survey]
The purpose of a management survey is to find and record the location, extent, and product type of any known or presumed ACM in a building or workplace.

[p19 | prose | Asbestos management survey]
It should include information about the accessibility, condition, and surface treatment of any known or presumed ACM.

[p19 | prose | Asbestos management survey]
The survey must provide sufficient information for an asbestos management plan to be prepared. This plan is then used to inform the safe management of asbestos during normal occupation and use of the building or workplace.

[p19 | prose | Asbestos management survey]
It will usually involve taking and testing samples of suspected ACM. In certain circumstances, the presence of ACM can be presumed.

[p19 | prose | Asbestos management survey]
See Section 3.2 for more information about management surveys.

[p19 | prose | Refurbishment/demolition survey]
Refurbishment/demolition survey

[p19 | prose | Refurbishment/demolition survey]
The purpose of a refurbishment/demolition survey is to find and record the location, extent, and product type of all ACM in a building or workplace (or part of it).

[p19 | prose | Refurbishment/demolition survey]
It should provide enough detailed information to enable a suitable risk assessment to be carried out in preparation for refurbishment or demolition of the building or workplace (or part of it).

[p19 | prose | Refurbishment/demolition survey]
It typically requires more extensive and intrusive sampling than is required for a management survey.

[p19 | prose | Refurbishment/demolition survey]
Unlike a management survey, all suspected ACM that may be disturbed must be confirmed by sample testing. The presence of ACM cannot be presumed.

[p19 | prose | Refurbishment/demolition survey]
See Section 3.3 for more information about refurbishment/demolition surveys.

For more information about the terms ‘refurbishment’ and ‘maintenance, see WorkSafe’s webpage Asbestos – Refurbishment versus maintenance

[p20 | prose | 3.2 What is an asbestos management survey?]
3.2 What is an asbestos management survey?

[p20 | prose | 3.2 What is an asbestos management survey?]
An asbestos management survey is a survey carried out to identify asbestos in a building or workplace. It is typically used by PCBUs who own or manage a building or workplace to inform their asbestos management plan. The Regulations require all PCBUs to have an asbestos management plan for their building or workplace, if there is asbestos or ACM suspected to be present.

[p20 | prose | 3.2 What is an asbestos management survey?]
For more information on PCBU duties to manage asbestos in their building or workplace, see WorkSafe’s good practice guidelines Managing asbestos in your building or workplace – for PCBUs

[p20 | prose | 3.2 What is an asbestos management survey?]
Management surveys are designed to assist with the management of asbestos in-situ. If a PCBU is planning to refurbish or demolish a building, or part of it, a refurbishment/demolition survey must be done - even if they already have a management survey.

[p20 | prose | 3.2 What is an asbestos management survey?]
For more information, see Section 3.3: What is a refurbishment/demolition survey?

[p30 | prose | Survey strategy]
Survey strategy

[p30 | prose | Survey strategy]
A survey strategy is an approach used to effectively identify the presence and location of asbestos material in a building.

[p30 | prose | Survey strategy]
As each building and workplace is likely to be different, a survey strategy will need to be developed for each asbestos survey carried out. Thinking about the survey strategy early in the planning process can help avoid challenges and make sure the survey is comprehensive and reliable.

[p30 | prose | Survey strategy]
In premises where there are large numbers of similar or near-identical rooms (such as offices or hotels), a survey strategy can be adopted which reflects the scale and nature of the buildings. All rooms should be visually inspected, as there can be differences due to location (such as the presence of risers or services) or function/facilities.

[p30 | prose | Survey strategy]
Rooms with similar locations or facilities rooms can be placed into groups, such as next to lifts or containing risers. There is likely to be greater uniformity in these groups for the presence of asbestos and ACM. In these groups, there may be less need for sampling in all rooms. Sampling can be conducted in a representative number of rooms and, if ACM is identified, the same items in other rooms in this group can be presumed to contain asbestos.

Use initiative when carrying out a survey

[p35 | prose | 5.2 Methodical approach to surveying]
Being inquisitive and using initiative is important when carrying out the survey. Materials should be tapped and prodded. Everything should be checked and inspected. Do not presume that an item is the same as another just because it looks similar. This is particularly relevant when presuming items are non-asbestos.

[p35 | prose | 5.2 Methodical approach to surveying]
Sample and take photos as the survey progresses. Look out for unusual potential sources such as overspray or packers.

[p35 | prose | 5.2 Methodical approach to surveying]
It is good practice to survey ‘in pairs’ (two people) working together, with both inspecting one area at the same time.

[p35 | prose | 5.2 Methodical approach to surveying]
Re-check areas which have many items (such as plant rooms). ACMs may be missed where surveyors are tired, rushed or make assumptions.

[p35 | prose | 5.2 Methodical approach to surveying]

5.4 Understand asbestos types

[p36 | prose | 5.4 Understand asbestos types]
Knowing the type of asbestos is necessary to carry out a material assessment.

[p36 | prose | 5.4 Understand asbestos types]
If the material has not been tested, and there are no similar products identified in the survey, assign a likely asbestos type. This is based on the product type and age.

[p36 | prose | 5.4 Understand asbestos types]
It may be possible to get information on the type of asbestos from close inspection of the material. For example, if fibres are visible in the product, these can give some extra clues to the type of asbestos (for example, by the colour of fibre bundles or if they have sharp or curly bundles).

[p36 | prose | 5.4 Understand asbestos types]
Unless there is evidence to show otherwise, presume the asbestos type is crocidolite asbestos. This is in line with the precautionary principle in risk management.

[p36 | prose | 5.5 Presumed presence of ACMs]
5.5 Presumed presence of ACMs

[p36 | prose | 5.5 Presumed presence of ACMs]
Presuming the presence or absence of asbestos can only be done in certain situations and only for management surveys. For refurbishment/demolition surveys, the asbestos regulations require that all asbestos likely to be disturbed during demolition must be identified.

[p36 | prose | 5.5 Presumed presence of ACMs]
For management surveys if a material cannot be identified but is likely to be asbestos or ACM, the PCBU must presume it is asbestos. If any part of the workplace is inaccessible, but likely to contain asbestos, it must also be presumed to be present. This does not apply if the PCBU already presumes asbestos is present or has good reason to believe it is not.

[p36 | prose | 5.5 Presumed presence of ACMs]
For more information, see Section 7.0: Presumptions about asbestos management surveys

Identifying asbestos in inaccessible areas during refurbishment and demolition surveys

[p37 | prose | Identifying asbestos in inaccessible areas during refurbishment and demolition surveys]
Regulations require that all asbestos that is likely to be disturbed during refurbishment or demolition must be identified. Usually the intrusive nature of demolition/refurbishment surveys will allow for testing and sample taking of all suspected asbestos.

[p37 | prose | Identifying asbestos in inaccessible areas during refurbishment and demolition surveys]
Commissioning PCBUs should take all practical steps to facilitate access to all areas to take samples. PCBUs should not restrict access unless there the site is unsafe (such as if there is fire damage).

[p37 | prose | Identifying asbestos in inaccessible areas during refurbishment and demolition surveys]
Where there are areas where asbestos is suspected to be present but cannot be accessed to take samples, it may be necessary to do a staged survey as the demolition or refurbishment work progresses. The surveyor should:

[p37 | prose | Identifying asbestos in inaccessible areas during refurbishment and demolition surveys]
– note the location and reasons why samples could not be taken

[p37 | prose | Identifying asbestos in inaccessible areas during refurbishment and demolition surveys]
– advise the commissioning PCBU that further testing for asbestos will be required as the demolition or refurbishment progresses and the area becomes accessible.

[p37 | prose | Identifying asbestos in inaccessible areas during refurbishment and demolition surveys]
If the suspected ACM is completely contained in a piece of plant or equipment (such as a hot water cylinder), and the plant or equipment can be removed without disturbing the asbestos inside – it does not need to be opened up to allow testing. To do so would create unnecessary risk.

[p43 | prose | 5.10 Manage risk while doing a survey and taking samples]
5.10 Manage risk while doing a survey and taking samples

[p43 | prose | 5.10 Manage risk while doing a survey and taking samples]
Controls should be put in place to prevent the spread of debris, which may contain asbestos. Refurbishment/demolition surveys should only be conducted in unoccupied areas to minimise risks to the public or workers on the premises. Ideally, the building should not be in service, and all furnishings should be removed.

[p43 | prose | 5.10 Manage risk while doing a survey and taking samples]
For minor refurbishment, this would only apply to the room involved or even part of the room where the work is small and the room large. In these situations, there should be effective isolation of the survey area (such as full floor-to-ceiling partition), and furnishings should be removed as far as possible or protected using sheeting.

[p43 | prose | 5.10 Manage risk while doing a survey and taking samples]
The ‘surveyed’ area must be shown to be fit for reoccupation before people move back in. This will require a thorough visual inspection and, if appropriate (for example, where there is significant destruction), reassurance air sampling.

[p43 | prose | 5.10 Manage risk while doing a survey and taking samples]
Under no circumstances should staff remain in rooms or areas of buildings when intrusive sampling is performed.

[p43 | prose | 5.10 Manage risk while doing a survey and taking samples]
A demolition survey may be conducted while a building is still in use. For instance, in schools or colleges, these surveys might take place during holidays, with actual work delayed until a later closure. Demolition surveys can also assess a building’s economic viability if there is asbestos and how this will change any part of the building. In these situations, the survey will need extremely careful managing with equipment/furnishings being removed or protected (as necessary), while the survey progresses through the building. Surveyed areas should be isolated, and reoccupation only allowed once the area is confirmed safe.

[p43 | prose | 5.10 Manage risk while doing a survey and taking samples]
If a surveyor identifies a high-risk situation while doing a survey (for example, they find damaged, friable asbestos in an occupied environment like a classroom) the surveyor must notify the commissioning PCBU immediately. The commissioning PCBU must then take immediate remedial action to manage the risk. The surveyor must not wait until their report is prepared and delivered to notify the commissioning PCBU of any high-risk situations they identify.

[p43 | prose | 5.11 Bulk sampling strategy]
5.11 Bulk sampling strategy

[p43 | prose | 5.11 Bulk sampling strategy]
The most convenient and efficient approach is to take samples during the survey. However, in cases involving large premises or inaccessible areas, sampling may be carried out later when the area becomes available.

[p43 | prose | 5.11 Bulk sampling strategy]
Carry out a thorough visual inspection of each room and area to identify materials and locations to take samples from.

[p43 | prose | 5.11 Bulk sampling strategy]
During the inspection, carefully assess the material for differences and variations in appearance. The size of the sample, the number of samples, and the spread of samples will vary depending on the density or spread of the suspected asbestos fibres in the ACM.

[p43 | prose | 5.11 Bulk sampling strategy]
Take samples of the surface area and, where necessary through the entire depth of the suspected ACM. This includes any backing paper. It is important to consider the likely spread of the asbestos fibres in the suspected ACM – making sure enough samples are taken to represent the whole material.

[p43 | prose | 5.11 Bulk sampling strategy]
Do not take samples where there is an electrical hazard or if the sampling will/ could damage the critical integrity of a roof, gutter, or pipe.

[p44 | prose | 5.11 Bulk sampling strategy]
Swab sampling (for example, wiping or using adhesive tape to pick up possible asbestos fibres on a surface) cannot be used for asbestos identification. IANZ no longer accredits laboratories to analyse swab samples.

[p44 | prose | 5.11 Bulk sampling strategy]
Swab sampling must not form a part of a sampling strategy during asbestos surveys.

5.12 Bulk sampling procedures

[p46 | prose | 5.12 Bulk sampling procedures]
Before sampling begins do a comprehensive risk assessment of the survey site. Safe procedures identified in the risk assessment must be adhered to, ensuring minimal disruption to the commissioning PCBU’s operations and maximum protection for the health and safety of everyone at risk.

[p46 | prose | General precautions during sampling]
General precautions during sampling

[p46 | prose | General precautions during sampling]
Unoccupied areas:

[p46 | prose | General precautions during sampling]
– Sampling should be carried out in unoccupied areas whenever possible.

[p46 | prose | General precautions during sampling]
– For normally occupied areas, sampling should occur during periods of minimal occupation.

[p46 | prose | General precautions during sampling]
Dust control and safety measures:

[p46 | prose | General precautions during sampling]
– Take all reasonably practicable steps to minimise dust from sampling.

[p46 | prose | General precautions during sampling]
– Entry to sampling areas should be restricted, with clear warnings posted. For example: ‘Asbestos sampling in progress: Keep out.’

[p46 | prose | General precautions during sampling]
– Surfaces where asbestos debris might fall should be protected using impervious material (such as polythene), which can be easily cleaned using wet-wiping or a Class H vacuum cleaner.

[p46 | prose | General precautions during sampling]
– All equipment used during sampling that may have come into contact with suspected asbestos should be treated as contaminated. Contaminated tools or equipment must be stored in impervious containers until they can be safely decontaminated or disposed of as asbestos waste.

[p46 | prose | Control of airborne emissions]
Control of airborne emissions

[p46 | prose | Control of airborne emissions]
Airborne asbestos emissions should be controlled by pre-wetting the material to be sampled using water and/or a suitable wetting agent.

[p46 | prose | Control of airborne emissions]
Techniques include:

[p46 | prose | Control of airborne emissions]
– spraying surfaces (such as boards and sheets)

[p46 | prose | Control of airborne emissions]
– injecting material (such as lagging and sprays)

[p46 | prose | Control of airborne emissions]
– shadow vacuuming (if wetting is incomplete or unsafe, such as where material might drip into electrical installations):

[p46 | prose | Control of airborne emissions]
this involves holding the suction inlet close to the area where dust is generated using a Class H vacuum cleaner (see Section 10: Tools and equipment)

[p46 | prose | Control of airborne emissions]
- examples of materials requiring shadow vacuuming include AC sheets, AIB boards, ropes, and gaskets.

[p50 | prose | Material and priority assessments are an important tool for PCBUs to use when prioritising actions as part of managing asbestos.]
Material and priority assessments are an important tool for PCBUs to use when prioritising actions as part of managing asbestos.

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
6.1 Material assessments

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
A material assessment identifies the materials that will most readily release airborne fibres if disturbed. A material assessment allows the PCBU with management or control of a workplace to assess the potential for fibre release for each ACM, then go on to prioritise the need for action as part of the plan for managing asbestos.

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
For more information on creating asbestos management plans for PCBUs, see WorkSafe’s webpage Managing asbestos in your building or workplace – for PCBUs

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
The material assessment should be carried out as part of the management survey using a standardised material assessment equation. It is based on an additive algorithm. The tool can be used to numerically assess the potential for fibre release. The tool is not designed to calculate absolute differences in potency or fibre release/hazard potential between ACMs. However, it does allow ACMs to be ranked in numerical order.

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
The material assessment focuses on identifying ‘high-hazard’ materials – those most likely to release airborne fibres if disturbed. However, a high score in the material assessment does not automatically indicate the need for immediate remedial action.

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
The priority for action must be determined through a risk assessment known as a priority assessment, which considers various additional factors, including:

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
– Location of the material: Where the material is situated

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
– Extent of the material: The size or spread of the material

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
– Purpose of the location: The functions or activities conducted in the area

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
– Occupancy of the area: The number and frequency of people present in the space

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
– Activities in the area: The nature of work or processes carried out in the vicinity

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
– Likelihood of maintenance: The probability and frequency of maintenance activities in the area.

[p50 | prose | 6.1 Material assessments]
For more information, see Section 6.4: Priority assessments

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
6.2 Carry out a material assessment

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
In a material assessment process, the main factors influencing fibre release are given a score that can be added together to obtain a material assessment rating.

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
The four main parameters that determine the amount of fibre released from an ACM, when subject to disturbance, are:

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
– product type

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
– extent of damage or deterioration

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
– surface treatment

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
– asbestos type.

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
Each parameter is scored between 1 and 3, where a score of:

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
– 1 indicates a low potential for fibre release

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
– 2 indicates a medium potential for fibre release

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
– 3 indicates a high potential for fibre release.

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
Any two of the parameters can also be given a score of zero (equivalent to a very low potential for fibre release).

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
The values assigned to each parameter are added together to give a total score of between 2 and 12 (see Table 20).

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
Presumed ACMs are scored as crocidolite (score = 3), unless there is strong evidence to show otherwise.

[p51 | prose | 6.2 Carry out a material assessment]
Non-asbestos materials are not scored.

[p52 | prose | 6.4 Priority assessments]

[p54 | prose | 6.5 Combined material and priority assessments]
6.5 Combined material and priority assessments

[p54 | prose | 6.5 Combined material and priority assessments]
Combining the material and priority assessments helps to evaluate and manage asbestos materials by assessing both the potential for fibre release and the priority for remedial action.

[p54 | prose | 6.5 Combined material and priority assessments]
This method helps to make sure that an effective plan is developed for managing asbestos in the workplace. PCBUs must determine individual action plans based on combined material and priority assessment, reflective of their specific risk profile (such as schools or healthcare settings).

[p54 | prose | 6.5 Combined material and priority assessments]
The combined material and priority assessment results should be used to establish the priority for those ACMs needing remedial action, and the type of action that will be taken. There are various remedial options available. Where removal is not reasonably practicable, the ACMs can be protected or enclosed, sealed or encapsulated, or repaired.

[p56 | prose | Presuming the presence or absence of asbestos can only be done in certain situations.]
Presuming the presence or absence of asbestos can only be done in certain situations.

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
Presuming the presence or absence of asbestos can only be done in certain situations and only for management surveys. For refurbishment/demolition surveys, the asbestos regulations require that all asbestos likely to be disturbed during demolition must be identified.

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
Management surveys do allow for the presence of asbestos to be presumed in certain situations. In some circumstances, it may be appropriate to presume that a material is asbestos instead of confirming the presence of asbestos by sampling.

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
Surveyors should always attempt to positively identify ACMs. Assuming the presence of asbestos should not be a default practice. Surveyors should only make such assumptions if they are suitably experienced and familiar with various asbestos products.

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
Experienced surveyors may use certain characteristics to compare with other materials they know contain asbestos:

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
– surface texture

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
– sound when knocked

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
– warmth to the touch

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
– surface hardness/deformation with a probe.

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
Unless the surveyor is convinced that there is adequate evidence to conclude that the material is free from asbestos (for example, in the plaster, plasterboard, or wood), a presumption should be made that it is an ACM.

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
Examples of situations where it may be appropriate to presume the presence of asbestos include if:

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
– ACM is confirmed in one area of the building and there is a second area with similar materials used in the same way, it may be acceptable to presume that the material in the second area is also ACM

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
– an area cannot be accessed for sampling (such as the roof of a building), but building records and a visual inspection indicate it is ACM, it can be recorded as presumed to be ACM

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
– an area or product cannot be accessed for sampling (such as water heater insulation), but it is known that asbestos was commonly used in that product at the time it was installed, it can be recorded as presumed to contain asbestos

[p56 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
– an area or product would require significant disturbance to access and sample (such as lift brakes), but it is known that asbestos was commonly used in that product at the time it was installed, it can be recorded as presumed to contain asbestos.

[p57 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
The surveyor should presume that any area that is not accessed or inspected contains asbestos unless there is strong evidence it does not (see Section 7.3: Previously inspected areas).

[p57 | prose | 7.1 Presuming that asbestos is present]
Presuming the presence of asbestos should only be done when the commissioning PCBU has agreed or where access genuinely cannot be obtained. Commissioning PCBUs should be aware of the disadvantages as well as advantages of presuming the presence of asbestos in their management surveys, as well as risks of exposure when taking samples and over-sampling.

7.2 Presuming that asbestos is not present

[p57 | prose | 7.2 Presuming that asbestos is not present]
Surveyors should take reasonably practicable steps to make sure that asbestos material is not present before recording it as such. Surveyors cannot presume that a material does not contain asbestos unless there is a strong reason to believe so.

[p57 | prose | Avoiding making false assumptions]
Avoiding making false assumptions

[p57 | prose | Avoiding making false assumptions]
Asbestos material may be hidden, even if a building or workplace has detailed records of previous work. Original building specifications may not have included asbestos, but workers may have used asbestos material for convenience.

[p57 | prose | Avoiding making false assumptions]
For example:

[p57 | prose | Avoiding making false assumptions]
– asbestos-containing material off-cuts may have been used as filler or packing in places and their use not recorded

[p57 | prose | Avoiding making false assumptions]
– asbestos containing paint may have been applied to non-asbestos roofing material at a later date (so not recorded in the original building records).

[p58 | prose | 7.3 Previously inspected areas]
7.3 Previously inspected areas

[p58 | prose | 7.3 Previously inspected areas]
Surveyors should not presume an area is free of asbestos just because a removal has occurred previously.

[p58 | prose | 7.3 Previously inspected areas]
Poor asbestos removal practices can leave behind asbestos-containing debris (such as asbestos-contaminated dust).

[p58 | prose | 7.3 Previously inspected areas]
Always reinspect areas where asbestos was previously removed. This helps to make sure that any leftover asbestos material is identified. See Reinspection surveys for more information.

[p60 | prose | Survey reports should be clear, and easy to understand and interpret by the intended recipient.]
Survey reports should be clear, and easy to understand and interpret by the intended recipient.

[p60 | prose | 8.1 What is an asbestos survey report?]
8.1 What is an asbestos survey report?

[p60 | prose | 8.1 What is an asbestos survey report?]
The survey report is a record of the information collected at a particular time on the presence and condition of ACMs. It will contain information and data that will be used by other PCBUs to undertake an asbestos risk assessment and prepare an asbestos management or removal plan. Errors in the report could lead to incorrect conclusions and inappropriate decisions.

[p60 | prose | 8.1 What is an asbestos survey report?]
The survey report should contain a summary of the results in a format that can be used as the basis for an updatable register of ACMs (such as the asbestos register) and a diagram (such as building drawings) indicating the locations of ACMs. This register will need to be readily accessible to all involved in initiating maintenance or other work on the building or workplace.

[p60 | prose | 8.1 What is an asbestos survey report?]
For more information see WorkSafe’s webpage Managing asbestos in your building or workplace – for PCBUs

[p60 | prose | 8.1 What is an asbestos survey report?]
The report should be completed in a written format, supplied either as a hard copy or as an electronic document, or both. It should be presented in a way that is easily understandable by the commissioning PCBU. In particular, the information in the survey report should be easy for the commissioning PCBU to extract and to use to prepare an asbestos register. For example, by presenting the results in a manner or format that can be directly lifted or employed to form the asbestos register. The report should contain the results of sample analyses.

8.2 What an asbestos survey report should include

[p60 | prose | 8.2 What an asbestos survey report should include]
Asbestos survey reports should contain these sections:

[p60 | prose | 8.2 What an asbestos survey report should include]
– executive summary

[p60 | prose | 8.2 What an asbestos survey report should include]
– introduction covering the scope of work

[p60 | prose | 8.2 What an asbestos survey report should include]
– general site and survey information

[p60 | prose | 8.2 What an asbestos survey report should include]
– survey results (including material assessment results)

[p60 | prose | 8.2 What an asbestos survey report should include]
– bulk analysis results

– conclusions and actions.

In hard-copy documents, it can be useful to separate the report into different parts, with the bulk analysis results and the individual survey results, particularly if displayed with accompanying photos, contained in separate detachable appendices.

Using photographs

[p61 | prose | Using photographs]
Photos can be very informative to the commissioning PCBU and should be included in the report. Photos can show the material sampled, its condition and location and its surrounding environment. They can also be used to identify the actual sampling points.

[p61 | prose | Using photographs]
Photos provide a context for the sample and can assist the commissioning PCBU in managing asbestos. For example, by providing a benchmark for the comparison of condition over time.

8.3 Asbestos survey report: Executive summary

[p61 | prose | 8.3 Asbestos survey report: Executive summary]
The executive summary should provide an overview of the survey, highlighting the scope, type, and extent of the work conducted.

[p61 | prose | 8.3 Asbestos survey report: Executive summary]
It should summarise the most important findings and recommended actions. The key points should include:

[p61 | prose | 8.3 Asbestos survey report: Executive summary]
– locations with identified or presumed ACMs

[p61 | prose | 8.3 Asbestos survey report: Executive summary]

[p61 | prose | 8.3 Asbestos survey report: Executive summary]
– ACMs with high material assessment scores

[p61 | prose | 8.3 Asbestos survey report: Executive summary]
– clear and comprehensible notes on any conclusions, actions, and priorities.

[p61 | prose | 8.4 Asbestos survey report: Introduction]
8.4 Asbestos survey report: Introduction

[p67 | prose | What is a quality management system?]
What is a quality management system?

[p67 | prose | What is a quality management system?]
A quality management system is a structured framework that sets out the procedures, processes, and responsibilities for achieving quality objectives.

[p67 | prose | What is a quality management system?]
An effective quality management system helps an organisation to consistently deliver high-quality services that meet commissioning PCBUs’ and regulatory requirements.

[p70 | prose | Asbestos surveyors should select the correct tools and equipment for any work on asbestos material.]
Asbestos surveyors should select the correct tools and equipment for any work on asbestos material.

[p70 | prose | 10.1 Use the right tools and equipment]
10.1 Use the right tools and equipment

[p70 | prose | 10.1 Use the right tools and equipment]
Care should be taken when choosing the appropriate tool for surveying and sampling.

[p70 | prose | 10.1 Use the right tools and equipment]
Tools and equipment that generate dust may be used, but under strict controls. These are referred to as controlled tools and equipment.

[p70 | prose | 10.1 Use the right tools and equipment]
Some tools and equipment, such as high-pressure water sprayers and air compressors, are prohibited for any use on asbestos. These are unlikely to be needed for surveying work.

[p70 | prose | 10.1 Use the right tools and equipment]
For a list of the tools and equipment commonly used in surveying and sampling, see Appendix 3: Example of a survey and sampling equipment checklist

[p70 | prose | 10.2 Controlled tools and equipment]
10.2 Controlled tools and equipment

[p70 | prose | 10.2 Controlled tools and equipment]
Power tools and other equipment (including, drills, brushes, or brooms) may only be used on asbestos material if:

[p70 | prose | 10.2 Controlled tools and equipment]
– the equipment is enclosed while being used

[p70 | prose | 10.2 Controlled tools and equipment]
– the equipment is designed to capture or suppress airborne asbestos, and is used according to manufacturer’s instructions

[p70 | prose | 10.2 Controlled tools and equipment]
– the equipment is used in a way designed to capture or suppress airborne asbestos safely, such as through engineering control measures like dust suppression or extraction ventilation

[p70 | prose | 10.2 Controlled tools and equipment]
– a combination of the above.

[p70 | prose | 10.2 Controlled tools and equipment]
Where possible, use non-powered hand tools. If more force is needed, consider using low-speed, battery-powered tools that can be used with wetting methods to minimise the release of dust.

[p70 | prose | 10.2 Controlled tools and equipment]
When using power tools with dust suppression or extraction, air monitoring may need to be carried out to ensure the controls in place are effective in reducing the release of fibres.

[p70 | prose | 10.2 Controlled tools and equipment]
For more information see Section 4.0: Asbestos assessments – good practice guidelines

[p71 | prose | 10.3 Prevent tool contamination]
10.3 Prevent tool contamination

[p71 | prose | 10.3 Prevent tool contamination]
There is a risk of cross-contamination when tools are used for surveying and sampling.

[p71 | prose | 10.3 Prevent tool contamination]
All tools and equipment used for sampling suspected ACMs must be thoroughly decontaminated. If thorough cleaning is not possible due to hard-to-reach areas like hollows, grooves, or dust reservoirs, the tools should be cleaned as much as possible.

[p71 | prose | 10.3 Prevent tool contamination]
The tools should then be sealed in a marked bag or container and clearly labelled to indicate asbestos contamination.

[p71 | prose | 10.3 Prevent tool contamination]
For more information see Section 16.2: Asbestos removal – good practice guidelines

[p71 | prose | 10.4 Industrial vacuum cleaners]
10.4 Industrial vacuum cleaners

[p71 | prose | 10.4 Industrial vacuum cleaners]
Vacuum cleaners used for hazardous dusts such as asbestos, must be fit for purpose to make sure the dust is safely captured and contained.

[p71 | prose | 10.4 Industrial vacuum cleaners]
Domestic or standard commercial vacuums are not suitable for this purpose, regardless of the filter used.

[p71 | prose | 10.4 Industrial vacuum cleaners]
Industrial vacuums rated for use with hazardous dusts are classified as L, M, or H, which refer to the hazard rating: light, medium, or high hazard.

[p71 | prose | 10.4 Industrial vacuum cleaners]
The commonly used vacuum by surveyors is the H-type. This is used to shadow vacuum when taking a sample to capture fugitive dust at a particular source and used at the early stages of decontamination.

[p71 | prose | 10.4 Industrial vacuum cleaners]
Licensed asbestos assessors and licensed asbestos removalists use the H-type vacuum to control the risk of exposure.

[p71 | prose | 10.4 Industrial vacuum cleaners]
Asbestos is classified as a high hazard, so only H-class industrial vacuums should be used for work involving asbestos material (including asbestos- contaminated dust).

[p71 | prose | 10.4 Industrial vacuum cleaners]
High-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filter does not mean H-class.

[p71 | prose | 10.5 Vacuum cleaner filters]
10.5 Vacuum cleaner filters

[p71 | prose | 10.5 Vacuum cleaner filters]
Filters used in vacuum cleaners for hazardous dusts must:

[p71 | prose | 10.5 Vacuum cleaner filters]
– be designed to fit the specific model of the vacuum cleaner being used

[p71 | prose | 10.5 Vacuum cleaner filters]
– achieve the same or higher filtration efficiency that the vacuum cleaner is rated for.

[p71 | prose | 10.5 Vacuum cleaner filters]
Class H14 vacuum cleaners used for asbestos work should not be used on wet materials or surfaces unless designed for that type of work.

[p71 | prose | 10.5 Vacuum cleaner filters]
For more information on how to select the right industrial vacuum or filter, or how to maintain a vacuum, see WorkSafe’s webpage Industrial vacuums and portable extractors for hazardous dust

[p72 | prose | 10.6 Maintaining and transporting vacuum cleaners used for asbestos]
10.6 Maintaining and transporting vacuum cleaners used for asbestos

[p72 | prose | 10.6 Maintaining and transporting vacuum cleaners used for asbestos]
When vacuum cleaners have been used with asbestos, removal and disposal of the contents must be performed by a competent person. Vacuum cleaners should be maintained according to the manufacturer’s guidance, which can include DOP (Dispersed Oil Particulate) testing.

[p72 | prose | 10.6 Maintaining and transporting vacuum cleaners used for asbestos]
DOP testing ensures HEPA filters in H-class vacuums and negative pressure units work properly by detecting particle leaks, ensuring no harmful fibres are released.

[p72 | prose | 10.6 Maintaining and transporting vacuum cleaners used for asbestos]
H-Class vacuum cleaners used on asbestos should be inspected and tested at least annually.

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
Competent person

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
A competent person is someone who has the appropriate skills, training, knowledge, and experience to perform the task or role.

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
The competent person should:

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
– wear personal protective equipment (PPE), including appropriate respiratory protective equipment (RPE) (which has been fit-tested)

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
– seal or cordon off an appropriate area to prevent unnecessary dust exposure to others

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
– make sure the dust bag has been removed and disposed of first

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
– use a damp cloth to clean the dust off the outside of the vacuum, and any inside parts that can be accessed

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
– dispose of dust and containment bags and contaminated damp cloths as asbestos waste in tightly sealed and labelled bags or containers

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
– only dispose of asbestos waste at authorised asbestos disposal sites.

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
For a more thorough clean, it can also be cleaned using another industrial vacuum cleaner.

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
Only use this method if the other vacuum cleaner is rated at the same class, or higher. A licensed asbestos assessor could perform this function in a controlled environment.

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
Dry brushing or using compressed air should never be used to clean vacuums. These methods cause the hazardous dust to spread and become airborne. Compressed air can also damage the filters, making them ineffective.

[p72 | prose | Competent person]
Treat vacuum cleaners used for asbestos work as asbestos waste during transportation. This means double bagging the vacuum cleaners and any hoses and attachments in suitable asbestos waste bags, clearly identified as containing asbestos.

[p96 | prose | ACETYLENE CYLINDERS]
ACETYLENE CYLINDERS

[p96 | prose | ACETYLENE CYLINDERS]
Acetylene cylinders are filled with a porous solid, then acetylene is added as a solution in liquid acetone to help dilute the gas to reduce the risk of them exploding. This is different from most other compressed gases.

[p96 | prose | ACETYLENE CYLINDERS]
Early cylinders were completely filled with diatomaceous earth, charcoal, asbestos, and cement. The earth and charcoal made the material porous, the asbestos added strength, and the cement held it all together. By 1994, cylinders were usually filled with silica and lime, though some manufacturers still added asbestos, charcoal, or other materials.

[p97 | prose | Sprayed coatings]
Sprayed coatings

[p97 | prose | Sprayed coatings]
These are typically coatings sprayed or trowelled onto surfaces. Often used for fire protection and thermal or acoustic insulation in larger scale commercial and industrial buildings, as well as decorative purposes. The depth of the spray usually varies from 10 to 150 mm thick. Dry sprayed coatings may have a candyfloss appearance if left untamped. Wet sprayed/trowelled coatings are usually denser. Coatings with higher proportions of Portland cement that have been well tamped can be quite hard. Surfaces may be sealed with an elasticised paint or proprietary encapsulant, reinforced with calico or man-made fibre mesh, or left completely unsealed.

[p97 | prose | Sprayed coatings]
Material description/uses Normally homogeneous coatings sprayed or trowelled onto surfaces

[p97 | prose | Sprayed coatings]
Used for: – fireproofing – thermal/anti condensation insulation – acoustic insulation

[p97 | prose | Sprayed coatings]
Locations – Underside of roofs and sides of industrial buildings – Widely used in the marine industry – On steel and reinforced concrete beams/columns and on underside of floors – Sprayed onto walls and ceilings for acoustic and decorative purposes. For example: – theatres – cinemas – studios – halls – Commonly found adjacent surfaces resulting from overspray of target areas

[p97 | prose | Sprayed coatings]
Asbestos type/material name – Sprayed coatings usually contain 55%–85% asbestos with a Portland cement binder – Sprayed coatings such as Vermiculite typically contain chrysotile, but other sprayed coatings may contain all three common asbestos types – Common brand names include Isolantite, Limpet, Monokote, and Super Six

[p97 | prose | Sprayed coatings]
Ease of fibre release – Sprayed coatings are considered a friable acm and may release airborne asbestos fibres if disturbed – Sprayed coatings are vulnerable to accidental damage and delamination due to water leakage – Damaged sprayed coatings may release debris onto the floor and other horizontal surfaces below – Dust released may accumulate on false ceilings, wiring and ventilation systems – Unsealed surfaces have a higher risk of fibre release if disturbed
