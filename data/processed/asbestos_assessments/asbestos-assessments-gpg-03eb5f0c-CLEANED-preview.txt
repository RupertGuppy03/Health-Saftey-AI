[p11 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
These guidelines provide good practice advice for conducting asbestos assessments relating to asbestos removal activities in buildings or workplaces. This includes air monitoring and clearance inspections.

[p11 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
The guidelines will help people conducting a business or undertaking (PCBUs) and asbestos assessors (assessors) to comply with:

[p11 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
– the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)

[p11 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
– the Health and Safety at Work (Asbestos) Regulations 2016 (Regulations)

[p11 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
– the Health and Safety at Work (General Risk and Workplace Management) Regulations 2016.

[p11 | prose | 1.1 What are these guidelines about?]
These guidelines do not cover the assessment of asbestos in soils. For more information, see New Zealand Guidelines for Assessing and Managing Asbestos in Soil

[p12 | prose | Competent persons]

[p12 | prose | Competent persons]

[p12 | prose | Competent persons]

[p12 | prose | Competent persons]

[p13 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)

[p13 | prose | 1.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA)]
HSWA is the primary work health and safety legislation in New Zealand. HSWA applies to all work and workplaces unless specifically excluded.

[p13 | prose | Primary duty of care]
Primary duty of care

[p13 | prose | Primary duty of care]
All PCBUs (including assessors) have a primary duty of care under HSWA. The primary duty of care means that, so far as is reasonably practicable, PCBUs must ensure the health and safety of:

[p13 | prose | Primary duty of care]
– its workers (such as employees, contractors, subcontractors, apprentices)

[p13 | prose | Primary duty of care]
– any other workers who are influenced or directed by the PCBU, (such as workers of other PCBUs on the same site).

[p13 | prose | Primary duty of care]
Assessors must so far as is reasonably practicable make sure their assessments and reports are thorough and accurate enough for PCBUs to rely on when managing asbestos.

[p13 | prose | Primary duty of care]
Self-employed persons are also considered PCBUs, and the primary duty of care applies. They must also ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, their own health and safety while at work.

[p13 | prose | Primary duty of care]
For more information, see WorkSafe’s webpage Introduction to the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 – special guide

[p13 | prose | Overlapping duties]
Overlapping duties

[p13 | prose | Overlapping duties]
When working with other asbestos professionals in a contracting chain (such as with building owners, and removalists) assessors are likely to have overlapping duties.

[p13 | prose | Overlapping duties]
Overlapping duties is where a PCBU may be managing similar risks to other PCBUs in the same environment, or while involved in the same work activity.

[p13 | prose | Overlapping duties]
Under HSWA, where there are overlapping duties, all PCBUs involved must, so far as is reasonably practicable:

[p13 | prose | Overlapping duties]
– consult each other

[p13 | prose | Overlapping duties]
– cooperate with each other

[p13 | prose | Overlapping duties]
– coordinate their activities.

[p13 | prose | Overlapping duties]
This is to make sure all workers across all the PCBUs, (and other people) are not put at risk before, during, and after the asbestos removal procedure. Examples are given in these guideline where assessors and removalists should work together to manage risk.

[p13 | prose | Overlapping duties]
For more information, see WorkSafe’s webpages Overlapping duties and PCBUs working together: advice when contracting

Asbestos assessors conduct air monitoring during and after asbestos removal. They also undertake clearance inspections and issue clearance certificates if the area where asbestos removal work took place is safe for reoccupation.

[p16 | prose | 2.1 Who can carry out asbestos assessor work]
2.1 Who can carry out asbestos assessor work

[p16 | prose | 2.1 Who can carry out asbestos assessor work]
Two groups can carry out assessor work: licensed assessors and competent persons.

[p16 | prose | 2.2 Licensed asbestos assessors]
2.2 Licensed asbestos assessors

[p16 | prose | 2.2 Licensed asbestos assessors]
Licensed asbestos assessors are responsible for:

[p16 | prose | 2.2 Licensed asbestos assessors]
– air monitoring during asbestos removal (Class A or B)

[p16 | prose | 2.2 Licensed asbestos assessors]
– clearance inspections for asbestos removal (Class A or B)

[p16 | prose | 2.2 Licensed asbestos assessors]
– issuing clearance certificates for asbestos removal (Class A or B).

[p16 | prose | 2.2 Licensed asbestos assessors]
A licensed asbestos assessor may also perform other activities under a contract, such as reviewing an asbestos removalist’s work plan before removal starts to make sure it is suitable.

[p16 | prose | 2.2 Licensed asbestos assessors]
Sometimes, assessors may also assist with responding to discoveries of asbestos during the work and then reviewing the amendments to the workplan.

[p16 | prose | 2.2 Licensed asbestos assessors]
Only licensed assessors can carry out asbestos assessor work for Class A asbestos removal.

[p16 | prose | 2.2 Licensed asbestos assessors]
Licensed asbestos assessors are authorised by WorkSafe to assess if asbestos removal work is completed and if the area where asbestos removal took place meets the clearance certificate criteria and therefore is safe for reoccupation.

[p16 | prose | 2.2 Licensed asbestos assessors]
Asbestos assessor licences are granted to individuals, not to PCBUs. WorkSafe manages the licensing process.

[p16 | prose | 2.2 Licensed asbestos assessors]
For more information, see WorkSafe’s webpage Asbestos licensing

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
2.3 When a licence is not required

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
A licence is not required for:

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
– air monitoring during Class B asbestos removal

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
– clearance inspections for Class B asbestos removal

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
– issuing clearance certificates for Class B asbestos removal.

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
Even though a licence is not needed, an independent competent person must do these activities.

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
A competent person for the purposes of doing clearance inspections is a person who has acquired, through training and experience, the knowledge and skills of relevant asbestos removal industry practice and who holds:

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
– a certificate in relation to a training course specified by WorkSafe for asbestos assessor work, or

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
– a tertiary qualification in occupational health and safety, occupational hygiene, science, or environmental health.

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
They must hold either of the following:

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
– a certificate from a training course, specified by WorkSafe for asbestos assessor work (BOHS P404, BOHS IP404 or NZQA Unit Standard 29768)

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
– a tertiary qualification in occupational health and safety, occupational hygiene, science, or environmental health.

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
They can demonstrate relevant industry experience including asbestos surveying, air monitoring, and clearance inspections undertaken under supervision of a licensed asbestos assessor. Minimum relevant industry experience for a competent person would be:

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
– 6 months for clearance inspections in domestic premises

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
– 12 months for clearance inspections in commercial premises

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
– 18 months for clearance inspections in industrial premises (including plant and machinery)

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
– This can be evidenced using (for example) task logs and employment history.

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
Note: In these guidelines the term ‘assessor’ will be used to mean licensed assessors and competent persons. Where guidance is specifically aimed at a licensed assessors only, the term ‘licenced assessor’ will be used.

[p17 | prose | 2.3 When a licence is not required]
See Section 8.3 of The Health and Safety at Work (Asbestos) Regulations 2016 – interpretive guidelines for more information about the duties of assessors under the regulations.

[p17 | prose | 2.4 Licence requirements when friability status changes during removal]
2.4 Licence requirements when friability status changes during removal

[p17 | prose | 2.4 Licence requirements when friability status changes during removal]
In most cases the friable and non-friable status of asbestos is determined by the condition of the asbestos before it is disturbed for removal.

[p17 | prose | 2.4 Licence requirements when friability status changes during removal]
However, in some cases the classification of friability of the asbestos can change based on the method of removal used. Table 1 outlines examples of where the method of removal may change the friability and therefore the conditions under which it can or must be removed and assessed.

[p18 | table | 2.4 Licence requirements when friability status changes during removal]
| PRODUCT TYPE AND ORIGINAL FRIABILITY | CAN BE TREATED AS NON-FRIABLE AND BE REMOVED AND ASSESSED UNDER CLASS B CONDITIONS IF: | MUST BE TREATED AS FRIABLE AND REMOVED AND ASSESSED UNDER CLASS A CONDITIONS IF: |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hot water cylinder with internal asbestos insulation Friable | Removed and disposed of as a complete unit and the asbestos inside remains fully contained. | Cylinder is opened up or broken down in any way for removal or disposal. |
| Fire door with AIB core Friable | Removed and disposed of as a complete unit and the asbestos inside remains fully contained. | The core of the door is exposed in any way during removal or disposal. This includes where removed screws or hardware expose the core. |
| Asbestos-containing paper backing attached to a non-asbestos vinyl Friable | Removed and disposed of as one piece with no disturbance to the paper backing (this will typically be limited to small rooms as the piece will need to fit through the door opening). | The paper backing is cut, torn or in any other way disturbed or separated from the vinyl it is attached to enable removal or disposal. |
| Asbestos-containing vinyl flooring (the asbestos is present in the vinyl matrix itself) Non-friable | Removed and disposed of as a single piece and no asbestos-containing adhesive is left behind that cannot be removed by solvent (this will typically be limited to small rooms as the piece will need to fit through the door opening). | The vinyl is cut or torn to enable removal, or if any remaining asbestos-containing adhesive requires power tools to remove. |
| Asbestos rope inside a chimney Friable | Chimney removed and disposed of as one piece with no disturbance to the rope. | The chimney needs to be broken down into pieces to enable removal and disposal. |
| Textured coating Non-friable | Removed and disposed of while still attached to the substrate without disturbing the textured coating (this will typically be limited to small rooms as the piece will need to fit through the door opening). | The textured coating is disturbed via manual or mechanical scraping/lifting or cutting through the ceiling/wall. |
| ‘Popcorn’ ceiling coating Friable | Removed and disposed of while still attached to the substrate without disturbing the textured coating (this will typically be limited to small rooms as the piece will need to fit through the door opening). | The ceiling surface is disturbed via manual or mechanical scraping/lifting or cutting through the ceiling. |

[p18 | prose | 2.5 Licence requirements for assessing asbestos containing dust (ACD) removal]
2.5 Licence requirements for assessing asbestos containing dust (ACD) removal

[p18 | prose | 2.5 Licence requirements for assessing asbestos containing dust (ACD) removal]
How the removal of ACD is assessed and who can do the clearance depends on whether the ACD was required to be removed under Class A or Class B licence conditions.

[p18 | prose | 2.5 Licence requirements for assessing asbestos containing dust (ACD) removal]
Asbestos-contaminated dust or debris (ACD) means dust or debris that has settled within a workplace and is, or is assumed to be, contaminated with asbestos.

[p18 | prose | 2.5 Licence requirements for assessing asbestos containing dust (ACD) removal]
Removal of ACD is treated separately under the regulations to asbestos removal. Regulatory requirements for the removal of ACD depends on how the ADC was created and how much is present.

[p18 | prose | 2.5 Licence requirements for assessing asbestos containing dust (ACD) removal]
ACD must be removed by a Class A asbestos removalist and assessed by a licensed asbestos assessor unless it is:

[p18 | prose | 2.5 Licence requirements for assessing asbestos containing dust (ACD) removal]
– associated with non-friable asbestos removal, or

[p18 | prose | 2.5 Licence requirements for assessing asbestos containing dust (ACD) removal]
– a ‘minor contamination’ not associated with friable or non-friable asbestos removal.

[p19 | prose | Clearances for ACD associated with removal of non-friable asbestos]
Clearances for ACD associated with removal of non-friable asbestos

[p19 | prose | Clearances for ACD associated with removal of non-friable asbestos]
ACD associated with removal of non-friable asbestos means ACD generated by or discovered during asbestos removal activities. These activities include site set up/pre clean and non-friable asbestos removal. For example: when removing an asbestos cement roof and settled ACD is discovered after lifting roof material (this can be dealt with under the Class B licence, and clearance done by a competent person).

[p19 | prose | Clearances for ACD associated with removal of asbestos that was originally non-friable, but condition has deteriorated to become friable]
Clearances for ACD associated with removal of asbestos that was originally non-friable, but condition has deteriorated to become friable

[p19 | prose | Clearances for ACD associated with removal of asbestos that was originally non-friable, but condition has deteriorated to become friable]
Where accumulated quantities of ACD have been generated by a non-friable asbestos source, and there is a sufficient quantity of ACD that it is identifiable before work has started, it should be assumed that the nature of the source material has changed due to deterioration or damage (accidental or due to an event such as fire). The source material will need to be reclassified as friable. The removal of it, and the ACD generated by it, must be undertaken by a Class A removalist. Clearance must be done by a licensed assessor.

[p19 | prose | Clearances for ACD not associated with removal]
Clearances for ACD not associated with removal

[p19 | prose | Clearances for ACD not associated with removal]
ACD not associated with removal includes where historically asbestos material was or might have been present. In this scenario ACD needs to be seen as a standalone asbestos contamination. Unless it is a minor contamination, it must be removed by a Class A removalist and clearance done by an independent licensed assessor.

[p19 | prose | Clearances for ACD not associated with removal]
See Section 4.2 of the Asbestos removal – good practice guidelines for more information and examples of what is considered a ‘minor contamination’.

[p20 | prose | 2.6 Independence and conflicts of interest]
2.6 Independence and conflicts of interest

[p20 | prose | 2.6 Independence and conflicts of interest]
Clearance inspections for licensed asbestos removal work must be done by an independent licensed asbestos assessor (for Class A removal work) or an independent competent person (for Class B removal work).

[p20 | prose | 2.6 Independence and conflicts of interest]
– At non-residential premises, the PCBU who commissions the licensed removal work must make sure that a clearance inspection of the asbestos removal area at is carried out by an independent licensed asbestos assessor/competent person after the removal worked has been completed.

[p20 | prose | 2.6 Independence and conflicts of interest]
– For homes that are not a workplace, the asbestos removalist must make sure that a clearance inspection of the asbestos removal area at is carried out by an independent licensed asbestos assessor/competent person after the removal worked has been completed .

[p20 | prose | 2.6 Independence and conflicts of interest]
– Any actual, perceived, or potential conflicts of interest should also be avoided, or appropriately managed.

[p20 | prose | Making sure the work is independent]
Making sure the work is independent

[p20 | prose | Making sure the work is independent]
To make sure asbestos removal is carried out to the required standard, the asbestos removalist and asbestos assessor or competent person should come from different businesses. This helps make sure the commissioning PCBU receives a fair and impartial service.

[p20 | prose | Making sure the work is independent]
To remain objective and impartial, an asbestos assessor or competent person cannot be unduly influenced or controlled by others when they carry out their regulated activities. Independence is unlikely to exist where:

[p20 | prose | Making sure the work is independent]
– there is a commercial or business ownership link between the assessor and removalist

[p20 | prose | Making sure the work is independent]
– there is a direct familial link between the removalist and assessor, for example, parent and child.

[p20 | prose | Managing conflicts of interest]
Managing conflicts of interest

[p20 | prose | Managing conflicts of interest]
Conflicts of interest, whether actual, perceived, or potential should be avoided where possible. Conflicts of interest can be financial or non-financial. Any actual, perceived, or potential conflict of interest that cannot be avoided will need to be acknowledged and appropriately managed:

[p20 | prose | Managing conflicts of interest]
– It should be clearly disclosed/declared to the commissioning PCBU, and a plan put in place to manage it. Ways to manage a conflict of interest will vary depending on the circumstances but may include:

[p20 | prose | Managing conflicts of interest]
including the commissioning PCBU in all communications between the removalist and assessor

[p20 | prose | Managing conflicts of interest]
- having the assessment peer-reviewed by a different independent assessor.

[p20 | prose | Managing conflicts of interest]
– If a conflict cannot be managed, a different assessor needs to be engaged.

[p25 | prose | 4.1 Purpose of air monitoring]
4.1 Purpose of air monitoring

[p25 | prose | 4.1 Purpose of air monitoring]
Air monitoring during and after asbestos removal detects respirable fibres and measures how many are present.

[p25 | prose | 4.1 Purpose of air monitoring]
This information is used to assess if control measures are working during removal and to confirm there is no risk from airborne asbestos left after removal has finished (during clearance procedures). It is critical for keeping workers and the public safe, especially during and after Class A asbestos removal.

[p25 | prose | 4.2 Air monitoring requirements]
4.2 Air monitoring requirements

[p25 | prose | 4.2 Air monitoring requirements]
Class A licensed asbestos removal

[p25 | prose | 4.2 Air monitoring requirements]
Air monitoring must be done:

[p25 | prose | 4.2 Air monitoring requirements]
– immediately before Class A asbestos removal starts if the licensed assessor determines that the air may contain respirable asbestos fibres in concentrations greater than trace levels, and

[p25 | prose | 4.2 Air monitoring requirements]
– while the licensed asbestos removal work is carried out and in a place that is adjacent to any negative pressure enclosure.

[p25 | prose | 4.2 Air monitoring requirements]
While asbestos work is being carried out means continuously (every day for the entirety of the asbestos removal shift duration) until the removal is complete and clearance inspection commences.

[p25 | prose | Class B asbestos removal and unlicensed asbestos removal]
Class B asbestos removal and unlicensed asbestos removal

[p25 | prose | Class B asbestos removal and unlicensed asbestos removal]

[p25 | prose | Class B asbestos removal and unlicensed asbestos removal]
– complying with their duty to eliminate or minimise exposure to airborne asbestos

[p25 | prose | Class B asbestos removal and unlicensed asbestos removal]
– not exceeding the airborne contamination standard for asbestos.

[p25 | prose | Class B asbestos removal and unlicensed asbestos removal]
Air monitoring should also be considered if the Class B removal is happening in or near a public area. For more information see Section 5.1 When sampling might be needed for Class B or unlicensed removal

[p25 | prose | ASSURANCE FOR ASBESTOS-RELATED WORK AND AFTER SURVEY SAMPLING]
Asbestos-related work is work involving asbestos other than asbestos removal. It includes work that may involve the disturbance of asbestos – for example drilling into a soffit to install a light or installing ducting for a heat pump against asbestos-containing cladding.

[p26 | prose | ASSURANCE FOR ASBESTOS-RELATED WORK AND AFTER SURVEY SAMPLING]

Measuring airborne fibre concentrations is key to managing the risk of workers being exposed to asbestos and ensuring that the control measures adopted during asbestos removal are adequate.

[p29 | prose | 5.1 Measure fibre concentrations to keep workers safe]
5.1 Measure fibre concentrations to keep workers safe

[p29 | prose | 5.1 Measure fibre concentrations to keep workers safe]
Measuring airborne asbestos fibres involves collecting particulates from a measured volume of air using a filter and pump. The filter is then examined under a microscope to count the airborne fibres, which is used to calculate the airborne respirable fibre concentration.

[p29 | prose | 5.1 Measure fibre concentrations to keep workers safe]
The results of this air sampling can provide assurance that:

[p29 | prose | 5.1 Measure fibre concentrations to keep workers safe]
– work practices are effective at minimising the amount or airborne particulates being released

[p29 | prose | 5.1 Measure fibre concentrations to keep workers safe]
– that enclosures are working properly

[p29 | prose | 5.1 Measure fibre concentrations to keep workers safe]
– that respiratory protective equipment (RPE) in use will be sufficient to protect workers from harmful exposure.

[p29 | prose | 5.1 Measure fibre concentrations to keep workers safe]
There are two types of air sampling methods:

[p29 | prose | Personal air sampling]
Personal air sampling

[p29 | prose | Personal air sampling]
Personal air sampling of workers is required under GRWM Regulations unless their exposure is not likely to exceed the airborne contamination standard for asbestos.

[p29 | prose | Personal air sampling]
Where personal air sampling is needed, it should cover a range of jobs and work methods, especially higher-risk activities, and be done regularly.

[p29 | prose | Personal air sampling]
In personal air sampling, the filter is placed in the person’s breathing zone.

[p29 | prose | Static air sampling]
Static air sampling

[p29 | prose | Static air sampling]
Static air sampling involves monitoring the air at fixed locations. For example, it can help assess a source, potential spread, or the control and containment of asbestos in certain situations such as leak testing of enclosures.

[p29 | prose | Static air sampling]
Static air sampling is also a key part of clearance inspection after Class A licensed removal work.

[p29 | prose | Static air sampling]
For more information on personal and static air sampling methods, see Appendix 4: Air monitoring and air sampling equipment

[p30 | prose | Trace level]
Trace level

[p30 | prose | Trace level]
Meeting the trace level threshold does not mean the air is completely free of asbestos. Some fibres may remain for a short time after removal but will naturally reduce through dilution, dispersion, and settling.

[p30 | prose | Trace level]
Airborne fibre levels will return to natural background levels over time as fibres disperse and settle. The trace level sets the maximum allowable fibre concentration after Class A asbestos removal.

[p37 | prose | Background and reassurance air sampling]
These tests measure airborne fibre levels.

[p37 | prose | Background and reassurance air sampling]
– Background air sampling is done before an activity, such as asbestos removal or remediation, to establish baseline fibre levels. If the baseline fibre concentration exceeds trace level, consideration should be given to adopt qualitative methods of fibre counting and to using buffer zones (RPE required for access) around the primary asbestos removal area. Buffer zones in such instances are classed as part of the asbestos removal area (secondary containment).

[p37 | prose | Background and reassurance air sampling]
– Reassurance air sampling is done after an asbestos incident, when material has been accidentally disturbed, or found during or after the removal, or when taking down the enclosure after the four-stage clearance. It should only be done once the area is confirmed to be visually clean of debris and dust.

Static air sampling may be needed to assess different situations and sources of asbestos release. For example:

[p38 | prose | Near-source static air sampling]
– specific areas inside large structures, such as large-scale industrial buildings

[p38 | prose | Near-source static air sampling]
– releases from known outdoor sources (such as asbestos disposal or waste sites), which may need multiple air sampling locations upwind and downwind to assess fibre release from changing wind directions and nearby fibre sources

[p38 | prose | Near-source static air sampling]
– tasks in very dusty operations

[p38 | prose | Near-source static air sampling]
– checking how well dust suppression methods are working

[p38 | prose | Near-source static air sampling]
– situations that need long-term air sampling (such as several 8-hour samples over a week to calculate a more accurate average exposure).

[p38 | prose | Near-source static air sampling]
When setting the air sampling parameters, consider the expected dust and fibre concentrations, air sampling time, air volume, and position or location.

[p38 | prose | Near-source static air sampling]
Where possible, collect larger air sampling volumes, to improve sensitivity. In many cases, longer air sampling periods will improve sensitivity and make the results more representative.

[p39 | prose | Far-source/perimeter/ambient air sampling]
Far-source/perimeter/ambient air sampling

[p39 | prose | Far-source/perimeter/ambient air sampling]
Far-source and related air sampling is often done for reassurance or to check conditions such as when an activity may release asbestos fibres. Where possible, these activities should still be controlled and dust suppression practices used. Samples may be taken for the following reasons (this is not a complete list):

[p39 | prose | Far-source/perimeter/ambient air sampling]
– around the exclusion zone of buildings containing asbestos after a fire

[p39 | prose | Far-source/perimeter/ambient air sampling]
– at the edge of an exclusion zone for demolition work where asbestos materials have remained because of the building’s condition, such as being fire-damaged, or being derelict or unsafe

[p39 | prose | Far-source/perimeter/ambient air sampling]
– around the exclusion zone of sites where external asbestos removal is taking place, and full enclosure is not possible

[p39 | prose | Far-source/perimeter/ambient air sampling]
– to assess the external background fibres levels.

[p39 | prose | Far-source/perimeter/ambient air sampling]
As concentrations are likely to be low, increase the sample volume of air to ensure representative results. In many cases, longer air sampling times may be possible, giving a better chance to detect peak release events and a more accurate estimate of average concentrations at the site perimeter.

[p39 | prose | Far-source/perimeter/ambient air sampling]
At low concentrations, the proportion of asbestos fibres in the PCM count may be much lower than in near-source situations. Fibre identification may be necessary to get reliable asbestos concentration results. For more information, see Appendix 4: Air monitoring and air sampling equipment

[p39 | prose | 5.4 Air sampling and analysis using phase contrast microscopy (PCM)]
5.4 Air sampling and analysis using phase contrast microscopy (PCM)

[p39 | prose | 5.4 Air sampling and analysis using phase contrast microscopy (PCM)]
PCM is the standard method used to sample and count fibres in air (see Figure 7). Air is drawn through a filter (the flow rate) for a set time, to collect airborne fibres and particles.

[p39 | prose | 5.4 Air sampling and analysis using phase contrast microscopy (PCM)]
The filter is then placed on a glass slide and made transparent to be examined under a microscope. A known part of the filtered deposit is examined using PCM at 500x magnification or more. All visible fibres within specific microscope graticule areas are counted. Only fibres longer than 5 m, narrower than 3 m, and with a length-to-width ratio (aspect ratio) over 3:1 are included.

[p39 | prose | 5.4 Air sampling and analysis using phase contrast microscopy (PCM)]
The fibre concentration (in f/ml) is calculated by dividing the number of fibres collected on the exposed area of the filter by the volume of air sampled.

6.4 Four-stage clearance procedure for clearance inspection

[p45 | prose | 6.4 Four-stage clearance procedure for clearance inspection]
Asbestos clearance procedures can be divided into four stages. For Class A removal all four stages must be completed and passed before an assessor can issue a clearance certificate.

[p45 | prose | 6.4 Four-stage clearance procedure for clearance inspection]

[p45 | prose | 6.4 Four-stage clearance procedure for clearance inspection]

[p45 | prose | 6.4 Four-stage clearance procedure for clearance inspection]
– an enclosure was used. Stage three surface testing should only be done if an enclosure is in place

[p45 | prose | 6.4 Four-stage clearance procedure for clearance inspection]
– a new removal methodology is being trialled, and it is unknown if potential contamination will fall below the airborne contamination standard for asbestos

[p45 | prose | 6.4 Four-stage clearance procedure for clearance inspection]
– the removal work is happening near a sensitive area, such as a school or hospital.

[p45 | prose | 6.4 Four-stage clearance procedure for clearance inspection]

[p45 | prose | 6.4 Four-stage clearance procedure for clearance inspection]

– no visible dust or debris remains on floors or surfaces

[p45 | prose | 6.4 Four-stage clearance procedure for clearance inspection]
– airborne fibre levels are below 0.01 f/ml (Class A removal only)

[p45 | prose | 6.4 Four-stage clearance procedure for clearance inspection]
– the asbestos removal area does not pose a risk to health and safety from exposure to asbestos.

[p45 | prose | 6.4 Four-stage clearance procedure for clearance inspection]

Decontamination after leaving an enclosure or removal area helps control the spread of asbestos fibres.

[p52 | prose | 7.1 Decontamination levels]
7.1 Decontamination levels

[p52 | prose | 7.1 Decontamination levels]
Decontamination is necessary to make sure assessors, their PPE, and RPE are free of asbestos fibres before leaving the enclosure or work area. It helps prevent the spread of asbestos outside the enclosure or work area.

[p52 | prose | 7.1 Decontamination levels]
Nothing that is likely to be contaminated with asbestos should be removed from the asbestos removal area unless it:

[p52 | prose | 7.1 Decontamination levels]
– is decontaminated before being removed

[p52 | prose | 7.1 Decontamination levels]
– is sealed in a container that has been decontaminated on the exterior and clearly labelled to show it may contain asbestos.

[p52 | prose | 7.1 Decontamination levels]
Decontamination is also important for preventing others who come into contact with asbestos workers, equipment, or waste from getting secondary contamination. Family members can be exposed to asbestos if contaminated clothing is taken home.

[p52 | prose | 7.1 Decontamination levels]
Assessors must follow decontamination procedures when entering and exiting asbestos enclosures or work areas. Assessors should use the decontamination facilities already provided on-site by the removalist. Details of the requirements for decontamination facilities are covered in Appendix 3: Design criteria for asbestos decontamination units (DCUs) of the Asbestos removal – good practice guidelines

[p52 | prose | 7.1 Decontamination levels]
Enclosures should only be entered when absolutely necessary. Some tasks, such as observing personal air monitoring equipment can be done from outside by looking through enclosure viewing panels or by using CCTV.

[p52 | prose | 7.1 Decontamination levels]
There are two levels of decontamination, depending on the level of contamination:

[p52 | prose | 7.1 Decontamination levels]
– Preliminary decontamination can be used when contamination is minimal. It involves decontamination within the airlock system of an enclosure (see Section 7.3 for examples).

[p52 | prose | 7.1 Decontamination levels]
– Full decontamination should be used when contamination is significant. It includes preliminary decontamination in the airlock followed by further decontamination in the decontamination unit (DCU) associated with the enclosure (see Section 7.4 for examples). If an assessor enters an enclosure while active removal is underway, they must do a full decontamination.

[p53 | prose | 7.1 Decontamination levels]
Decide on which decontamination procedure to use each time exiting an enclosure, based on the level of contamination inside. Full decontamination may be needed at any stage, so arrangements must always allow for this. DCUs must remain on site and operational when assessments are being done.

[p53 | prose | 7.2 Entering an enclosure]
7.2 Entering an enclosure

[p53 | prose | 7.2 Entering an enclosure]
Assessors must enter enclosures through the airlock. They should wear appropriate layers of PPE to allow for correct decontamination when leaving.

[p53 | prose | 7.2 Entering an enclosure]
Assessors entering enclosure for clearance purposes should be prepared for the possibility of either preliminary or full decontamination.

[p53 | prose | 7.2 Entering an enclosure]
Place domestic items such as clothes and towels, at the clean end of the DCU for use after decontamination. If the DCU is not connected directly to the airlock, make sure blue transiting coveralls are available in the second stage of the airlock for exiting procedures.

[p53 | prose | 7.2 Entering an enclosure]
Clearance inspections often involve crawling, kneeling, stretching, and climbing. These actions can tear or rip coveralls and contaminate underclothes. Assessors should not wear any domestic clothes inside the enclosure.

[p53 | prose | 7.2 Entering an enclosure]

[p53 | prose | 7.2 Entering an enclosure]
– remove all domestic clothes

[p53 | prose | 7.2 Entering an enclosure]
– put on any necessary disposable underclothes and overshoes

[p53 | prose | 7.2 Entering an enclosure]
– put on white coveralls

[p53 | prose | 7.2 Entering an enclosure]
– make sure any equipment taken into the enclosure is clean.

[p53 | prose | 7.2 Entering an enclosure]
– Put on required RPE and perform a seal check (including negative pressure checks as required). See Section 8.2 Pre-use checks for more information. RPE should be worn inside enclosures for clearance stage two and stage three.

If only one layer of coveralls is worn and it gets damaged, or two layers are worn and both layers are damaged – all underwear worn underneath will have to be disposed of as asbestos contaminated waste as well as the coveralls.

[p54 | prose | 7.3 Preliminary decontamination]

[p54 | prose | 7.3 Preliminary decontamination]

[p54 | prose | 7.3 Preliminary decontamination]

[p60 | prose | Using PPE to manage risks other than asbestos]
Using PPE to manage risks other than asbestos

[p60 | prose | Using PPE to manage risks other than asbestos]
Asbestos will often not be the only hazard present at a removal work site. If the work involves other hazards, for example working at height, working in extreme temperatures, or near hazardous substances, the PCBU must do a further risk assessment to manage these risks, and provide any appropriate PPE related to managing those risks.

[p60 | prose | Using PPE to manage risks other than asbestos]
The following general guidelines relating to PPE/PRE also apply to assessor work:

[p60 | prose | Using PPE to manage risks other than asbestos]
– The PCBU or assessor must make sure the PPE is suitable to reduce health and safety risks. This means it is appropriate for the work and hazards, fits well, and is reasonably comfortable for the assessor.

[p60 | prose | Using PPE to manage risks other than asbestos]
– The PCBU must provide the assessors with information, training, and guidance on how to use, store, and maintain the PPE to make sure it remains effective and safe.

[p60 | prose | Using PPE to manage risks other than asbestos]
– PPE must be kept clean, in good condition, and repaired or replaced when needed to keep reducing risks. Assessors must wear or use the PPE so far as it is reasonably practicable, and it must work with any other PPE they need to wear.

[p60 | prose | Using PPE to manage risks other than asbestos]
– When selecting the PPE and RPE check that the chosen equipment will not introduce other risks or affect the functionality of other equipment.

8.2 Respiratory protective equipment

[p61 | prose | 8.2 Respiratory protective equipment]

Tight-fitting facepieces, fit testing, and seal checking

[p62 | prose | Tight-fitting facepieces, fit testing, and seal checking]
RPE must be fitted properly and worn correctly to work as intended. The manufacturer’s instructions should include information on how to put on RPE and perform a pre-use seal check. Assessors must be trained on how to put on their RPE correctly and perform a seal check. Securely fit RPE on the head before putting on other PPE or headgear. Wearing RPE over caps, hoodies, or goggle straps can affect the seal or cause it to slip during wear. Stubble, beards, sideburns, or wearing glasses can stop a tight-fitting facepiece from sealing properly. Keep faces clean-shaven in the area where the face seal meets the respirator. Keep long hair out of the face seal to avoid interference, especially with full-face respirators.

[p65 | prose | 8.3 Other PPE for assessment work]
8.3 Other PPE for assessment work

[p65 | prose | 8.3 Other PPE for assessment work]
PPE, especially coveralls and footwear, is important for preventing the asbestos spreading and reducing secondary exposure for assessors and others. Disposable coveralls should be used, and cleanable footwear or footwear covers should be worn to avoid taking asbestos outside the work area.

[p65 | prose | Using coveralls during asbestos work]
Using coveralls during asbestos work

[p65 | prose | Using coveralls during asbestos work]
Assessors should wear coveralls that meet certain criteria during asbestos removal. Disposable coveralls are most common because they do not need to be washed. They can be double-bagged and thrown away as asbestos waste during decontamination. The material should be strong enough to handle physical contact from crawling, kneeling, or climbing on removal sites. - Coveralls should prevent asbestos fibres from getting through the material. - Assessors should wear disposable coveralls that comply with BS EN ISO 13982-151 Type 5 (Category 3 PPE) whenever there is a risk of asbestos fibre contamination. Colour coding coveralls can help workers and managers/supervisors stay aware of what level of contamination or exposure risk they are working at. For example: - red/orange coveralls can be used for removalists - white for assessors (except blue while transiting) - blue can be used for transiting and waste

Using domestic clothes during asbestos work

[p66 | prose | Using domestic clothes during asbestos work]

[p66 | prose | Using domestic clothes during asbestos work]
In these cases, only protective, disposable clothing should be worn. Disposable coveralls should be worn with disposable undergarments.

Health monitoring looks at whether a worker’s health is being harmed because of what they are being exposed to while working.

[p69 | prose | 9.1 Purpose of health monitoring]
9.1 Purpose of health monitoring

[p69 | prose | 9.1 Purpose of health monitoring]
Working in hazardous conditions can adversely affect workers’ health – in both the short (acute) and long term (chronic). This includes when the work involves substances that are harmful to people’s health.

[p69 | prose | 9.1 Purpose of health monitoring]
Health monitoring can be used to detect if workers are experiencing health effects from potential exposures – for example from exposure to temperature extremes, UV radiation, hazardous substances, and asbestos.

[p69 | prose | 9.1 Purpose of health monitoring]
Asbestos-related diseases take years to appear, but doctors can run tests to monitor the health of people who work with asbestos and early detection may improve health outcomes for patients.

[p69 | prose | 9.1 Purpose of health monitoring]

9.2 Who does health monitoring apply to?

[p69 | prose | 9.2 Who does health monitoring apply to?]

9.3 Who is responsible for making sure health monitoring is conducted?

[p69 | prose | 9.3 Who is responsible for making sure health monitoring is conducted?]

[p69 | prose | 9.3 Who is responsible for making sure health monitoring is conducted?]
PCBUs should monitor their worker participation rates in health monitoring at regular periods, to make sure that they are fulfilling their duty to provide it.

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]
The PCBU must tell its workers about any asbestos-related health monitoring requirements before they start any work that may expose them to asbestos.

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]
The PCBUs must give those workers the following information:

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]
– what the health monitoring will consist of and how it will be done

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]
– the information that has to be given to the occupational health practitioner – that the PCBU has a duty to obtain a health monitoring report from the occupational health practitioner

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]
– that the PCBU has a duty to notify the regulator and other relevant PCBUs

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]
– how health monitoring reports will be kept, stored, and shared, including keeping confidentiality

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]
– the reasons for health monitoring:

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]
to help the PCBU reduce the risk of exposure to health hazards in the workplace

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]
to enable the PCBU and other PCBUs in the workplace to take remedial action within the workplace to manage the health risk

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]
to help with treating and protecting workers who are or were exposed to health hazards.

[p70 | prose | 9.4 Informing workers about health monitoring]
– that they will inform WorkSafe if test results indicate a worker may have a disease, illness, or injury caused by the work that triggered the health monitoring. This helps WorkSafe carry out its functions under the Act.

[p74 | prose | 10.1 The purpose of exposure monitoring]
10.1 The purpose of exposure monitoring

[p74 | prose | 10.1 The purpose of exposure monitoring]
The purpose of exposure monitoring is to ensure that no person at a workplace is exposed to a substance hazardous to health in a concentration that exceeds the prescribed exposure standard for that substance.

[p74 | prose | 10.1 The purpose of exposure monitoring]
The prescribed exposure standard for asbestos is the airborne contamination standard for asbestos as specified in Section 4 of the asbestos regulations – an average concentration over any 8-hour period of 0.1 respirable asbestos fibres per millilitre of air.

[p74 | prose | 10.1 The purpose of exposure monitoring]
Workplace exposure standards for all types of asbestos are limited to WES (worker exposure standard) – TWA (time weighted average) reflective of the airborne contamination standard for asbestos and can be found here Workplace exposure standards and biological exposure indices

[p74 | prose | 10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring]
10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring

[p74 | prose | 10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring]
WorkSafe NZ recommends that professional help be sought in the development and implementation of an exposure monitoring strategy and interpretation of results (for example, from an appropriately qualified occupational hygienist).

[p74 | prose | 10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring]
Exposure monitoring can be set up by an assessor as part of their assessor work activities if the assessor is considered a competent person for the task. They must be suitably qualified and trained in setting up personal air monitoring (for example a licensed assessor that is also a qualified occupational hygienist).

[p75 | prose | 10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring]
When carrying out exposure monitoring, assessing health risks, or assessing the effectiveness of controls, the person should have competence in:

[p75 | prose | 10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring]
– the risk assessment process

[p75 | prose | 10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring]
– the tasks, processes or exposures being assessed when developing a sampling strategy

[p75 | prose | 10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring]
– selection and use of sampling equipment and sampling media sampling methods

[p75 | prose | 10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring]
– interpretation of data

[p75 | prose | 10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring]
– criteria on which WES are based

[p75 | prose | 10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring]
– relevance and application of statistical analysis of exposure data.

[p75 | prose | 10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring]
The person doing the exposure monitoring must not act beyond their relevant competency and experience. PCBUs should be made aware of the limits of the advice that can be provided and be referred to other professionals for further advice where circumstances require it.

[p75 | prose | 10.2 Who can do exposure monitoring]
WorkSafe encourages PCBUs to use the services of professionals who are listed on the HASANZ Register

[p75 | prose | 10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors]
10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors

[p75 | prose | 10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors]
Exposure monitoring should be done periodically (for example 5% of air sampling jobs or based on professional advice). A sampling strategy should be created that includes all assessors and has an increased focus on higher-risk activities.

[p75 | prose | 10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors]
Exposure monitoring for assessors should focus on tasks such as:

[p75 | prose | 10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors]
– when carrying out visual inspections and air monitoring inside enclosures during four-stage clearance procedures

[p75 | prose | 10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors]
– while collecting bulk samples

[p75 | prose | 10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors]
– when entering active enclosures for any reason.

[p75 | prose | 10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors]
Assessor exposure monitoring is done using personal air sampling.

[p75 | prose | 10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors]
The results of personal air monitoring (for the purposes of exposure monitoring) can be used to:

[p75 | prose | 10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors]
– identify, assess and confirm health risks

[p75 | prose | 10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors]
– identify, monitor and confirm suitability of the RPE in use.

[p75 | prose | 10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors]
Static monitoring (including clearance air sampling) is not a substitute for personal exposure sampling. Static air monitoring may underestimate personal exposures where the disturbance is close to the breathing zone of the person. Clearance air sampling cannot be relied on to provide details of the assessor’s personal exposure.

[p75 | prose | 10.3 When and how exposure monitoring should be done for assessors]
For more detailed information on exposure monitoring and how to apply WES to monitoring results see Applying the workplace exposure standards

Appendix 4: Air monitoring and air sampling equipment

[p96 | appendix | Appendix 4: Air monitoring and air sampling equipment]
The World Health Organisation (WHO) air sampling method recommendations provide a foundation to the development of membrane filter methods developed by WHO member states.

[p96 | appendix | Appendix 4: Air monitoring and air sampling equipment]
Open faced filter holder

[p96 | appendix | Appendix 4: Air monitoring and air sampling equipment]
Use an open-faced filter holder to comply with the WHO standard method. It should:

[p96 | appendix | Appendix 4: Air monitoring and air sampling equipment]
be fitted with an electrically conducting cylindrical cowl

[p96 | appendix | Appendix 4: Air monitoring and air sampling equipment]
expose a circular filter area at least 20mm in diameter for air sampling.

[p96 | appendix | Appendix 4: Air monitoring and air sampling equipment]
The cowl usually extends 1.5 to 3.0 times the filter’s effective diameter in front of the filter.

[p96 | prose | Types of air sampling heads]
Types of air sampling heads

[p96 | prose | Types of air sampling heads]
Several manufacturers make injection-moulded conductive plastic air sampling heads that come preloaded with a suitable filter.

[p96 | prose | Types of air sampling heads]
Alternatively, use metal cowls with a PTFE O-ring. A cowled filter holder protects the filter and allows a uniform deposit. The cowl points downwards during air sampling.

[p96 | prose | Types of air sampling heads]
Required components

[p96 | prose | Types of air sampling heads]
Set up the filter holder with these components:

[p96 | prose | Types of air sampling heads]
Use flexible tubing to connect the filter holder to the pump

[p96 | prose | Types of air sampling heads]
Fit a cap or bung over the cowl entrance to protect the filter from

[p96 | prose | contamination during transport.]
contamination during transport.

[p96 | prose | contamination during transport.]
Use different filter diameters and shorter cowls if they give comparable results. Measure them to confirm the effective filter area.

[p96 | prose | Filter specifications]
Filter specifications

[p96 | prose | Filter specifications]
Use membrane filters made from mixed esters of cellulose or cellulose nitrate,

[p96 | prose | with a pore size between 0.8 and 1.2 micrometres (optically clear grade).]
with a pore size between 0.8 and 1.2 micrometres (optically clear grade).

[p96 | prose | with a pore size between 0.8 and 1.2 micrometres (optically clear grade).]

[p96 | prose | with a pore size between 0.8 and 1.2 micrometres (optically clear grade).]
are 25mm in diameter (minimum 20mm)

[p96 | prose | with a pore size between 0.8 and 1.2 micrometres (optically clear grade).]
have a printed grid on the air sampling side. This grid aligns with the

[p96 | prose | with a pore size between 0.8 and 1.2 micrometres (optically clear grade).]
collected particles and helps with focusing during analysis. Distorted grid lines suggest the filter was not mounted correctly.

[p96 | prose | with a pore size between 0.8 and 1.2 micrometres (optically clear grade).]

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
Asbestos air sampling blanks

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
Three types of blanks support quality control and help maintain the integrity of asbestos air sampling:

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
– Air sampling media blanks help check the quality of unused filters. To prepare these, extract filters from a box of unused filters, mount them, and count them before use. This confirms the batch is suitable. The procedure includes:

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
- selecting at least four blank filters from each manufacturer’s batch, or at least 1% of larger batches

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
- confirming that average blank counts do not exceed three fibres per mm2 (for example, five fibres per 200 fields)

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
investigating the cause if laboratory records show consistently higher counts, including checking the source supply.

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
– Field blanks support quality control. They are made by taking filters from suitable batches to the air sampling area and handling them the same way as air sampling filters.

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
- Keep filters in capped, cowled heads during transport. Do not draw air through them or connect them to the pump.

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
- Briefly remove and replace the cap at the air sampling site.

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
– Laboratory blanks help check for contamination during laboratory handling. They are prepared by mounting and counting filters from approved batches. Use laboratory blanks if a field blank shows possible contamination or if there are concerns about contamination from laboratory sources.

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
A laboratory blank may be analysed:

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
– with each batch of routine samples

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
– after air sampling, if contamination is suspected.

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
The number and type of blanks tested depend on several factors. Always test air sampling media blanks before use to confirm they are suitable.

[p103 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
The air sampling organisation must prepare and label field blanks for identification. Investigate any contamination found in blank filters and monitor the consistency of membrane filters between batches.

[p104 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
Note: The WHO method recommends subtracting blank counts, but this guidance does not. Evidence shows that blank counts are usually low and do not affect compliance measurements based on counts above 20 fibres (40 ends). For low counts, the precision is poor. Subtracting two low numbers, each with a wide confidence interval, is unlikely to give reliable results. For example, a count of four fibres has a 95% confidence interval of 1–10 fibres.

[p104 | prose | Asbestos air sampling blanks]
Only subtract blank counts if contamination on a field or laboratory blank is more than 8 fibres per mm2 and re-air sampling is not possible. In these cases, report both the original and adjusted counts and clearly state that subtraction was used due to high contamination on the blank.

[p105 | prose | ACETONE-TRIACETIN MOUNTING METHOD]
ACETONE-TRIACETIN MOUNTING METHOD

[p105 | prose | ACETONE-TRIACETIN MOUNTING METHOD]
Assessors must use the acetone-triacetin mounting method.

[p105 | prose | ACETONE-TRIACETIN MOUNTING METHOD]
– Condensing acetone vapour collapses the filter pores, making the filter stick to the glass slide. The filter becomes clear, and asbestos fibres stay close to the top surface.

[p105 | prose | ACETONE-TRIACETIN MOUNTING METHOD]
– Triacetin forms a layer between the collapsed filter and the coverslip.

[p105 | prose | ACETONE-TRIACETIN MOUNTING METHOD]
– When stored flat, the mounted slide can last for years with little damage, though some fibres may move slightly.

[p105 | prose | ACETONE-TRIACETIN MOUNTING METHOD]
– If mounting half-filters, too much triacetin used to form the mount can cause slight changes in the area over time.

[p105 | prose | MOUNTING THE FILTER]
MOUNTING THE FILTER

[p105 | prose | MOUNTING THE FILTER]
– Place the filter in the centre of a clean microscope slide, with the sample side facing up. Align the grid lines parallel to the slide edges if possible.

[p105 | prose | MOUNTING THE FILTER]
– Check that the filter is dry. Moisture interferes with the clearing process. Dry filters exposed to high humidity before mounting.

[p106 | prose | MOUNTING THE FILTER]
– Before mounting, place filters and containers in a warm air cabinet without a fan, or on a slide warmer. Partially remove the container lid to let moisture escape.

[p106 | prose | MOUNTING THE FILTER]
– Place a metal or insert plastic ring around the filter to control the spread of acetone and improve clearing.

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
ACETONE APPLICATION

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
– Use only enough acetone to clear the filter completely (approximately 0.25 ml).

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
– Place the clean slide under the outlet of the hot block.

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
– Slowly inject acetone into the hot block to produce a steady stream of vapour over the filter. The filter should clear instantly.

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
– Using a small amount of acetone lowers the risk of fire hazards and health risks.

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
The image below shows clearing a filter using a hot block.

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
Acetone vapour is highly flammable and has low toxicity. Carry out a proper risk assessment and follow safety precautions before starting.

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
– Remove or isolate all ignition sources. Keep the acetone storage bottle sealed when not in use.

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
– Perform the procedure in a fume cupboard to reduce exposure of acetone vapour:

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
– Place the slide on a hot plate at 50–60°C for a few seconds to evaporate any remaining acetone before adding triacetin and the coverslip.

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
– After evaporation, use a micropipette or suitable dropper to place approximately 120 l triacetin on the filter. Use just enough to cover it without spilling when the coverslip is added.

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
– Gently lower the clean coverslip at an angle onto the filter to push out any air.

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
– Do not press or move the coverslip once placed.

[p106 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
– Use a tissue or similar to draw off any excess triacetin using capillary action. Do not touch or wipe the coverslip. The filter will still look grainy under the microscope.

[p107 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
If immediate counting is planned, place the slide on the hot plate (up to 15 minutes at 50–60°C) to help clear the filter.

[p107 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
If not counting immediately, leave the slide overnight at room temperature. Keep it clean and horizontal, with the coverslip facing up, until cleared and ready to count.

[p107 | prose | ACETONE APPLICATION]
If further analysis (such as SEM or TEM) or fibre identification is or may be needed, cut the filter in half before mounting. Mount one half using the acetone- triacetin method and store the other half in a clean, clearly labelled container.

For the recommended cutting filter method, see Filter handling and transportation in this appendix.

[p107 | prose | MICROSCOPE REQUIREMENTS]
MICROSCOPE REQUIREMENTS

[p107 | prose | MICROSCOPE REQUIREMENTS]
To consistently detect fibres at the limit of visibility, the microscope and its accessories must meet the following specifications:

[p107 | prose | MICROSCOPE REQUIREMENTS]
– A binocular stand with Köhler or Köhler-type illumination, including a field iris. The condenser (sub-stage assembly), objectives, and eyepieces must be compatible with each other and with the stand.

[p107 | prose | MICROSCOPE REQUIREMENTS]
– A sub-stage assembly with an Abbé or achromatic phase contrast condenser in a centrable focusing mount. The phase annulus centring must operate independently of the condenser centring mechanism.

[p107 | prose | MICROSCOPE REQUIREMENTS]
– A built-in mechanical stage with slide clamps and x-y displacement.

[p107 | prose | MICROSCOPE REQUIREMENTS]
– A low-powered objective lens (for example, 10x or 4x) for checking the evenness of the dust deposit on the filter and for locating the stage micrometre and test slide tramlines.

[p107 | prose | MICROSCOPE REQUIREMENTS]
– A positive-phase contrast objective lens (preferably par focal with the low- powered lens) with 40x magnification. The numerical aperture (NA) (which helps distinguish between two very close points), must be between 0.65 and 0.70. The phase ring absorption must be between 65% and 85%.

[p107 | prose | MICROSCOPE REQUIREMENTS]
– A pair of optically matched binocular eyepieces, preferably wide field, high eye-point type, providing at least 500x total magnification. One eyepiece must be a focusing type and allow a graticule to be inserted.

[p107 | prose | MICROSCOPE REQUIREMENTS]
Note: Some microscope stands have a tube extension that increases total magnification. To calculate total magnification, multiply the magnifications of the objective lens, tube extension, and eyepiece. The total magnification must not exceed 1000 times the NA.

[p108 | prose | MICROSCOPE REQUIREMENTS]
– A Walton-Beckett eyepiece graticule, 32 type G22, with an apparent diameter of 100 ±2 m in the object plane (when checked against a calibrated stage micrometre), to define the counting area.

[p116 | prose | Starting Stage 2]
Starting Stage 2

[p116 | prose | Starting Stage 2]
Only start Stage 2 when Stage 1 is completed satisfactorily.

[p116 | prose | Starting Stage 2]
This stage is a thorough visual inspection of the entire enclosure or work area. The aim is to make sure, as far as reasonably practicable, all surfaces and areas are free from any visible dust and debris.

[p116 | prose | Starting Stage 2]
Asbestos removal work spreads dust and debris in the enclosure or work area, which can settle on any surface – especially in hard-to-reach or poorly cleaned areas.

[p116 | prose | Starting Stage 2]
Any remaining asbestos contamination is a serious risk when the enclosure is removed or access restrictions to the work area are lifted. It can spread when the enclosure is dismantled and pose ongoing and persistent risk of harm to workers, maintenance staff, cleaners, or building occupants who may disturb it without knowing.

[p116 | prose | Starting Stage 2]
This stage is the most significant part of the clearance procedure.

[p119 | prose | Stage 3: Surface testing by dust disturbance and clearance air monitoring/trace-level air sampling]
This stage involves surface testing by simulating a worst-case disturbance of the surfaces inside the enclosure immediately followed by air sampling.

[p120 | prose | Stage 3: Surface testing by dust disturbance and clearance air monitoring/trace-level air sampling]
The purpose of this simulation is to ensure that the risk posed by even the most aggressive and deliberate disturbance of non-visible surface dust is negligible as the transient/peak respirable asbestos fibre level is below the trace limit.

[p120 | prose | Surface testing by dust disturbance]

Surface testing involves deliberately and thoroughly disturbing all surfaces inside the enclosure, including the enclosure itself. This means sweeping the floors and brushing all other accessible surfaces inside and of the enclosure.

[p120 | prose | Surface testing by dust disturbance]
Licensed assessors doing surface testing need to:

[p120 | prose | Surface testing by dust disturbance]

[p120 | prose | Surface testing by dust disturbance]
– for enclosures larger than 20m2, use a broom to sweep the floor – for ergonomic and practical reasons

[p120 | prose | Surface testing by dust disturbance]
– use a short-handled brush to brush other surfaces

[p120 | prose | Surface testing by dust disturbance]
– sweep and brush for at least 1.5 minutes for each air sample to be taken (for example, if four air samples are to be taken, disturb surfaces for a total of six minutes (4 x 1.5 minutes).

[p127 | prose | Wet enclosures]
Wet enclosures

[p127 | prose | Wet enclosures]
Assessors often find wet enclosures during clearance inspections. Wetness may result from condensation, dampness, overuse of water for dust suppression, leaks, incorrect or recent sealant use, or even from using prohibited equipment (such as high-pressure water spray). Enclosures must be clean and dry before the start of Stage 2 of the clearance inspection, and then throughout the rest of the clearance inspection.

[p127 | prose | Wet enclosures]
– If the enclosure is wet at the start of the four-stage clearance, the issue must be fixed by the removalist. This may include drying the area or arranging a plumber to enter the enclosure to repair leaks.

[p127 | prose | Wet enclosures]
– Incorrect sealant use can delay the thorough visual inspection (see Sprayed sealant in this appendix).

[p127 | prose | Wet enclosures]
– If the enclosure remains wet and the issue cannot be fixed quickly, the assessor must fail the visual examination.

[p127 | prose | Wet enclosures]
– If the visual examination goes ahead as the issue cannot be fixed, the assessor needs to photograph and record the wet or damp areas, then record the reasons they could not be dried.

[p127 | prose | Wet enclosures]
Leaving water in the enclosure must be seen by the licenced assessor as incomplete cleaning. Removalists not allowing drying time after cleaning is not a valid reason for wet enclosures. Wet enclosures can compromise or even fully invalidate results of the stage 3 of the clearance inspection.

[p127 | prose | Sprayed sealant]
Sprayed sealant

[p127 | prose | Sprayed sealant]
Sealants should not be sprayed before completing Stage 3 of the clearance inspection. Exceptions can be made, but these should be agreed in writing with the licensed assessor before the asbestos removal starts. For example, exceptions may apply if the floor is porous, difficult to clean, or could release enough non-asbestos dust (such as from concrete) to clog filters and invalidate the air test.

[p127 | prose | Sprayed sealant]
The assessor can use discretion and, after air testing, may allow sealant in these cases (see Dusty surfaces inside enclosures). This should be recorded in the clearance inspection records and the air test should begin.

[p127 | prose | Sprayed sealant]
If the enclosure is still wet from sealant when the licensed assessor begins Stage 2 of the clearance inspection, the licensed assessor must:

[p127 | prose | Sprayed sealant]
– fail Stage 2 of the clearance inspection

[p127 | prose | Sprayed sealant]
– inform the removalist that the clearance inspection can only restart once the sealant is washed off and the enclosure is dry.

[p127 | prose | Sprayed sealant]
If the sealant has already dried, the assessor must fail the stage 2 of the clearance and carefully consider advice to be given on next steps.

[p127 | prose | Sprayed sealant]
If there is evidence that the sealant is protecting significant amount of asbestos dust, which may pose a risk to future occupants:

[p127 | prose | Sprayed sealant]
– the sealant must be removed

[p127 | prose | Sprayed sealant]
– the relevant areas must be recleaned

[p127 | prose | Sprayed sealant]
– the commissioning PCBU should be fully informed about the use of sealant and its implications for any remaining asbestos.

The licenced assessor must check the loose rubble or soil flooring for asbestos debris.

[p128 | prose | AT STAGE 4 OF THE CLEARANCE INSPECTION]
If any debris or suspicious material is found, the licensed assessor should advise the removalist that the top layer must be removed. The removal depth depends on how much contamination is present. The assessor must inspect the newly exposed surface. If no contamination is identified, the area passes Stage 4 of the clearance inspection.

[p128 | prose | AT STAGE 4 OF THE CLEARANCE INSPECTION]
– If asbestos contamination of loose rubble/soil is identified before the asbestos removal starts, the removalist must treat the rubble or soil as part of the asbestos removal.

[p128 | prose | AT STAGE 4 OF THE CLEARANCE INSPECTION]
The ARCP must outline the loose rubble/soil removal methodology. It is advisable that the removalist consults the assessor before starting the removal work, as the rubble/soil must be removed to a depth where no asbestos contamination is visible.

[p128 | prose | AT STAGE 4 OF THE CLEARANCE INSPECTION]
– The assessor must then inspect the newly exposed surface as part of Stage 2 of the clearance inspection.

[p128 | prose | AT STAGE 4 OF THE CLEARANCE INSPECTION]
For removal of over 10m3 of soil and subsequent clearances see: New Zealand Guidelines for Assessing and Managing Asbestos in Soil

[p128 | prose | CLEARANCE WITH FIXED SCAFFOLDING OR ACCESS EQUIPMENT IN PLACE]
CLEARANCE WITH FIXED SCAFFOLDING OR ACCESS EQUIPMENT IN PLACE

[p128 | prose | CLEARANCE WITH FIXED SCAFFOLDING OR ACCESS EQUIPMENT IN PLACE]
Fixed or mobile scaffolding and other access equipment may be used inside the enclosure or work area to remove items such as high-level panels or ceiling tiles. Removalists should cap end pieces of the scaffolding before the start of asbestos removal and must fully decontaminate all access equipment during final cleaning, before the enclosure or work area is handed over to the assessor for clearance inspection.

[p128 | prose | CLEARANCE WITH FIXED SCAFFOLDING OR ACCESS EQUIPMENT IN PLACE]
The equipment must stay inside the enclosure or work area for the duration of Stage 2 of the clearance inspection to allow full access to all surfaces.

[p128 | prose | CLEARANCE WITH FIXED SCAFFOLDING OR ACCESS EQUIPMENT IN PLACE]
The assessor must thoroughly inspect all access equipment, but only after it has been used by the assessor for access. Assessor should pay additional attention when visually inspecting scaffolding boards, gaps between them, poles, and fixings.

[p128 | prose | CLEARANCE WITH FIXED SCAFFOLDING OR ACCESS EQUIPMENT IN PLACE]
After the removalist dismantles the enclosure, they can remove the scaffolding or access equipment. The assessor then inspects the area where the equipment was present and makes sure the removalist has cleaned any remaining material.

[p129 | prose | CLEARANCE WITH FIXED SCAFFOLDING OR ACCESS EQUIPMENT IN PLACE]
Note: If the scaffolding remains for other maintenance work, the assessor should reinspect the area around and under it for debris, so as far as is reasonably practicable. They should record in the clearance inspection that (likely minor) debris may fall when the structure is finally removed.

[p129 | prose | ASBESTOS INTENDED TO REMAIN]
ASBESTOS INTENDED TO REMAIN

[p129 | prose | ASBESTOS INTENDED TO REMAIN]
Sometimes asbestos is left in the enclosure or work area. For example, damaged lagging may be removed from pipework, but undamaged lagging remains, or only some asbestos ceiling tiles are removed. The assessor should be told about this during the Stage 1 scope of work discussion, and it should be recorded in the ARCP.

[p129 | prose | ASBESTOS INTENDED TO REMAIN]
The removalist must check the condition of any remaining ACMs. If any materials are in poor condition, the four-stage clearance may fail when the assessor inspects them.

[p129 | prose | ASBESTOS INTENDED TO REMAIN]
If the assessor finds poor-quality asbestos materials, they must be dealt with (for example, repaired, encapsulated, or removed). The commissioning PCBU must agree to these actions, and the removalist must be involved.

[p129 | prose | ASBESTOS INTENDED TO REMAIN]
The clearance inspection must stop at this point. The removalist or commissioning PCBU must be informed, and the clearance inspection must not restart until those issues are resolved.

[p129 | prose | ASBESTOS INTENDED TO REMAIN]
Assessors must record any remaining ACMs in good condition in the clearance inspection records. This allows the commissioning PCBU to update the asbestos register and management plan.

[p129 | prose | Asbestos waste remaining in the enclosure]
Asbestos waste remaining in the enclosure

[p129 | prose | Asbestos waste remaining in the enclosure]
In rare cases, asbestos waste (bagged or wrapped) may need to stay inside the enclosure until Stage 4 of the clearance inspection. This can happen when bulky waste, such as large pipes, vessels, or AIB panels, cannot pass through the baglock system.

[p129 | prose | Asbestos waste remaining in the enclosure]
– The waste should remain in the enclosure and be included in Stage 2 of the clearance inspection to confirm the outer wrapping is free of asbestos.

[p129 | prose | Asbestos waste remaining in the enclosure]
– During the thorough visual inspection, the removalist must move the items on the assessor’s instruction so the assessor can check the surfaces underneath.

[p129 | prose | INACCESSIBLE OR IMPOSSIBLE TO REMOVE ASBESTOS]
INACCESSIBLE OR IMPOSSIBLE TO REMOVE ASBESTOS

[p129 | prose | INACCESSIBLE OR IMPOSSIBLE TO REMOVE ASBESTOS]
Spray-applied asbestos is often found in crevices or holes in walls where pipes or girders run. These areas may contain asbestos residues that that cannot be removed. In these cases, the assessor may allow non-flammable sealants, such as foams or plaster, to fill the hole and seal the asbestos inside.

[p129 | prose | INACCESSIBLE OR IMPOSSIBLE TO REMOVE ASBESTOS]
Before applying the sealant, the assessor must make sure, as far as is reasonably practicable, asbestos has been removed.

[p130 | prose | INACCESSIBLE OR IMPOSSIBLE TO REMOVE ASBESTOS]
This image shows remnants of asbestos on materials.

[p130 | prose | INACCESSIBLE OR IMPOSSIBLE TO REMOVE ASBESTOS]
The commissioning PCBU should be told about the proposed encapsulation before it begins. This should be recorded in the ARCP.

[p130 | prose | INACCESSIBLE OR IMPOSSIBLE TO REMOVE ASBESTOS]
The sealant location and remaining asbestos should be recorded in the clearance inspection records to help the commissioning PCBU update the asbestos register and management plan.

[p130 | prose | INACCESSIBLE OR IMPOSSIBLE TO REMOVE ASBESTOS]
If the assessor finds holes or asbestos residues already sealed with foam or proprietary sealant, they should check the sealant’s condition. The sealant should adequately cover the area or material and remain intact.

[p130 | prose | INACCESSIBLE OR IMPOSSIBLE TO REMOVE ASBESTOS]
If the sealant is not in good condition, more will be needed before Stage 2 of the clearance inspection can be completed.

[p130 | prose | Encapsulant and sealant use]
Encapsulant and sealant use

[p130 | prose | Encapsulant and sealant use]
Where asbestos was sprayed onto porous surfaces (such as breeze blocks, bricks, plaster, and concrete), achieving an asbestos-free surface is nearly impossible.

[p130 | prose | Encapsulant and sealant use]
The assessor should:

[p130 | prose | Encapsulant and sealant use]
– Confirm further removal is not reasonably practicable.

[p130 | prose | Encapsulant and sealant use]
– Advise the removalist and/or commissioning PCBU to seal the remaining asbestos with a permanent proprietary sealant.

[p130 | prose | Encapsulant and sealant use]
– Restart the visual inspection once the sealant has dried.

[p130 | prose | Encapsulant and sealant use]
– Contain the asbestos after viewing the residual material.

[p130 | prose | Encapsulant and sealant use]
Only use encapsulants or sealants after the assessor:

[p130 | prose | Encapsulant and sealant use]
– has seen the residual asbestos

[p130 | prose | Encapsulant and sealant use]
– given approval to go ahead.

[p130 | prose | Encapsulant and sealant use]
The sealant location and remaining asbestos should be recorded in the clearance inspection records to help the commissioning PCBU update the asbestos register and management plan.
