[p11 | prose | at height]
What is working at height?

[p11 | prose | at height]
Working at height means working in a place where – if precautions are not taken — a worker could fall and be hurt, injured or die.

[p11 | prose | at height]
Under HSWA you must manage fall risks from any height.

[p11 | prose | at height]
In New Zealand, there is no minimum height threshold for working at height —- any fall risk from any height must be managed.

[p11 | prose | at height]
WorkSafe recommends any potential fall greater than one metre (1m) in height should be managed using physical barriers or a fall restraint.

[p11 | prose | at height]
This includes working above or below ground level, using ladders, scaffolds, working on roofs, or operating near unprotected edges.

[p11 | prose | at height]
Factors contributing to injuries sustained while working at height can include:

[p11 | prose | at height]
lack of, or inadequate, planning, design and risk assessment

[p11 | prose | at height]
inadequate supervision

[p11 | prose | at height]
insufficient training for the task being carried out

[p11 | prose | at height]
incorrect protection or equipment choices

[p11 | prose | at height]
incorrect use or set-up of equipment including personal protective equipment

[p11 | prose | at height]
unwillingness to change the way a task is carried out when a safer alternative

[p11 | prose | at height]
is identified

[p11 | prose | at height]
suitable equipment being unavailable.

[p12 | prose | at height]
Working at height does not include slipping, tripping or falling at the same level. For example, slipping on a flat floor would not be considered working at height.

[p12 | prose | at height]
Clarifying the ‘3-metre’ rule

[p12 | prose | at height]
Many people believe that if a fall is less than 3 metres, no control measures are needed. This is dangerous and incorrect.

[p12 | prose | at height]
If there is potential for a person to fall from any height, the general duties of care under HSWA require PCBUs to take reasonably practicable steps to prevent harm, including harm from a fall.

[p12 | prose | Short duration height work]
Short duration height work

[p12 | prose | Short duration height work]
Short duration work at height must be treated the same way as any other activity at height.

[p12 | prose | Short duration height work]
Short duration work lasts minutes rather than hours. While it may not be reasonably practicable to provide (for example) full edge protection for short duration work it still needs to be considered during the assessment of hazards and should not be ruled out.

[p12 | prose | Short duration height work]
Appropriate fall prevention control measures must be put in place and a risk assessment done, regardless of the short duration nature of the work.

[p14 | prose | The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 applies to all businesses and workers in New Zealand.]
The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 applies to all businesses and workers in New Zealand.

[p14 | prose | The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 applies to all businesses and workers in New Zealand.]
HSWA is New Zealand’s primary work health and safety legislation. HSWA covers all work-related activities – wherever they take place. It includes physical and mental health and safety. WorkSafe New Zealand is the main regulator responsible for administering and enforcing HSWA.

[p14 | prose | The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 applies to all businesses and workers in New Zealand.]
Section 2 highlights keys aspects of HSWA that are particularly important to understand when managing the risks or working at height in New Zealand.

[p14 | prose | 2.1 Duty holders under HSWA]
2.1 Duty holders under HSWA

[p14 | prose | 2.1 Duty holders under HSWA]
A duty holder is a person who has a duty under HSWA. There are four types of duty holders – PCBUs, officers, workers, and other persons at workplaces.

[p14 | prose | PCBUs]
PCBUs

[p14 | prose | PCBUs]
A PCBU is a ‘person conducting a business or undertaking’. It includes businesses, companies, sole traders or self-employed people and not-for-profit organisations.

[p14 | prose | PCBUs]
Examples of PCBUs include businesses involved in:

[p14 | prose | PCBUs]
– Construction and trades such as building companies, residential and commercial builders, and roofing specialists.

[p14 | prose | PCBUs]
– Maintenance and facilities such as window cleaners, painters, and HVAC/air conditioning installers, who regularly access roofs and high points.

[p14 | prose | PCBUs]
– Specialist contractors such as scaffolding companies, temporary edge protection installers, and building envelope contractors.

[p14 | prose | PCBUs]
– Industrial and plant such as inspectors and maintenance crews for tall structures or industrial plants.

[p15 | prose | Officers]
Officers

[p15 | prose | Officers]
An officer is a person who occupies a position within a business or organisation where they have significant influence over the management of the business or undertaking. Businesses/organisations can have more than one officer. Examples include:

[p15 | prose | Officers]
– company directors (even if they do not have ‘director’ in the title)

[p15 | prose | Officers]
– any partner in a partnership (other than a limited partnership)

[p15 | prose | Officers]
– any general partner in a limited partnership

[p15 | prose | Officers]
– any person who occupies a position that allows them to exercise significant influence over the management of the business or undertaking.

[p15 | prose | Officers]
Officers have a duty to exercise due diligence because they make decisions that can affect workers’ health and safety.

[p15 | prose | Workers]
Workers

[p15 | prose | Workers]
Under HSWA, a worker is an individual who carries out work in any capacity for a business or undertaking (PCBU). This includes permanent workers, temporary workers, contract workers, and apprentices.

[p15 | prose | Others]
Others

[p15 | prose | Others]
Under HSWA, other people are people who are present at a workplace but are not workers. This includes visitors, members of the public who may be affected by the work activity, and casual volunteers.

[p15 | prose | Others]
For more general information see: Introduction to the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 – special guide | WorkSafe

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
2.2 PCBU primary duty of care

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
A PCBU has the main responsibility for people’s health and safety at work:

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
– PCBUs must ensure (so far as is reasonably practicable) the health and safety of workers who work for them (or workers they influence) while they are working.

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
– PCBUs must also make sure (so far as is reasonably practicable) that other persons’ health and safety is not put as risk by the work that they do.

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
– PCBUs must (so far as is reasonably practicable) have effective practices in place for providing and maintaining:

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
- a work environment that is without risk to health and safety

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
- safe plant and structures

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
- safe use, handling, and storage of plant, substances and structures

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
- safe systems of work

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
- adequate facilities for the welfare of workers when carrying out work for the business or undertaking, including ensuring access to those facilities.

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
– PCBUs must provide (so far as is reasonably practicable) information, training, instruction or supervision to protect people from work-related risks to their health and safety.

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
– PCBUs must (so far as is reasonably practicable) monitor the conditions at the workplace and worker health to prevent illness or injury to workers related to the work that they do.

[p15 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
– Where worker accommodation is provided, the PCBU must (so far as is reasonably practicable) maintain the accommodation so that the worker is not exposed to risks to their health and safety arising from the accommodation.

[p16 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
Self-employed people must take all reasonably practicable actions to ensure their own health and safety while at work.

[p16 | prose | 2.2 PCBU primary duty of care]
See s36 Primary duty of care of HSWA.

2.3 Worker duties

[p16 | prose | 2.3 Worker duties]

[p16 | prose | 2.4 Overlapping duties]
2.4 Overlapping duties

[p16 | prose | 2.4 Overlapping duties]
When two or more businesses operate together, for example at the same location or in a contracting arrangement, they must consult, cooperate and coordinate together to fulfil their primary duties of care.

[p16 | prose | 2.4 Overlapping duties]
A PCBU cannot contract out its duties. However, reasonable arrangements can be made with the other PCBUs to fulfil its duties. While a PCBU can enter into reasonable agreements with other PCBUs, this does not mean they no longer hold any health and safety responsibilities. They must still monitor the other business to ensure they follow through on what was agreed.

[p16 | prose | 2.4 Overlapping duties]
The more influence and control a PCBU has over a health and safety matter, the more responsibility it is likely to have. This is especially important in contracting chains or at works sites where multiple PCBUs may be working at height or working near others who are working at height.

[p16 | prose | 2.4 Overlapping duties]
The PCBU with more influence and control (and the greatest share of the responsibilities) will usually be in the best position to manage the associated risks.

[p16 | prose | 2.4 Overlapping duties]
All PCBUs with overlapping duties should:

[p16 | prose | 2.4 Overlapping duties]
– discuss what work activities are being carried out

[p16 | prose | 2.4 Overlapping duties]
– agree on the degree of influence and control each business has

[p16 | prose | 2.4 Overlapping duties]
– agree on who will manage what and how it will be managed

[p16 | prose | 2.4 Overlapping duties]
– monitor and check how things are going on an ongoing basis.

[p18 | prose | 2.8 What does reasonably practicable mean?]
2.8 What does reasonably practicable mean?

[p18 | prose | 2.8 What does reasonably practicable mean?]
Health and safety duties need to be carried out ‘so far as is reasonably practicable’. There are two parts to ‘reasonably practicable’. First consider what is possible in your circumstances to ensure health and safety. Then consider, of these possible actions, what is reasonable to do in your circumstances.

[p18 | prose | 2.8 What does reasonably practicable mean?]
When deciding what is ‘reasonably practicable’, consider:

[p18 | prose | 2.8 What does reasonably practicable mean?]
– How likely is the risk and how severe is the illness or injury that might result?

[p18 | prose | 2.8 What does reasonably practicable mean?]
– What do you know, or should reasonably know, about the hazard or risk and the ways of eliminating or minimising the risk?

[p18 | prose | 2.8 What does reasonably practicable mean?]
– What is the availability of the control measures, and how suitable are they for the specific risk?

[p18 | prose | 2.8 What does reasonably practicable mean?]
– What are the costs of the control measure and are the costs grossly disproportionate to the risk?

[p20 | prose | Using a risk management approach can help you to identify and manage health and safety risks.]
Using a risk management approach can help you to identify and manage health and safety risks.

[p20 | prose | Using a risk management approach can help you to identify and manage health and safety risks.]
This section offers a general introduction on ways PCBUs can apply good risk management principles when managing work-related risks. The approach outlined here can be applied when identifying work at height hazards and deciding how to manage the associated risks.

[p20 | prose | 3.1 Risk management generally]
3.1 Risk management generally

[p20 | prose | 3.1 Risk management generally]
Risks to health and safety arise from people being exposed to a hazard. A hazard is a potential source or cause of harm (such as a physical injury, or harm to a person’s health) and can include a person’s behaviour.

[p20 | prose | 3.1 Risk management generally]
As a PCBU, you must manage risks so that workers and other peoples’ health and safety is not put at risk by the work that you do.

[p20 | prose | 3.1 Risk management generally]
Good risk management can be broken down to four main steps:

[p20 | prose | 3.1 Risk management generally]
FIGURE 2: Risk management steps

[p21 | prose | 3.1 Risk management generally]
You must engage with your workers and their representatives at all steps of the risk management process.

[p21 | prose | 3.1 Risk management generally]
For more information see Section 2.6 of these guidelines - Worker engagement, participation and representation

[p21 | prose | 3.2 Step 1 - Identify the hazards]
3.2 Step 1 - Identify the hazards

[p21 | prose | 3.2 Step 1 - Identify the hazards]
With your workers, identify hazards that could be associated with the work activity.

[p21 | prose | 3.2 Step 1 - Identify the hazards]
Every work environment will be different. Looking at your work environment and thinking about things that could go wrong may help you to identify hazards. Reviewing your incident and injury records (including near misses) may also help identify hazards. Ask your workers what hazards they can identify.

[p21 | prose | 3.3 Step 2 – Assess the risk]
3.3 Step 2 – Assess the risk

[p21 | prose | 3.3 Step 2 – Assess the risk]
With your workers, you will need to carry out a risk assessment for each hazard you have identified (in this case working at height). Things you should consider include:

[p21 | table | 3.3 Step 2 – Assess the risk]
| THINGS TO THINK ABOUT | CONSIDER |
| --- | --- |
| Who might be exposed to the hazard? | Who may be at the workplace, for example, workers, clients/ customers, delivery drivers, or visitors? |
| How could workers or others be harmed? | What type of harm might occur - this includes physical and mental health harm as well as the risk of physical injury? |
| How severe could the harm be? | Could someone be seriously harmed or injured? Could many people be harmed at once? |
| How often is the hazard likely to cause a risk? | How often could people be harmed - very rarely, sometimes, or frequently? |
| How could people’s health be affected? | Could the affected person/people develop short- or long- term health problems? |
| How likely are these consequences? | What is the likelihood of somebody being harmed? |

[p21 | prose | 3.3 Step 2 – Assess the risk]
TABLE 1:

[p21 | prose | 3.3 Step 2 – Assess the risk]
Things to think about when assessing risks

[p21 | prose | 3.3 Step 2 – Assess the risk]
When deciding which risks to deal with first, prioritise risks with potentially significant consequences such as serious injury or death, chronic ill-health, or those with a high chance of happening.

[p21 | prose | 3.4 Step 3 - Manage the risks]
3.4 Step 3 - Manage the risks

[p21 | prose | 3.4 Step 3 - Manage the risks]
You must take all reasonably practicable actions to eliminate or minimise the risks you and your workers have identified.

[p21 | prose | 3.4 Step 3 - Manage the risks]
Think of what you can do to reduce the risk of harm. The different ways of managing risk are called control measures. Control measures can be ranked from those with the highest level of protection and reliability to the lowest. This ranking is known as the hierarchy of control measures. Using the hierarchy of controls when choosing how to manage risks will help you to make sure you are using the most effective control measures first.

[p22 | prose | FIGURE 3: Hierarchy of control measures]
FIGURE 3: Hierarchy of control measures

[p22 | prose | FIGURE 3: Hierarchy of control measures]
The best action is to eliminate the risk. For working at height this would likely mean finding a way to do the work at ground level. For example, you could eliminate the need for working from a ladder by using long-handled tools, such as paint rollers or window brushes with extendable handles, or using camera- equipped drones or robots for inspecting structures. If you are unable to eliminate the risk, then work down from the top of the hierarchy to find the next most reasonably practicable control measures.

[p22 | prose | FIGURE 3: Hierarchy of control measures]
Safer design (elimination/substitution) and alternative construction methods (substitution/engineering controls) must also be considered, so far as is reasonably practicable.

[p22 | prose | Health and safety by design]
Health and safety by design

[p22 | prose | Health and safety by design]
At the concept phase designers and engineers must identify hazards and risks early and try to eliminate the need to work at height so far as is reasonably practicable.

[p23 | prose | Health and safety by design]
During the design phase, and prior to the final design the focus should be on designing out working at height risks during the construction phase (for example, off-site manufacturing or hoist-in building elements). The life of the plant or structure must be considered, as well as the risks associated with ongoing (for, example designing guardrails or static line systems to allow windows to be cleaned safely).

[p23 | prose | Health and safety by design]
At the delivery phase engineering design choices such as these mean workers are not exposed to the risk of falling in the first place. Changes to designs during delivery must trigger a risk assessment review.

[p23 | prose | Health and safety by design]
Designers should document all design risk control decisions on a safety by design risk register or similar.

[p23 | prose | Health and safety by design]
Examples of safer design include:

[p23 | prose | Health and safety by design]
– using low-maintenance building materials

[p23 | prose | Health and safety by design]
– using catch net hook off points and scaffolding tie systems

[p23 | prose | Health and safety by design]
– locating air conditioning and similar plant at ground level

[p23 | prose | Health and safety by design]
– installing walkways with handrails

[p23 | prose | Health and safety by design]
– having permanent guardrails or other forms of edge protection, for example parapet walls.

[p23 | prose | Health and safety by design]
For more information see Health and safety by design: an introduction

[p23 | prose | Using alternative construction methods]
Using alternative construction methods

[p23 | prose | Using alternative construction methods]
Examples of alternative construction methods include:

[p23 | prose | Using alternative construction methods]
– prefabricating wall frames horizontally before standing them up

[p23 | prose | Using alternative construction methods]
– using precast tilt-up concrete construction instead of concrete walls constructed in place

[p23 | prose | Using alternative construction methods]
– prefabricating structures on the ground or before installation and lifting them into position

[p23 | prose | Using alternative construction methods]
– pre-painting fixtures/roofs before installation

[p23 | prose | Using alternative construction methods]
– installing and maintaining antennae and satellite dishes or air conditioning in areas other than at height.

[p23 | prose | Using alternative construction methods]
A combination of control measures can be used if a single control measure is not enough to eliminate or minimise the risk. When considering which control measures are the most suitable, check that the proposed control measure will not introduce new risks.

[p23 | prose | Using alternative construction methods]
Remember that control measures used to manage risk must not create new, unmanaged risks to workers. For example, using a mobile elevating work platform or metal scaffolding as a control measure for preventing falls and being near overhead powerlines could introduce an electrocution hazard for workers. The risks associated with this hazard would also need to be managed.

[p23 | prose | Using alternative construction methods]

3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures

[p24 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
Check your control measures regularly to make sure they are working as planned. Control measures need to be regularly reviewed in consultation with your workers or their representatives to make sure they remain effective.

For more information see: Section 2.6 Worker engagement, participation and representation.

[p24 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
If your workplace or work activities change, you need to check that your existing control measures are still the most appropriate ones to use. If an incident happens or there is a near miss, you should also review your control measures and make changes if needed.

[p24 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
For more general information see: How to manage work risks | WorkSafe

[p26 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
Where there is a duty under HSWA you should first try to eliminate risks to health and safety. If it is not reasonably practicable to eliminate risk you should minimise the risk so far as is reasonably practicable.

[p26 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
This section outlines a range of controls to isolate or minimise the potential for harm resulting from a fall. The preferred approach is to apply controls that isolate workers from the risk of falling.

[p26 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
Examples are:

[p26 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
– scaffolding

[p26 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
– edge protection

[p26 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
– mechanical access plant

[p26 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
– safety mesh.

[p26 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
Controls such as harness systems and temporary work platforms provide a lesser form of protection and should only be considered when the above controls are not reasonably practicable.

[p26 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
The Table 2 shows some examples of control measures from highest to lowest levels.

[p26 | table | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
| CONTROL LEVEL (HIGHEST — LOWEST) | WHAT IT MEANS | TASK/SCENARIO | CONTROL MEASURE |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Eliminate the risk | Removes the need to work at height entirely | Installing gutters on a new build | Assemble gutters on the ground and lift into place |
| Substitute/isolate | The work is done in a safer way that avoids exposure to a fall | Painting exterior walls | Use an extendable pole system from ground level instead of climbing |
| Engineering controls (edge protection/ platforms) | Provides a physical barrier or stable working platform | Roofing work on a residential house | Install scaffolding with full edge protection (top rail, mid-rail, toeboard) |
| Engineering controls (fall prevention) | Prevents the worker from reaching a fall edge | Working near an unprotected roof edge | Use a travel restraint system (harness and fixed-length lanyard) |
| Engineering controls (fall arrest) | Minimises harm if a fall occurs | Steel erection or open-edge work where prevention is not reasonably practicable | Use a fall arrest system (harness, lanyard, anchor and rescue plan) |
| Administrative Controls | Reduces risk through procedures and supervision | Short-duration maintenance at height | Use permits, training, supervision, exclusion zones |
| Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) | Protects the individual only | General site work at height | Use helmets, non-slip footwear, harness (as a last line of defence) |

[p27 | prose | 3.4 Step 4 - Review control measures]
Remember: - you should start at the top of the hierarchy of controls and work down - you should use the highest-level control measure that is reasonably practicable - edge protection and scaffolding are strongly preferred over harness-based systems - PPE alone is never sufficient where higher-level controls can be used.

4.1 Scaffolding

[p28 | prose | 4.1 Scaffolding]
Scaffolds are a common way to provide a safe work platform. There are a wide variety of scaffolding systems available.

[p28 | prose | 4.1 Scaffolding]
– All scaffolds should comply with WorkSafe’s Scaffolding in New Zealand guidelines or a higher standard such as the AS/NZS 1576 Scaffolding series

– Scaffolds should be regularly inspected to ensure they are still compliant and fit for purpose.

[p28 | prose | 4.1 Scaffolding]
– A scaffold register should be kept on site as a record of regular inspection.

[p28 | prose | 4.1 Scaffolding]
– filing a Notification of Particular Hazardous Work online

[p28 | prose | 4.1 Scaffolding]

[p30 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
4.2 Edge protection

[p30 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
Edge protection is used to stop workers, objects or materials from falling. Areas where the likelihood of a fall exists, and edge protection should be used include:

[p30 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
– perimeters of working places

[p30 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
– openings

[p30 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
– where there is brittle material that cannot safely support the weight of a worker.

[p30 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
Where the roof pitch is greater than 25 degrees edge protection should be both system-rated and fitted with infill panels to prevent workers, tools, equipment, materials and other objects from falling. A gap of no more than 100mm to 200mm should be left between the work deck and the gutter line.

[p30 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
For roof slopes over 35 degrees, edge protection should be designed specifically.

[p30 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
Edge protection should be designed and installed to comply with AS/NZS 4994 series of standards – Temporary edge protection.

[p32 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
Edge protection may involve:

[p32 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
– a proprietary (engineered) system

[p32 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
– materials to form a guardrail and/or physical barriers

[p32 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
– erected scaffolding that supports a temporary edge-protection system

[p32 | prose | 4.2 Edge protection]
– a combination of solutions.

[p32 | prose | Integrity of the edge protection]
Integrity of the edge protection

[p32 | prose | Integrity of the edge protection]
Make sure edge protection is:

[p32 | prose | Integrity of the edge protection]
– erected, used, maintained and dismantled safely and in accordance with its design information

[p32 | prose | Integrity of the edge protection]
– regularly inspected by a competent person

[p32 | prose | Integrity of the edge protection]
– inspected after a storm or other occurrence that could affect its purpose to prevent falls

[p32 | prose | Integrity of the edge protection]
– free of any defects before use.

[p32 | prose | Installing and dismantling edge protection]
Installing and dismantling edge protection

[p32 | prose | Installing and dismantling edge protection]
Workers installing edge protection could potentially be exposed to the risk they are trying to prevent – falling from height - until the installation is completed. Pre-planning, such as a task analysis and a risk assessment, will identify the risk involved and which controls can be implemented to prevent harm during the erection process. Installation workers should have control measures in place.

[p32 | prose | Installing and dismantling edge protection]
Install edge protection as early as possible on a job so multiple groups of trades and workers can use it throughout the project.

[p33 | prose | DO NOT INSTALL EDGE PROTECTION FROM THE ROOF]
DO NOT INSTALL EDGE PROTECTION FROM THE ROOF

[p33 | prose | DO NOT INSTALL EDGE PROTECTION FROM THE ROOF]
It should be installed and dismantled from a safe location such as scaffolding or a mobile platform.

[p33 | prose | DO NOT INSTALL EDGE PROTECTION FROM THE ROOF]
Unsafe dismantling can lead to structural collapse or the uncontrolled fall of components. Competent people must follow specific sequences to keep the structure stable throughout the dismantling and removal process.

[p33 | prose | DO NOT INSTALL EDGE PROTECTION FROM THE ROOF]
Edge protection systems should comply with AS/NZS 4994.1 (design) and AS/NZS 4994.2 (installation and dismantling).

[p33 | prose | Guardrails]
Guardrails

[p33 | prose | Guardrails]
A guardrail is a barrier that can physically stop workers from falling. Guardrails can be installed to protect workers from building edges, roof edges, building openings, lift shafts and other similar ducts with wall or floor openings.

[p33 | prose | Guardrails]
A guardrail should be constructed to withstand the forces that are likely to be applied to it during the work. Temporary guardrails should generally be constructed using a proprietary metal tube and clip system.

[p33 | prose | Guardrails]
General guardrail systems should be between 900mm and 1100mm in height with a single mid rail located halfway between the work platform and the top rail. If there is a potential for tools or objects to be dropped during work, a toeboard should also be installed. Refer to Scaffolding in New Zealand

[p33 | prose | Guardrails]
Guardrail systems that are installed to protect an edge of a sloping roof surface have specific design requirements because of the increased potential for workers to fall against them and the potential for workers to slip under the mid rail.

[p33 | prose | Guardrails]
Guardrail systems for sloping roofs should be configured to prevent a worker sliding between the roof surface and the rails. It is important that such systems are installed by a competent person. For guidance on the configuration of these types of edge-protection systems refer to the standard AS/NZS 4994.2 Temporary edge protection – Roof edge protection – Installation and dismantling.

[p33 | prose | Guardrails]
If the slope of the roof is more than 25 degrees, a roof ladder should be used as well as perimeter guardrails (or a harness system) to reduce the likelihood of workers slipping.

[p33 | prose | Guardrails]
Floor openings can also be protected by a fit-for-purpose, fully decked working platform. Work inside of shafts should, so far as is reasonably practicable, be done from a fully decked working platform; a harness system should be used if using a fully decked platform is not reasonably practicable.

[p33 | prose | Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)]
Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)

[p33 | prose | Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)]
Barriers should be used to cordon off elevated areas including roofs, balconies and open excavations where there is no edge protection and workers and others are not permitted access. The barriers should be secure and with access restricted to authorised people only. Signs should warn against entry to a cordoned-off area.

[p33 | prose | Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)]
Barriers should be placed at least two metres in from any unprotected edge or opening. Barriers to restrict access should not be used for roofs with a pitch over 10 degrees. They should be highly visible and able to stay in place during bad weather.

[p34 | prose | Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)]
Installing timber temporary edge protection

[p34 | prose | Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)]
Temporary timber guardrails are sometimes used for edge protection. Timber guardrails should:

[p34 | prose | Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)]
– have a top rail positioned 900mm to 1100mm above the working surface

[p34 | prose | Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)]
– a mid-rail installed halfway between the top rail and the platform

[p34 | prose | Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)]
– a toeboard at least 90mm high

[p34 | prose | Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)]
– be free of defects

[p34 | prose | Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)]
– be no less than 90mm x 45mm (‘4 x 2’) for rails and 150mm x 25mm for toeboards.

[p34 | prose | Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)]
Timber edge protection should be constructed by a competent person, and extreme caution is required to ensure the appropriateness of all materials used. Construction planning should consider the forces that are likely to be applied to the edge protection because of the work.

[p34 | prose | Barriers to restrict access (also called bump rails)]
For more information, refer to Scaffolding in New Zealand Section 13.10 Timber scaffolds.

[p34 | prose | DO NOT USE SINGLE-POLE EDGE PROTECTION]
DO NOT USE SINGLE-POLE EDGE PROTECTION

[p34 | prose | DO NOT USE SINGLE-POLE EDGE PROTECTION]
Single-pole edge protection systems lack the structural integrity of designed proprietary systems or full scaffold structures, making them unable to reliably prevent falls.

[p34 | prose | DO NOT USE SINGLE-POLE EDGE PROTECTION]
Putting up single-pole systems often requires workers to work at height without protection during the installation process itself.

[p34 | prose | DO NOT USE SINGLE-POLE EDGE PROTECTION]
Proper edge protection requires guardrails, toeboards, and a platform, which single-pole systems usually do not have.

[p34 | prose | DO NOT USE SINGLE-POLE EDGE PROTECTION]
Single-pole systems often cannot withstand the high force applied to it if a worker falls, unlike engineered systems.

[p34 | prose | Edge protection at gable ends]
Edge protection at gable ends

[p34 | prose | Edge protection at gable ends]
Edge protection should be installed first so that it is continuous, follows the roofline to the apex - including at gable ends - and includes top rails, mid-rails and toeboards where required.

[p34 | prose | Edge protection at gable ends]
– This is particularly important at gable ends, where sloping surfaces and awkward access points increase the likelihood and severity of falls.

[p34 | prose | Edge protection at gable ends]
– Edge protection should not be left off, incomplete, or stop short of the apex, and work should not rely solely on harness systems where full edge protection is reasonably practicable.

4.3 Mechanical access plant

[p35 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical access plant]
Mechanical access plant includes all mechanically operated plant that can be used to gain access for the purpose of working at height. Commonly used mechanical access plant includes:

[p35 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical access plant]
– mobile elevating work platforms

[p35 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical access plant]
– forklift platforms

[p35 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical access plant]
– crane lift platforms

[p35 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical access plant]
– vehicle extension arms.

[p35 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical access plant]
These are specialised pieces of equipment often designed for specific kinds of operation. It is essential the right kind of machine is chosen for the intended work. The operator should be competent to operate the chosen type of mechanical access plant.

[p35 | prose | 4.3 Mechanical access plant]

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
Common types of MEWPs include scissor lifts, hoists and travel towers. There are some key safety issues that should be considered before using a MEWP.

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
Some MEWPs are designed for hard flat surfaces only (for example, a concrete slab), while others are designed for operating on rough and uneven terrain.

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
Units powered by internal combustion engines are not suitable for use in buildings or areas with poor natural ventilation unless appropriate artificial ventilation is installed.

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
Mobile elevating work platforms:

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
– need to be clearly marked with the rated lifting capacity

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
– need to have a six-monthly inspection certificate displayed.

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
Before use the operator should make sure that:

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
– they are trained and competent in the use of the MEWP

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
– the MEWP has been inspected and tested within the previous six months

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
– the MEWP is set up level and on firm surfaces

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
– hazards associated with power lines are appropriately controlled

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
– the MEWP will not create a hazard (for example, the boom will not swing out into the path of other vehicles)

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
– the MEWP will not be overloaded or used as a crane. (As an estimate, a worker carrying tools is deemed to weigh a minimum of 120kg)

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
– the operator logbook should be completed before use or if there is a change in operator.

[p35 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
An operator in a boom-style MEWP should wear a safety harness with a lanyard including a short energy absorber attached to a certified anchor point. The line should be just long enough to allow free movement inside the bucket.

[p36 | prose | Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs)]
One means of demonstrating competency is through completing nationally recognised MEWPs NZQA unit standards or an equivalent or higher qualification.

Forklift platforms

[p37 | prose | Forklift platforms]
Work platforms may be constructed to be raised or lowered using a forklift.

Crane lift platforms

[p38 | prose | 4.4 Safety mesh]
4.4 Safety mesh

[p38 | prose | 4.4 Safety mesh]
Safety mesh systems protect construction workers from falling through a roof while they are laying roof sheets. If securely fixed, they also provide fall prevention for maintenance and repair workers.

[p38 | prose | 4.4 Safety mesh]
Safety mesh should be used alongside appropriate edge protection, such as guardrails. If isolation is not reasonably practicable then a safety harness system should be used.

[p38 | prose | 4.4 Safety mesh]
Safety mesh should comply with AS/NZS 4389 Safety Mesh. This specifies the minimum requirements for the design, construction, testing and installation of safety mesh for use in domestic, commercial and industrial building applications.

[p38 | prose | Installing safety mesh]
Installing safety mesh

[p38 | prose | Installing safety mesh]
Workers installing safety mesh should only use mesh where the product information has been made available by the manufacturer/supplier, including evidence of compliance with AS/NZS 4389 Safety Mesh.

[p38 | prose | Installing safety mesh]
Special care is required to make sure the mesh is securely connected to the structure and the overlap between adjoining sections of mesh is enough to get the necessary strength to resist the force of a worker falling onto it.

[p38 | prose | Installing safety mesh]
Use scaffolding or elevating work platforms to obtain safe access for installation workers.

[p38 | prose | Installing safety mesh]
The safety mesh should be covered by the roof cladding as soon as possible after it has been installed. Cladding installers should make sure this does not happen until the mesh has been formally inspected by a competent person in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

[p38 | prose | Installing safety mesh]
The mesh is first cut to the right length and is then run out over the roof using a continuous rope system. Installers should not walk across the open purlins to draw the mesh.

[p39 | prose | 4.5 Safety decking]
4.5 Safety decking

[p39 | prose | 4.5 Safety decking]
Safety deck systems (also known as modular work platforms or safety platforms) provide a stable, walkable surface for workers to work at any height (often used to fill voids such as lift shafts, atriums or stairwells).

[p39 | prose | 4.5 Safety decking]
Although it can be more expensive and take longer to install than fall arrest systems (such as safety nets) it can reduce reliance on scaffolding.

[p39 | prose | 4.5 Safety decking]
Alongside providing a more stable base (similar to standing on solid ground) safety decking can allow for better worker posture and increased freedom and range of movement.

[p39 | prose | 4.5 Safety decking]
It can also provide a clear working space and unobstructed access between levels in a construction zone while eliminating fall zones from upper-level floors and floor pits.

Installing safety decking

[p39 | prose | Installing safety decking]
Installation should be done in compliance with manufacturer’s instructions. Safety decking must be installed by a trained, competent person.

[p39 | prose | Installing safety decking]
If a safety deck is used for edge protection it must meet standards such as AS/NZS 4994.1.

[p40 | prose | 4.6 Harness systems]
4.6 Harness systems

[p40 | prose | 4.6 Harness systems]
A harness system allows a worker to be positioned and safely supported while they are working at height. Harness systems are used for gaining access to, and working at, a workface where there is a risk of a fall. The most common harness systems include:

[p40 | prose | 4.6 Harness systems]
– travel restraint systems

[p40 | prose | 4.6 Harness systems]
– fall arrest systems

[p40 | prose | 4.6 Harness systems]
– work positioning systems

[p40 | prose | 4.6 Harness systems]
– safety lines, lifelines, prescribed or proprietary (engineered) systems.

[p40 | prose | Hierarchy of fall-protection systems]
Hierarchy of fall-protection systems

[p40 | prose | Hierarchy of fall-protection systems]
All fall-protection systems are considered personal protective equipment (PPE). If it is not reasonably practicable to eliminate the risk (for example, by working from the ground) or use risk-minimising engineering controls (such as scaffolding) then the last resort is using fall-protection PPE.

[p40 | prose | Hierarchy of fall-protection systems]
The list in Table 3 follows the hierarchy of controls - preferring restraint over fall arrest when minimising the risk of a fall:

[p40 | table | Hierarchy of fall-protection systems]
| SYSTEM RANKING | EXAMPLE | EXPLANATION |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Travel restraint | Fixed-length lanyards | Preferred PPE system, as this physically prevents workers reaching the edge. |
| 2. Restrained fall | Short lanyard and anchor | Often used interchangeably with ‘travel restraint’ as long as this method stops the user from reaching the fall edge. |
| 3. Limited free-fall | Work-positioning straps | Work-positioning straps usually allow a small fall (less than 600mm) if positioning fails. |
| 4. Free-fall | Inertia reels | Inertia reels are a fall arrest method. They are low on the hierarchy because they allow falls to happen. |
| 5. Restraint technique | Active re-anchoring | This is a method within fall restraint (constantly re- anchoring to avoid the edge). |

[p41 | prose | Travel restraint system]
Travel restraint system

[p41 | prose | Travel restraint system]
The preferred harness system for working at height is the travel restraint system. This system protects workers from approaching an unprotected edge, preventing a free fall from happening.

[p41 | prose | Travel restraint system]
The system consists of equipment rated for a fall — such as a full body harness that is connected by a lanyard or safety line to a suitable anchorage point or horizontal lifeline.

[p41 | prose | Fall arrest system]
Fall arrest system

[p41 | prose | Fall arrest system]
A fall arrest system is designed to support and hold a worker if they fall. It is not a work positioning system as work positioning systemsare not designed to support someone while working.

[p41 | prose | Fall arrest system]
A fall arrest system should be considered only when a travel restraint system is not reasonably practicable to use. Fall arrest is a minimisation measure as it does not stop the fall from happening. These systems require a higher level of operator competency and supervision.

[p41 | prose | Fall arrest system]
When working at height, fall arrest systems should be considered only where higher-order controls (such as elimination, edge protection, scaffolding, MEWPs) are not reasonably practicable, and where sufficient fall clearance, rescue capability, and worker training and competency are in place. Fall arrest is not a substitute for edge protection - where edge protection is reasonably practicable.

[p41 | prose | Fall arrest system]
A fall arrest system is an assembly of interconnected components consisting of a harness which is connected to an anchorage point by means of a lanyard incorporating an energy absorber. They can be used where workers are required to carry out their work near an unprotected edge.

[p41 | prose | Fall arrest system]
When fall arrest systems are used an appropriate safety helmet should be worn to protect the worker from head injury during an uncontrolled fall.

[p41 | prose | Fall arrest system]
Harnesses should be connected, by a lanyard, to an anchor point. A harness not connected to an anchor point in a fall arrest system is rendered useless and is a critical safety failure that could create a fatal fall risk.

[p41 | prose | Fall arrest system]
Also consider the distance and consequences of a fall. Fall-arrest systems will be ineffective if the deployment length is greater than the fall height because the worker will hit the floor before the system can deploy.

[p41 | prose | Work positioning systems]
Work positioning systems

[p41 | prose | Work positioning systems]
Work positioning systems enable workers to work supported in a harness under tension in a way that stops a fall. Generally, the system allows the worker to maintain a stable position and to work hands-free while completing a task. The harness arrangement should not allow a fall of more than 600mm.

[p42 | prose | Work positioning systems]
A permanent anchor point should be designed by a chartered professional engineer.

[p42 | prose | Work positioning systems]
The manufacturer and designer should ensure that each permanent anchor is uniquely identified so that its installation, testing and maintenance can be tracked during its lifetime.

[p42 | prose | Work positioning systems]
Permanent anchor systems are exposed to environmental and other working stressors over time. They are also reliant on the condition and strength of the material they are installed into. This is why anchor testing and inspection regimes should consider all these factors.

[p42 | prose | Work positioning systems]
The expected design life of the anchor and the required maintenance should be specified by the designer.

[p42 | prose | Work positioning systems]
Anchors should be suitably rated for the expected use. All fall arrest and abseil anchors should be tagged and recertified annually to remain compliant with AS/NZS 1891 Industrial Fall Arrest Systems and Devices series of standards.

[p42 | prose | Work positioning systems]
For more information see Maintaining, inspecting and re-certifying permanent anchorage systems

[p42 | prose | Temporary anchorage]
Temporary anchorage

[p42 | prose | Temporary anchorage]
A temporary anchor can include proprietary fittings or an appropriate arrangement of strops and ropes. All temporary anchors should be set up by a competent person. Where a proprietary temporary system is used, it should be installed in accordance with the manufacturers’ or designers’ instructions and specifications.

[p42 | prose | Temporary anchorage]
The roof or other building component to which an anchor is attached should be checked by a competent person to verify that it is suitable for supporting the anchor.

[p42 | prose | Temporary anchorage]
Anchor points should ideally be positioned above head height of the worker to limit the free-fall distance. This is particularly important when using an inertia reel, as this will stop the line making contact with an obstruction and to limit the free-fall distance to that recommended by the designer/manufacturer.

[p43 | prose | Training for harness work]
Training for harness work

[p43 | prose | Training for harness work]
All harness work requires training and competence and only trained and competent workers can install and use harness systems on site. They should also be supervised at all times by another person who is also trained and competent. They should have their workplace competence verified through a combination of NZQA-registered training, practical, on-the-job assessments by a competent supervisor, and a Verification of Competency (VoC).

[p43 | prose | Training for harness work]

[p43 | prose | Training for harness work]

[p43 | prose | Minimising the potential fall distance]
Minimising the potential fall distance

[p43 | prose | Minimising the potential fall distance]
When a fall arrest system is being used, the potential free-fall distance should be less than 2 metres. Energy-absorbing lanyards should not be used in conjunction with inertia reels as this can result in an excessive distance of fall prior to the fall being arrested.

[p43 | prose | Minimising the potential fall distance]
There should be sufficient distance between the work surface and any surface below to enable the system, including the action of any shock absorber, to deploy fully.

[p44 | prose | Maintain minimum of slack in fall arrest line]
Maintain minimum of slack in fall arrest line

[p44 | prose | Maintain minimum of slack in fall arrest line]
There should be no excessive slack in the fall arrest line between the worker and the attachment. The anchorage point should be as high as the equipment allows. Never work above the anchor point, as this will increase the free-fall distance if a worker falls, resulting in higher forces on the body and greater likelihood of the arrest line snagging on obstructions.

[p44 | prose | Positioning the inertia reel anchor points]
Positioning the inertia reel anchor points

[p44 | prose | Positioning the inertia reel anchor points]
Inertia reels should be anchored above head height to prevent the line making contact with an obstruction and to limit the free-fall distance to that recommended by the designer/manufacturer. The worker should work within an arc of up to 30 degrees below the inertia reel unless otherwise specified by the manufacturer.

[p44 | prose | Swing fall (the pendulum effect)]
Swing fall (the pendulum effect)

[p44 | prose | Swing fall (the pendulum effect)]
Swing fall is a potential hazard with the use of harness systems. It can happen in two situations, swing down and swing back.

[p44 | prose | Swing fall (the pendulum effect)]
To stop swing fall:

[p44 | prose | Swing fall (the pendulum effect)]
– place the anchorage point at a right angle to the position of the line at the perimeter edge; a mobile anchorage is helpful here

[p44 | prose | Swing fall (the pendulum effect)]
– use secondary anchor points and/or anchor lines

[p44 | prose | Swing fall (the pendulum effect)]
– use a perimeter guardrail to prevent any fall over the perimeter edge.

[p44 | prose | Swing fall (the pendulum effect)]
Where swing fall is possible, it is better to use a work positioning system, or another means of access such as an elevating work platform.

[p45 | prose | Rescue planning]
Rescue planning

[p45 | prose | Rescue planning]
A rescue plan should be developed before installing the harness system. It is critical that a suspended worker can be rescued quickly.

[p45 | prose | Rescue planning]
A worker suspended in a harness can develop suspension intolerance. This is a condition where blood pooling in the legs can lead to loss of consciousness, renal failure and, in extreme cases, death.

[p45 | prose | Rescue planning]
A pre-rigged retrieval system is a good way of ensuring quick rescue. A rescue plan should consider:

[p45 | prose | Rescue planning]
– the rescue method, such as using a crane or elevating work platform

[p45 | prose | Rescue planning]
– available equipment

[p45 | prose | Rescue planning]
– responsibilities and training

[p45 | prose | Rescue planning]
– communication

[p45 | prose | Rescue planning]
– medical requirements

[p45 | prose | Rescue planning]
– involving the emergency services.

[p45 | prose | Rescue planning]
Workers using fall arrest systems should never work alone.

[p45 | prose | Rescue planning]

[p53 | prose | 4.10 Safety nets]

[p53 | prose | 4.10 Safety nets]
Safety nets are used on construction sites and similar work sites mainly to stop a worker falling, although they can also be used to catch or contain debris.

[p53 | prose | 4.10 Safety nets]

[p54 | prose | 4.11 Fixed roof ladders and crawl boards]
4.11 Fixed roof ladders and crawl boards

[p54 | prose | 4.11 Fixed roof ladders and crawl boards]
Fixed crawl boards and roof ladders can be used as permanent access to a work positioning system, or on pitched or brittle roofs as access to service plant.

[p54 | prose | 4.11 Fixed roof ladders and crawl boards]
– crawl boards should have a minimum width of 450mm and should have handrails

[p54 | prose | 4.11 Fixed roof ladders and crawl boards]
– on brittle roofs guardrails should be permanently installed on crawl boards and fixed roof ladders

[p54 | prose | 4.11 Fixed roof ladders and crawl boards]
– crawl boards should have a non-slip surface or cleats, depending on their pitch. Ensure that permanent access complies with the Building Act 2004

[p54 | prose | 4.11 Fixed roof ladders and crawl boards]
– temporary roof ladders and crawl boards should be of the same standard as for permanent installations

[p54 | prose | 4.11 Fixed roof ladders and crawl boards]
– roof ladders should be used on roof pitches over 25 degrees

[p54 | prose | 4.11 Fixed roof ladders and crawl boards]
– the bracket on the top of a crawl board or roof ladder should be deep enough to reach over the ridge and lap the roof framing.

[p54 | prose | 4.11 Fixed roof ladders and crawl boards]
Crawl boards, when used on their own, do not stop falls. Where the potential of a fall still exists while using crawl boards, extra measures such as edge protection and/or fall restraint systems may need to be used.

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
Ladders and step ladders do not offer fall protection and should be the last form of work access equipment to be considered.

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
Ladders or stepladders should be used for low-risk and short-duration tasks. The worker should maintain three points of contact with a ladder or stepladder to reduce the likelihood of slipping and falling.

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
Never overreach while working from a ladder.

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
Where reasonably practicable you should use a podium ladder.

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
Ladders and stepladders should be of trade or industrial standard and be rated at not less than 120kg. In New Zealand, industrial-use ladders should be compliant with the AS/NZS 1892 series of standards.

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
Ladders should be:

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
– clearly labelled as “industrial” or “industrial rated” complying with AS/NZS 1891 series of standards

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
– structurally sound and free of defects, this means:

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
- no bent/split styles or rungs

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
- no rust

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
- no missing rubber footings

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
- styles must be parallel

[p54 | prose | 4.12 Ladders, stepladders, and means of access]
– not covered in chemicals or other materials.

[p55 | prose | Issues for ladder or stepladder use]
Issues for ladder or stepladder use

[p55 | prose | Issues for ladder or stepladder use]
– Overload — the worker and anything they are taking up should not exceed the highest safe working load stated on the ladder.

[p55 | prose | Issues for ladder or stepladder use]
– Over-reach — keep the line of the belt buckle (navel) inside the stiles with both feet on the same rung throughout the task.

[p55 | prose | Issues for ladder or stepladder use]
– Do not keep tools or other items resting on the steps or hanging from the rungs.

[p55 | prose | Issues for ladder or stepladder use]
– Carry tools on a tool belt.

[p55 | prose | Issues for ladder or stepladder use]
– Stop at the third step from the top of a straight ladder.

[p55 | prose | Working from stepladders]
Working from stepladders

[p55 | prose | Working from stepladders]
When working from stepladders, avoid work that imposes side loading, such as side-on drilling through solid materials. Face the steps of the ladder towards the work activity.

[p55 | prose | Working from stepladders]
Where side-on loadings cannot be avoided, stop the stepladder from tipping over by tying the steps to a suitable point, or use a more suitable type of access equipment.

[p55 | prose | Working from stepladders]
Avoid holding items when climbing ladders and stepladders by using tool belts.

[p56 | prose | On a stepladder]
On a stepladder

[p56 | prose | On a stepladder]
Where a handhold cannot be maintained, the use of a stepladder should take into account:

[p56 | prose | On a stepladder]
– the height of the task

[p56 | prose | On a stepladder]
– if a safe handhold is available on the stepladder

[p56 | prose | On a stepladder]
– if it is light work

[p56 | prose | On a stepladder]
– if it avoids side loading

[p56 | prose | On a stepladder]
– if it avoids over-reaching

[p56 | prose | On a stepladder]
– if the worker’s feet are fully supported

[p56 | prose | On a stepladder]
– if the stepladder can be tied

[p56 | prose | On a stepladder]
– location, for example, away from driveways and doorways unless isolated

[p56 | prose | On a stepladder]
– that there is four metres clearance from electricity lines

[p56 | prose | On a stepladder]
– use of hand tools that require extra leverage.

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
Preventing ladders from slipping

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
All reasonably practicable steps should be taken to prevent a leaning ladder from slipping or falling. Where possible:

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
– tie (or equally effectively secure) the ladder at the top. If this is not possible tie it where reasonably practicable

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
– use an effective ladder stability device

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
– wedge the ladder against a suitable fixed structure, such as a wall

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
– ‘foot it’ by facing the ladder with both feet on the bottom rung, each foot as far apart as possible on the rung (stile to stile), and both hands on the stiles.

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
The person footing the ladder should remain in the position described until the person using the ladder has descended to a point where they can safely step onto the ground. The worker and footer should not overload the ladder.

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
When in use, the portable leaning ladder should:

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
– rest against a solid surface at the top

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
– rise at least one metre or three rungs above the landing point

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
– be positioned so workers do not have to over-reach or climb over obstacles (workers should be able to do the job with both feet and one hand on the ladder)

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
– rest on firm, level ground

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
– be in good condition and free from slippery substances

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
– be used with adequate clearance from traffic routes

[p56 | prose | Preventing ladders from slipping]
– be at an angle of one metre out for every four metres up.

[p56 | prose | Ladder stability devices (LSDs)]
Ladder stability devices (LSDs)

[p56 | prose | Ladder stability devices (LSDs)]
Ladder stability devices can give extra ladder stability where other methods do not work. For example, tying or footing.

[p56 | prose | Ladder stability devices (LSDs)]
Ladder stability devices and ladder levellers should only be used strictly in accordance with the manufacturers’ and suppliers’ instructions for use.

[p57 | prose | Checking ladders before use]
Checking ladders before use

[p57 | prose | Checking ladders before use]
The following should be checked before using a ladder and after any incident (for example, a ladder being dropped):

[p57 | prose | Checking ladders before use]
– check all feet and caps are present and in good condition and securely fastened

[p57 | prose | Checking ladders before use]
– make sure all side stays and clips are present and fixed in place

[p57 | prose | Checking ladders before use]
– all rivets are present and in good condition

[p57 | prose | Checking ladders before use]
– rungs have not been bent or damaged

[p57 | prose | Checking ladders before use]
– side stiles have no deformities such as dents or structural faults.

[p57 | prose | Access and egress]
Access and egress

[p57 | prose | Access and egress]
A single portable ladder set up and secured at a slope of a ratio of 4:1 (four metres up by one metre out) and extending at least one metre or three rungs above the stepping-off point is a suitable means of access and egress, provided it is:

[p57 | prose | Access and egress]
– a step ladder of maximum length six metres

[p57 | prose | Access and egress]
– a single ladder of maximum length nine metres

[p57 | prose | Access and egress]
– secured against sliding top and bottom

[p57 | prose | Access and egress]
– set on firm, level ground

[p57 | prose | Access and egress]
– extending by one metre higher than the roof or other step off point

[p57 | prose | Access and egress]
– used by no more than one person at a time except when footing.

[p57 | prose | Access and egress]
Climbing a ladder to secure it at the top can be dangerous. There should be another worker to secure the ladder at the bottom while doing this.

[p57 | prose | Access and egress]
For more information see: Safe working with ladders and stepladders

[p59 | prose | 5.1 Working conditions]
5.1 Working conditions

[p59 | prose | 5.1 Working conditions]
Slopes, rough or uneven ground, obstructions and traffic can decide what equipment you use. For example, an elevating work platform (EWP) could reach over bad ground or obstructions as long as its stability was not compromised. An EWP may be better than a tower scaffold in these kinds of situations.

[p59 | prose | 5.2 Risk of falling objects]
5.2 Risk of falling objects

[p59 | prose | 5.2 Risk of falling objects]
Falling tools, building materials, debris and other objects all have the potential to cause harm.

[p59 | prose | 5.2 Risk of falling objects]
Falls can be caused by these objects being unsecured, gaps in toeboards or scaffolding, high winds and items being stored poorly.

[p59 | prose | 5.2 Risk of falling objects]
If it is not reasonably practicable to eliminate the risk of falling objects, PCBUs should:

[p59 | prose | 5.2 Risk of falling objects]
– prevent the object falling from height by way of containment (scrim or other suitable sheeting material).

[p59 | prose | 5.2 Risk of falling objects]
If preventing the fall is not reasonably practicable, PCBUs should arrest the fall of the object (for example, with catch nets or lanyards).

[p59 | prose | 5.2 Risk of falling objects]
If arresting the fall is not reasonably practicable, a physical exclusion zone should be erected and maintained on the ground (for example, site fencing or another physical barrier).

[p59 | prose | 5.2 Risk of falling objects]
Preventing objects from falling is the easiest and most effective way of controlling the risks linked with falling objects.

[p59 | prose | 5.3 Emergency planning]
5.3 Emergency planning

[p59 | prose | 5.3 Emergency planning]
An emergency plan outlines the actions required of all onsite workers and must be accessible to them.

[p59 | prose | 5.3 Emergency planning]
It must be easy for everyone to understand and effective immediately if needed.

[p59 | prose | 5.3 Emergency planning]
For more information see Workplace emergency plans

[p59 | prose | 5.4 Duration and frequency of use]
5.4 Duration and frequency of use

[p59 | prose | 5.4 Duration and frequency of use]
Long-duration, higher frequency work justifies a higher standard of fall protection such as a tower scaffold rather than a ladder but a ladder may be justified for short duration low-risk repetitive work.

[p59 | prose | 5.5 Rescue]
5.5 Rescue

[p59 | prose | 5.5 Rescue]
If rescue from a deployed fall arrest system is going to be difficult, choose other work equipment. For example, an EWP.

[p60 | prose | 5.6 Additional risk posed by the installation and removal of work equipment]
5.6 Additional risk posed by the installation and removal of work equipment

[p60 | prose | 5.6 Additional risk posed by the installation and removal of work equipment]
An EWP used by one person may be less risky than getting two or three people to put up a tower or scaffold for the one person to work safely.

[p60 | prose | 5.7 Operator logbooks]
5.7 Operator logbooks

[p60 | prose | 5.7 Operator logbooks]
Operator logbooks are a practical way for PCBUs and workers to show working at height competency.

[p60 | prose | 5.7 Operator logbooks]
Logbooks provide a verifiable record of a worker’s training, experience, and proficiency across tasks such as harness-based work, scaffolding, MEWP operation, or rope access. They support compliance with relevant standards by showing operators are competent, appropriately supervised where required, and using the correct methods for the specific height safety control system.

[p60 | prose | 5.7 Operator logbooks]
For equipment, logbooks help track the safe use and condition of height safety control systems, including harnesses, lanyards, anchor points, scaffolds, and MEWPs.

[p60 | prose | 5.7 Operator logbooks]
Operators should complete entries that record dates of use, task details, equipment used, pre-use inspections, and any defects or incidents, along with confirmation of the relevant control system (such as fall restraint or fall arrest systems).

[p60 | prose | 5.7 Operator logbooks]
Entries may also include supervisor sign-off where needed. By maintaining accurate and up-to-date records, properly filled out logbooks show equipment is being used correctly, inspections are being carried out, and both worker competency and equipment management are being actively maintained.

[p62 | prose | 6.1 Training and inductions]
6.1 Training and inductions

[p62 | prose | 6.1 Training and inductions]
PCBUS have duties under Regulation 9 of the GRWM regulations to provide information, training and instruction to workers. In relation to working from height, this should include training workers on their role in managing the risks outlined in these guidelines – particularly in relation to managing the risks from working at height.

[p62 | prose | 6.1 Training and inductions]
In addition to training specific to work at height risks, workers should get training on general workplace requirements and expectations such as:

[p62 | prose | 6.1 Training and inductions]
– when/how to report a health and safety incident

[p62 | prose | 6.1 Training and inductions]
– information on how they can engage meaningfully on work health and safety matters.

[p62 | prose | 6.1 Training and inductions]
Initial training should be done before the worker starts and during their induction phase. Regular refresher training may also be required - refresher training is recommended every 2 years.

[p62 | prose | 6.1 Training and inductions]
Untrained workers require supervision by a competent person.

[p62 | prose | 6.1 Training and inductions]
You should have documented rescue plans, with on-site personnel trained in the rescue plans.

[p62 | prose | 6.1 Training and inductions]
For more general information see: Providing information, training, instruction or supervision for workers | WorkSafe

[p62 | prose | 6.2 Fitness to work]
6.2 Fitness to work

[p62 | prose | 6.2 Fitness to work]
Fitness for work means a worker is in a physical and mental condition that allows them to perform their assigned tasks safely and competently, without being a risk to themselves or others.

[p62 | prose | 6.2 Fitness to work]
This means workers should not go to work with severe fatigue or illness or be under the influence of drugs or alcohol.

[p62 | prose | 6.2 Fitness to work]
PCBUs should ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, that a worker is fit to carry out their tasks safely, including monitoring health when required.

[p62 | prose | 6.2 Fitness to work]
Workers should take reasonable care of their own health and safety and ensure their actions do not harm others. They should comply with reasonable policies and instructions from the PCBU regarding fitness for work.
